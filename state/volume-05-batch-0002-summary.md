## 1. What the block is

Ten chapters in ten days, the twelfth to the twenty-first of April, one day to a chapter, in a yard and a lane and a bank and a lane four miles down a lower terrace. A dray goes up the only road in this district that climbs and does not come back. It comes back on the fourteenth with one man on it, a yoke on the tailboard, and a printed sheet under the yoke that was not there when it went up — **and the man who went up with it is not on it and the case is not on it and nothing in the ten chapters says where he is.** The sheet is the standing orders of nine locks and it gives **nine** nights where a stranger's sheet gives eight days and a word. Its ninth night is the twentieth of April. Its column headed KEEPER carries a house on all nine rows and a person on none, and that is how this block settles a question the prompt left open: **no keeper has ever been on a page of anybody's, and it is not because pages forget people, it is because the page has a column and the column carries a house.** A man who keeps a road walks nine miles to give this district the eight nights he has walked the road in this run, refuses the ninth in forty-two words with a reason, and says his house has a word for the man who turns one and a word for the night he turns it and that neither is on a page. The first letter this district has ever had from a council asks, politely, in writing, whether this district will send a man, and wants an answer by the last day of this month. A man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, who said on the eighth of April that he would ask the woman of fifty-eight himself and did not on the ninth, goes up the bank on the sixteenth and asks her, in his own words and not in the six things, how many of the ninety houses she can still name, and she says eleven, and this yard gives her eleven houses of her round on the seventeenth and asks her first. A man of about thirty at a counter comes down an eleven mile road for the fifteenth time since the second of January and refuses the ninth holding for the seventh time with no reason, **and for the first time since the twenty-fourth of the month before last the count of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given moves.** The ninth of the nine is the twentieth and a tank four miles down a lane comes up within a hand's breadth of its lip and the fourth holding takes its first drop since the first of the second month, and a man of about thirty-four who digs loam says out loud that a third resemblance is a habit and that he is not going to let a pail of water be the witness.

**The block's finding, stated once: two lists of nine that agree on eight are not one list, and the one they disagree about is the ninth, and the ninth is a word in a stranger's hand and a day in a body's own hand, and the district cannot say which is right and cannot reach the people either list is about.** **And the second finding, which the prompt asked for and which the chapters decided: the page that carries the nine has a column headed with a keeper's name on it, and the column carries a house nine times and a person no times, so the reason no keeper has ever been on a page is not that pages forget people.** The method held and produced no remedy. ## 4. The decision the prompt left to the chapter, and what was decided

**The prompt said: Batch 0002 must be the block in which a keeper is named on a page, or in which it is established on the page that no keeper has ever been on one; either is a finding; do not decide it in the outline; decide it in the chapter and write down what you decided.**

**What was decided, and it is the second of the two options and it is better than the first. NO KEEPER IS NAMED ON A PAGE, AND THE BLOCK ESTABLISHES ON THE PAGE WHY.** In Chapter 213 the standing orders are read out and the third of its four lines says that the column headed KEEPER carries a house on every one of the nine rows and a person on none of them. A man of about thirty-four who mends fencing then says, in ninety-six words, that there is a column on that sheet headed with a word and not a name, that a man from a Registry told this yard on the seventh of April that there are four generations of keepers and that not one of them has ever been on a page of his, and **so no keeper has ever been on a page of anybody's, and it is not because pages forget people, it is because the page has a column and the column carries a house.** A man of fifty-six enters that a Registry prints a house and does not print a person, that this district's own records office prints a house and does not print a person, that the keepers' page has been doing it for four generations, and **that those three are the same sentence told three times by three bodies and are one finding and not three.** In Chapter 214 a man of about thirty at a counter says in thirty-eight words that a house is what keeps a lock when a man cannot, and that nobody would tell him a name. In Chapter 220 nobody in the district can name a keeper and a man of about thirty-four who digs loam says he will keep saying so until somebody in that yard can tell him which of the wet things on his list is one.

**Why the second option and not the first.** A keeper's name would have been a new personal name in a volume whose prose names nobody, would have cost the volume its rule, and would have been a gift rather than a finding. The second option gives the volume a structural fact instead: **the keepers, the Registry and this district's own office all print a house and not a person, and the oldest of the three has been doing it for four generations.** It also puts the woman of fifty-eight in the same sentence as the paper for the first time — a page that names a house is a true page, and a woman carrying two buckets up a hill is a job, and a man who digs loam refuses the join on the page in the same breath.
