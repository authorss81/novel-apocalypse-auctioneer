## 4. THE TEN MORNINGS, ONE LINE EACH

1. **971, the two hundred and fifty-fifth.** The boards, the wind on the corner of the lot book, the man of fifty-six's four and the patch of paler stone, the boy's lane count and his saying out loud that he is saying no figure, and the woman at the scale putting a figure on the edge of her own board and not saying it. **Her change: she stands at the scale about a quarter of an hour longer than she needs to.**
2. **972, the two hundred and fifty-sixth.** She is at the foot of the bank before the boards and has worked out that everything that matters in that yard is written down twice, once where anybody can read it. The tally-man's number goes into about four hundred yards of air and goes across that yard three times over. **Her change: she writes the last figure of the day into the clean stretch at the end of the board, where the chalk has never been.**
3. **973, the two hundred and fifty-seventh. ESCALATION 1.** She says one figure of her own out loud, once, at about a quarter to one, in the ordinary voice, looking along the yard and not at her own board. Nobody answers, nobody repeats it, nobody thanks her, nobody asks her what she weighs things for. It goes out and does not come back off the bank. The boy does not turn round, and standing at her scale afterwards she understands that if he had, it would have been worse. **Her change: it cannot be unsaid, and it is on no page.**
4. **974, the two hundred and fifty-eighth.** The day after, and the whole morning is spent establishing that the yard carried on. The man of about thirty-two's table, the heel, the cloth taken over the same stretch twice because the grit in the grain only comes off on the second pass. The man who digs loam has the north end to himself and nobody at those boards says good morning to him. **Her change: she decides she is not going to find out whether anybody heard by waiting to be asked.**
5. **975, the two hundred and fifty-ninth.** The man of fifty-six's morning, and the seven hundredth of them. The top figure has rain in it and is the one that can go, so he reads it last, after three that have told him what kind of morning his eyes are having. He gets all four. **Change: after the boards come down he is still there, reading the four to himself under his breath, and he gets them again.**
6. **976, the two hundred and sixtieth. ESCALATION 2.** The man who makes round things takes a thing out of the fire, looks at the place where the colour has gone further than the rest of the ring, and puts it back in. At about one he says a number to the man beside him with his thumb on the rim. Then the tally-man's number goes out at half past one. **She heard both and could not tell you afterwards which she heard first.**
7. **977, the two hundred and sixty-first.** The woman of about fifty-two comes down with a frame with a rebate cut round the inside of it and nothing in the rebate, and the corners worn through. The second space on the form is empty in front of her for the best part of an hour; she picks the pen up twice and puts it down twice. She stands the frame against the rail and looks through it, and the yard goes further away than it is. **The second space goes up that bank in the bag, still empty.**
8. **978, the two hundred and sixty-second.** The block's one reason. The man of about thirty-four says that he squares that table every morning with the same corner and the same amount because the leg nearest the near rail moves, and that the knee on that side is the reason for that. Nobody answers, nobody thanks him, nobody asks him why or to say it again. **The page takes one line, carrying the morning and not the reason. There is a worn place about four inches from the foot of that page and the line went over the top of it and did not sit level.**
9. **979, the two hundred and sixty-third.** The gap in the bank, worn round at the edges by wheels, with the cart waiting on the high side of it. The man of forty-eight is not standing where he stands; he is at the gap, and he puts his number out from there. The cart comes through a foot away from where it went out and does not stop. **She has seen that cart go through four hundred times and has never once seen it go through with a number still in the air.**
10. **980, the two hundred and sixty-fourth.** The man of thirty-eight's whole silent day, told from him. The route: out along the top of the table, round the end, up the slope, across, back down, in at the same corner. The line along the near edge with its ends squared off, because a line can be counted by looking along it and a heap cannot. At about four he stops and looks at the line without touching any of it. **Nobody says a word, and he goes up the slope about nine feet behind the others.**
