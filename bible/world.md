# World

## Overview

The setting is the **Morrow Basin**, a river-and-rail region where the Long Fracture broke the link between emergency services and the people who depended on them. The disaster did not create magic in the familiar sense. It made the rules underneath ordinary infrastructure visible and negotiable: a route could remember a destination, a door could refuse a person, a fire could be assigned to a keeper, or a seed could borrow a season from a child.

The dangerous object at the center of each affected area is called an **anchor lot**. It is usually mundane: a transit token, a cracked bell, a salt key, a shuttle, a shoe, a ration tin, a child’s compass. Its danger comes from the one local exception it can sustain and the toll required to keep that exception active. The object is not sentient and does not issue quests. People negotiate around it.

The region remains inhabited. Food is grown, trains run on repaired track, clinics share batteries, and families keep records by hand. The apocalypse is an ongoing civic emergency, not an empty world.

## Historical timeline

### Before the Long Fracture

The last pre-Long-Fracture cities used public works and private service concessions together. Water, transit, power, medical triage, and weather control were increasingly governed by **use contracts**: a company or municipality could hold the right to operate a service for a term. The contracts were meant to keep essential services running. They also allowed a holder to decide who counted as a beneficiary.

The final months before the Long Fracture saw a public effort to place the region’s emergency rights into one open settlement. The effort was called the **Common Measure**. It was never completed.

### The Long Fracture — Year 0

At 03:17, a chain of service contracts failed across the basin. Control boards failed, hospitals lost their triage permissions, and rail signals began accepting destinations that had not been entered. The physical disaster was real: floodwater, fires, blackouts, and collapse. The lasting change was political and physical. In its wake, ordinary places could preserve a local rule only by repeatedly paying a cost.

The official historical name is the **Long Fracture**; survivors often shorten it to the **Fracture**. These are two names for the same Year 0 event, not separate disasters. No single person is confirmed as its sole cause, although the later investigation establishes that the failed Common Measure was the trigger, not a natural disaster or an outside invasion.

### The First Tally — Years 1–3

People began holding **closing circles**: a small group described an object’s use, agreed on its toll, and recorded who could bear it. The Tally appeared as a plain written notice in ledgers and, for people who had handled an anchor lot, as a brief internal inscription. It recognized valid closings and made unpaid tolls visible as **backwash**.

Early closings were improvised and dangerous. People bought the right to use a well, a bridge, or a storm shelter without realizing that the right carried a claim on their memories or labor. The first guilds formed to certify valuers and preserve the few surviving records.

### The Charter Wars — Years 4–9

Guilds, municipal Wardens, and caravan companies fought over the most useful anchor lots. A charter became a weapon, a passport, and a financial asset. Some cities stabilized by limiting charters to public terms. Others sold future labor to underwriters and left ordinary residents with a nominal right they could never exercise.

The Tally never declared a winner. Outside the founder's emergency process, it continued to accept any consensual settlement whose terms were internally coherent, including a settlement designed to benefit one faction.

### Present day — Year 17

Alder Reach has a functioning Ward market, a damaged tramway, and a patchwork of agreements with neighboring settlements. The central question is no longer whether the Tally exists. It is whether a region can build a market that survives the people who founded it.

## Physical rules of a Fault

A **Fault** is a bounded area where a local rule has separated from ordinary cause and effect. Faults are not portals to another world. They are damaged agreements made physical.

Every Fault has:

- a boundary that can move when the anchor is used or its charter changes;
- one primary anchor lot at the origin;
- a use that produces a practical but limited effect;
- a toll that must be paid by the bearer, the beneficiary, or the surrounding place;
- a standing requirement that determines who can safely activate it.

A Fault can be a single stairwell, a rail cut, a hospital ward, a seed vault, or a stretch of coast. Its size does not determine its danger. A tiny Fault can hold a critical water rule; a huge Fault may only produce a useless weather change.

### Activation

Activation requires all of the following:

1. **A named use.** Someone states what change is intended.
2. **A defined bearer.** The person or group who will physically operate or carry the object.
3. **A toll path.** The Tally must be able to identify what is paid and when.
4. **Witnesses.** At least two independent witnesses must understand and freely accept the disclosed role after full disclosure. The operator and materially affected people must also understand the limits and retain a real chance to refuse; a Custodian's substitution does not count as their consent.
5. **A term.** The use lasts for a stated duration or until a stated condition ends.

A person can change a term only by opening a new settlement. It is not possible to quietly reinterpret an active charter.

The founder's mark is the sole emergency exception. It can append existing active charters to a visible forced record without fresh independent voluntary witnesses and without a term limit, but that record is not a valid consensual charter. The exception does not create consent, authorize a new use, or remove the recorded toll and backwash. Because a forced record is only an emergency claim, a later valid consensual closing may expressly replace it for the named lots by identifying the override, stating the remedy, and receiving acceptance from at least two fresh independent witnesses.

### Renewal and change

Using an anchor lot changes its conditions. A route may become shorter but require a different kind of passenger. A shelter may become safer but refuse people without a recorded name. A repaired object may offer a new use while retaining an old toll. A renewed lot is therefore a new negotiation, even when the object looks unchanged.

The Tally records the current terms. It does not guarantee that the object will obey the same terms after a person dies, leaves, or changes their mind.

### Removal and destruction

An anchor can be moved from its Fault only through a valid charter. A narrow, previously closed **inactive custody charter** is the exception for an unopened object: it names the finder, the receiving bonded depot, the custody term, two witnesses, and a remedy for an unclaimed delivery. It grants physical custody only and never authorizes activation. If such a transfer is missing, incomplete, or unrecorded, moving the object leaves the new custodian exposed as a bare claimant. It can be destroyed, but destruction does not cancel the rule. It removes the visible handle and usually produces a **blind change**: the affected rule continues in an uncontrolled form, or the local service disappears. This is why experienced communities destroy an anchor only after building a replacement or accepting the loss.

### Backwash

When a charter expires, is revoked by breach, or is abandoned before its toll is paid, the unpaid cost spills into nearby active lots. The result is called **backwash**. Backwash may appear as a physical hazard, a memory loss, a social accusation, or a rule that spreads beyond its original boundary. A false reading can therefore kill people after the object has left the room.

Backwash can be reduced, transferred, or pooled, but never erased. A public lot that fails visibly can distribute the loss among known bearers before it becomes a disaster.

## Use rights and ownership

A person may physically own an inert object. The Tally does not grant ownership of a rule or a person. Activating an anchor without a charter leaves the operator as a **bare claimant**: the Tally assigns the immediate toll to them and may refuse to recognize any later claim.

A valid **charter** grants only a defined use of an object for a stated term. It does not grant ownership, the right to alter the object, the right to compel another person, or the right to resell the underlying rule without a new settlement. A founder-mark forced record is enforced as an emergency claim over already active charters, not as ownership of a lot or a person's valid consent.

A charter must include:

- the lot's name and condition;
- the use and its limits;
- the bearer, beneficiaries, and affected community;
- the duration and territory;
- the toll and the point at which it is paid;
- the release, return, or destruction condition;
- at least two independent witnesses and a way to dispute a breach.

Money is one valid form of consideration. So are food, labor, care, a route, a skill, a share of future harvest, or a promise that can be checked. The Tally can record a promise; it cannot decide whether the promise was kind or whether the person had meaningful alternatives.

A public lot may place a dangerous object in documented public custody under a stated steward term. The registrar, the seller, and the current public steward must be present to open the case, and the term must be renewed or allowed to expire in public. Custody limits handling; it does not create a new use, ownership right, or bearer role.

## Geography

### Alder Reach

A river city built on layered floodworks and old tram tunnels. The center is partly flooded, but elevated wards remain connected by footbridges and a damaged rail line. Alder Reach has the region's most developed public auction tradition and the largest concentration of unlicensed salvage.

Important locations:

- **Lot 17:** Adrian's converted tram depot and auction room.
- **Ward Nine:** a dense residential district of kitchens, small workshops, and suspended walkways.
- **St. Orra Underpass:** the first major Fault; the Last Fare is found in a stranded bus.
- **The Brass Verdict Hall:** a municipal chamber where charter disputes are heard.
- **Hearth Line:** the proposed cross-fault bus and freight route.

### The Salt Verge

Dry agricultural country west of the basin. Cooperative farms grow salt-tolerant grain, preserve seed, and trade water. Faults there often involve seasons, storage, and the right to plant. The Verge is the first region to prove that public markets can feed a city without becoming a private land company.

### The Needle Tramway

An elevated rail network linking Alder Reach to the coast. The line is physically intact in places, but the signals carry different rules in different valleys. Broken signals create Faults that move with the trains. Caravans and signal keepers have more influence than local governments.

### The Quiet Orchard

An agricultural region where the ordinary cycle of growth and memory has become unstable. A tree may remember a person who never lived there. A harvest can erase a local skill. The Orchard is home to memory keepers, seed brokers, and people who refuse the Tally's records.

### Brass Coast

Tidal shipyards, salt flats, and floating markets. Its anchor lots govern tides, hull memory, and the right to leave a shore. The Coast is the first place where the market becomes a regional infrastructure rather than a local survival mechanism.

### The Crownless Uplands

High ground above the basin where several settlements share a single watershed. The Uplands are politically independent, religiously skeptical of the Tally, and rich in old civic records. Their refusal to accept a central registry makes them a useful test of whether a public market can exist without a capital.

### The Unmapped Reach

Beyond the established routes are places that cannot be reached safely without a charter. The Unmapped is not a final hidden kingdom. It contains independent communities, abandoned infrastructure, and unanswered questions. The series reveals enough to show that the world continues, while leaving some geography intentionally unresolved.

## Factions and institutions

### The Civic Wardens

Municipal emergency workers who mark safe zones, inspect charters, and run evacuation services. The Wardens began as public employees. Some departments still believe in shared protection; others use emergency powers to control access to scarce routes.

### The Lot Guilds

Independent valuers, inspectors, and auctioneers licensed to read ordinary anchor lots. The Guilds preserve technical knowledge and provide an important check on Adrian, but their senior councils profit from certification and secrecy.

### The Underwriters

Firms that pool tolls and guarantee trade routes. They make large-scale movement possible, but many use deferred labor, inherited obligations, and secret addenda to turn risk into ownership. Underwriters are not inherently evil; their model becomes dangerous when private credit replaces public accounting.

### Road Houses

Independent caravans, repair crews, and settlement councils. Road Houses keep the basin supplied and often have better local knowledge than the Wardens. They distrust permanent central registries but can be captured by charismatic leaders.

### The Unlisted

People who have been erased from official records, refused registration, or disappeared when a toll was assigned to the wrong person. The Unlisted include smugglers, fugitives, people protecting children from state conscription, and communities that remember the cost of being visible. They use paper copies, songs, and trusted witnesses to maintain continuity.

### The Quiet Registry

An administrative faction that treats omission as a form of peace. It removes unstable names, closes dangerous lots, and withholds information that could cause panic. The Registry's public services are often effective. Its leaders eventually discover that a stable record can be built by making certain people disappear.

### The First House

The remnant of the unfinished Common Measure. It claims the right to hold the Tally's unresolved obligations until a final settlement. Most First House members are archivists, emergency officials, and people who believe the original system cannot survive without a single guarantor. Their existence is the political shadow of Iven Tallow.

## Social consequences of the Tally

- **Names become infrastructure.** A missing name can mean denied shelter, water, or compensation.
- **Memory tolls create hidden inheritance.** A family may not know which promise or skill a charter consumed.
- **Public lots are political.** A market that lists tolls openly can challenge a powerful guild; a market that hides them can be used to control labor.
- **Technical knowledge is not neutral.** The person who understands a charter decides which costs are visible and which remain “private.”
- **Uncertainty becomes a weapon.** Counterfeit readings, missing witnesses, and altered terms can look as valid as honest ones until a failure exposes them.
- **The apocalypse changes work.** Auctioneers, witnesses, route keepers, memory stewards, toll insurers, and record repairers become as important as fighters and engineers.

## Concrete object examples

| Lot | Use | Toll | Standing and failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Last Fare** — brass bus token | Opens one route across a Fault for twelve minutes | Each traveler loses the memory of why they were traveling | The route must have a driver, a return point, and a passenger who can remember the destination; repeated use can strand the route permanently |
| **The Hearth Bell** — cracked emergency bell | Calls everyone within a district to a designated shelter | Everyone who hears it has their name recorded for one night, exposing Unlisted people and refusing them entry to some shelters | A community must provide a shelter and a way to remove the record; a false alarm leaves a bell unable to ring for a month |
| **The Salt Loom** — wooden shuttle | Repairs irrigation channels and stores one season of seed | Takes a year of memory from one worker and binds that worker to the channel until it is repaired | A worker who does not consent cannot be made to bear the loom; if the channel fails, the stored season is lost |
| **The Cinder Compass** — child's compass | Points toward the nearest person in immediate danger | Compels the bearer toward that person and prevents the bearer from abandoning the search | The bearer must be able to give physical aid; a false emergency can expose a whole convoy |
| **The Quiet Shoe** — one repaired boot | Lets one person cross a rule-bound boundary without waking its guardian | Removes the traveler's ability to recognize home for one night | The traveler must return before dawn or be treated as Unlisted by every ward along the route |

These examples are limits, not abilities that can be stacked without limit. Two compatible anchor lots can be adjacent, but their tolls may interact badly.
