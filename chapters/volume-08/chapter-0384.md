# Chapter 384: The Hand That Opened in the Middle of the Word

---

The twelfth of the tenth month came in wet again and the boards at the end of that trestle table were damp by ten, and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen. The man of fifty-six read the four figures out loud over the clerk's shoulder and got all four, and the clerk of nineteen years did not stop writing while he said them and did not have to be asked to.

"**The board outside that room carries two hundred and ninety-two days and the train on the siding carries six hundred and eight, and nobody has put a line on a board in a hundred and ninety-five days counting that first day in, and a line in a lot book is a line about a lot and is not a line on a board.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the bid stands at forty-two days and was not run on one of them today.

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in the bottom of that ditch before nine and came up the bank at about ten with his right hand in the pocket of his coat, which he does not usually do, and stood at the end of that trestle table for about two minutes without saying anything to anybody.

---

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against had the reading written out on a sheet and came to that table at about half past eleven with it. He was not asked to bring it and he had it in his coat, and everybody in that yard saw him take it out and nobody said one word about that either.

He said the first of the five things a document that sets a lot out has to say, and the first of them is the lot, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got a hundred and sixty.

"**This is a building with two doors and about nine feet of brick in the end wall and no window in it, and there is a plank door in that end wall with a nail in a hasp and a curtain on a nail inside the door, and a table with four legs stands against the outside wall of it and there is a three foot ditch behind it with about a foot of standing water in that ditch this morning, and the water in the ditch and the water inside the building are the same water and both of them come off the same low ground four hundred yards from here, and it has been catching that water for eleven years and nobody has ever once been asked what it is worth, and I am saying all of it out loud because a lot is a thing and not a figure and I have not got to the figure.**"

Then he said the second of the five, and the second of them is the term, and he said it in seventy-eight words and did not hurry it and did not put anything in front of it.

"**The term is a month. A month because that is the shortest interval anybody in this district has any evidence for, and because whoever holds a thing has to be able to lose it, and because somebody has to be able to sleep in it. I said the reason out loud on the day I set it and I am not going to say it a second time in a yard to keep another man on his feet.**"

Then he came to the third of them, which is the toll, and his right hand opened about four inches in the middle of the sentence.

It was not dramatic. He did not swear and he did not stop for a minute first. The hand went flat and the sheet went off the top of that table with the corner going over the front edge and landed about a foot away on the wet boards with the damp coming up through it, and everybody close enough saw it go and two of them came forward and one of them got there first.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing got there with his right forearm and put it flat on the sheet and held it against the boards for about as long as it took him to get his knee down, which is about nine seconds, and he did not pick it up and he did not read it and he did not turn it over.

Then the man the figure of twenty-one years is against started again from the top. He said the first of the five things again, all of it, and then the second, and then the third, and he said all three holding the sheet in his left hand, and nobody in that yard said one word while he was doing it. He did not say the fourth. He has got to the fourth three times in a month and stopped three times, and the fourth of those five is a person, and there is no person in that yard.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing had his right forearm on that sheet for about eleven minutes and then took it off and squared it up against the edge of the table with the heel of his hand and gave it back, and the man the figure of twenty-one years is against took it in his left hand and put it inside his coat.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a reading was begun twice this morning and finished once, and that the second beginning is the part a ledger usually leaves out, and that a lot was under another man's forearm for about eleven minutes, and that a lot in a man's hands is not held by him, and that he was not asked to hold it and said so out loud at the time.

The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about one and nothing went into it by anybody. The clerk of nineteen years entered that she was not asked to write a name in it and is not going to, and that a column with a word over it is not a column of people, and that a man who has read a thing out loud in a yard is a figure about a reading and not a figure about the man.

---

The man of about nineteen was in the second chair from about half past eleven until about one with his own page face up in his lap, and he did not count one thing in that yard for an hour and a half. Two people in that yard noticed that he was not counting and said so to each other, and he said one thing out loud to nobody, and nobody counted it.

"**There is a difference between a figure and a thing somebody said while they were holding a piece of paper with a hand that does not close, and I am not going to put the second one in the same column as the first one.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man who has not counted a thing has not refused to count it and cannot be counted either way, and that the record about the not asking says not asked.

Two people in that yard said out loud that the fourth of those five things ought to be whoever was in the second chair this morning, and the man of about nineteen said that he was not going to be the fourth of anything and that a chair is a chair. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud that the fourth of those five is a person and not a chair, and that a person is better than a chair but not much, and nobody in that yard said the word keeper out loud today.

Then two people in that yard came and asked the man who mends fencing what his forearm had been doing for eleven minutes, and he told them, in about four sentences and in the ordinary way, that his forearm had been lying on a wet sheet in a yard so that the man who was reading could say the same thing twice without the paper going into the mud, and that he had not been asked to and had not said yes, and that if he had said yes it would have been a thing done to him, and that lying on it is not the same as saying yes because nobody has said anything to him at all.

Two people in that yard said that was a fine distinction and three of them said it was the only distinction there is, and what the book says about it is that a man who puts his forearm on a thing for eleven minutes has not agreed to hold it and has not refused to hold it and is not entered either way.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a road came up that lane at about half past two with his boots black to the ankle, stopped at the end of that table for about two minutes, read the four figures off the boards and got all four, and said one thing out loud to nobody about the sheet that had gone into the wet, and the man of about nineteen counted it and got fifty-five.

"**That sheet came down wet and it is going to be soft at one corner by tomorrow and a thing that is soft at one corner is a thing that is not going to be there in a month, and I am not saying that to anybody in particular, I am saying it about paper.**"

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in that ditch with his hook and his bundle until about half past four and came up the bank past that yard. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his cart and said one thing out loud at about four to about four people, and nobody counted it and nobody has claimed it since.

"**A chair is a chair and a forearm is a forearm and I have not got either of mine and that is the whole of what I know about this morning.**"

Nobody asked him about his hands and he was given nothing, and his tent is still rolled on the back of that hand-cart and has not gone up on any day since the thirtieth of the sixth month, and about four people in that yard said out loud that a man who put a chair down four days ago is the man anybody would come to about a wet sheet, and he said that a chair is what he has and a sheet is not a chair.

Along that lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall on the eighty-third night of his run, with his coat on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and about four people in that yard said good afternoon to him as they came down the lane and he said it back, and he was not asked to sit in the second chair and has not refused to and is not entered either way.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets and filled them at the trough and carried them up the two goes, and she read the one line in that lot book standing up on her way past and she did not read it back out loud, and nobody asked her to, and she was not asked and has not offered.

That bid is open forty-two days and was not run on this day and nobody proposed anything about closing it. That term on that reading is thirteen days past and did not renew. The reading of that lot is begun and is not finished and it was begun twice this morning and finished once, and the fifth of the five things a document that sets a lot out has to say is a remedy and there is nobody in this district who can use one.

A clerk of nineteen years entered at about seven that the sheet came out of the man's coat and went back into it inside forty minutes, and that the lot book is on the end of that trestle table under the stone where it has been since the thirtieth of the ninth, and that a man who begins a thing twice in a yard and gets through it once has not done that thing twice, and that nobody in that yard has said a word about what his hand did and a clerk has not entered that his hand did anything, and the page says not asked.
