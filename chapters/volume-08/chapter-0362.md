# Chapter 362: The Twenty-First

---

The twenty-first of the ninth month came in flat and cold with the light coming off the low ground under the cloud the way it does about two days in that month, and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten. There were four figures on the end of the trestle table and the frost was still in the shadow of the east wall and had gone off the boards by eleven. The man of fifty-six read the middle one of the four out loud over the mender's shoulder and got the other three, and said nothing further, because saying them was the job and there was no pay in it. Along the lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the sixty-first night of his run and sixty-one days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands.

"**Two hundred and seventy days on that board and five hundred and eighty-six days on the train on the siding, and the days nobody has entered anything since the twenty-fourth of November stand at three hundred, and two hundred and sixty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the four hundred and eleven on the sheet at that gatepost had not moved a figure this morning and is a figure about the people who answered a door and not about anybody in that yard, and that the man of about sixty-four was not asked which of the sixty-one nights of that run he had not slept on, and that a figure about a man's own nights is a figure about a night and not about the man, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

Nothing happened in that yard on the twenty-first of the ninth month that had not happened in it the day before, and that was the whole of it, and about nine people standing in that yard worked that out inside about four minutes without one of them putting it into words.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was at the end of that trestle table from about ten until about one with the cloth on his left hand and about nine inches of fresh wire in the fingers of his right one, and he bent it and put it down and picked it up again about four times in an hour and about four people in that yard watched him do it and none of them asked him what the wire was for. The man who digs loam came in at about eleven, looked at the four figures from about nine feet off without going near them, and went out again at about a quarter past eleven and said nothing to anybody on his way through. The man who keeps a road came up that lane at about two with his boots black to the ankle and went straight out of that yard and up that road, and nobody in that yard asked him where he was going and he did not say, and nobody in that yard said one word about the four days either.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds out of that ditch was in the bottom of it with his hook and his bundle until about half past three and came up the bank past the door of that building without saying one word about the building. At the foot of the east wall the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up for anybody who will use them had his hand-cart against the brick with the tent still rolled on the back of it, and it has not gone up on any day since the thirtieth of the sixth month, and he stood against that brick for about an hour and about nine people in that yard walked past him and none of them asked him about either of those things.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about two with two empty buckets and filled them at the trough and put one of them inside that building and carried the other one up the two goes of the bank on her own, and she did not ask anybody anything on this day and nobody in that yard asked her anything, and every question anybody has asked in that yard this month has been about a bucket.

---

The clerk of nineteen years went up that bank four times that day and the fourth time she did not go.

She went at about half past eleven and again at about one, and again at about half past three, and each time she was gone about nine minutes and came back down that lane with the page still under her arm, because the first of the two books in that room is the only place a page in this district gets put where a person could come in and read it. At about five she stood at the foot of that bank with the day's page under her arm and she did not go up it.

She stood there for about nine minutes with the mud-coloured light going flat around her and the fanlight over that door not lighting anything behind it, the way it has not lit anything since the day that door was put on. About four people in that yard saw her standing at the foot of that bank not going up it, and none of them said anything to her about it, and she did not say anything to them, and then she turned round and went back into that yard and put the page on the end of that trestle table under a stone that was already there.

---

Then the woman of fifty-eight, who was coming down that bank at about five with two empty buckets of her own, stopped at the foot of it, and she said one thing out loud, in the ordinary voice, to about nine people, and the count came to a hundred and forty and the boy wrote it out in his own hand.

"**Put it on the table there and a person can walk up to it and read it. Put it in that room and a person cannot, because there is a shut door on that room and a step of two stones and a light over it that does not light anything, and a person who has not been let in does not know that a page is in there at all. I have been coming up and down that bank for nineteen years and I have never been let into that room, and I have never asked to be, and I am not asking this morning. I am saying that a page on a table is a page a person can read, and that is all I am saying about it, and I have got two buckets to carry up.**"

About nine people in that yard turned round and looked at the clerk of nineteen years, and the clerk of nineteen years did not say one word back to her and did not look up from the stone. About four people in that yard said nothing at all. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said thank you to her from about nine feet off the end of that table and nothing else, and she said nothing back to him about it, and the page stayed on the end of that trestle table under a stone.

A woman who has carried two buckets up that bank for nineteen years and has asked three questions in that time, and every one of them about a bucket, said one thing out loud in that yard about a page and was not asked for it and cannot be counted either way, and the record about the not asking says not asked. A page on the end of a table in a yard is not a book a stranger can walk up to and read, because a table in a yard is not furniture and it is not a door and it is not a shelter, and a page has to be behind something before it is behind anything at all, and a clerk did not ask her what she meant by it and a clerk entered what she said.

---

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud at about ten past five, in the ordinary voice, to about nine people, and what he said was counted and got a hundred and fifty-four, and it is in the minute in his own words.

"**I have bent about nine feet of wire in that yard in the first week of this month and I have never found out what it is for and nobody has asked me, and a woman has just stood at the foot of that bank and told about nine of us that anybody at all can walk up to a page and read it. I want it written down that she said it and not me, because I have been standing at that end of this table for a month saying the other half of whatever it is somebody else has just said, and that is not the same trade. The wire is not a page and the wire is not a lot and I am not a man who fixes things for a district. I mend fencing. I would like it noticed that I have said that on a day when nothing happened.**"

About four people in that yard said nothing back to what she had said and about four of them said that the day had not been nothing, and the man of fifty-six said that he had been in that yard at ten and at four and would be there again in the morning and that was how the day went, and nobody in that yard put those two answers together. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man who has said out loud in a yard that he is not a man who fixes things for a district has not refused a post and cannot be counted either way, because there was no post and there is not one, and that the wire was not asked for and he did not offer it.

---

The rest of that afternoon went quietly and the man the figure of twenty-one years is against was in that yard from about ten until the light went, and he did not go up that bank, and about four of them noticed it before anybody spoke and said nothing to each other about it. He stood about nine feet off the end of that trestle table with his right hand in his coat and his left hanging at his side and he did not look at the page on the end of it and he did not ask anybody about the page on the end of it, and nobody in that yard asked him anything on this day or on any of the days of that run, and he was not asked where he had been on any of them.

The reading of that lot stands where it stopped on the sixteenth, at the third of the five things a document that sets a thing out has to say, and it has not got to the fourth of them and the fifth of them has not been said either, and the man who convened the bid was about nine feet off the end of that trestle table for the whole of the afternoon and did not try to go on with it, and nobody in that yard asked him to and nobody in that yard asked him not to.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the twenty-first of the ninth month is entered on the page as a day she entered out of a ladder and did not ask anybody about, and that a ladder is a figure about a ladder, and that a day this district has not put anything in it is a day and not a finding, and that there were about nineteen people in that yard this morning and about four in it at half past seven in the evening, and that every one of the things said out loud in it this morning had been said out loud in it on some day before it, and that a clerk who could have made a finding out of that has not made one.

The light went off that yard at about half past eight and the number on that sheet is still what it was at ten. The rival record has been in force a hundred and eleven days and has been read out seven times in that yard and had its eighth reading refused out loud with a reason on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month, and it is unanswered, and there is still no date in any ledger in this district for the next reading. The page with the twenty-first of the ninth month on it was still on the end of that trestle table under a stone at about half past eight, and it was still on it in the morning.

**THE TWENTY-FIRST OF THE NINTH MONTH WENT PAST IN THAT YARD LIKE EVERY OTHER DAY OF THAT RUN, AND A CLERK ENTERED IT OUT OF A LADDER AND DID NOT ASK ANYBODY ABOUT IT, AND A WOMAN OF FIFTY-EIGHT SAID OUT LOUD THAT A PERSON CAN WALK UP TO A PAGE AND READ IT.**
