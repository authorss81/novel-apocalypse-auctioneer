# Chapter 381: A Minute in the Chair

---

The ninth of the tenth month came in cold and clear, with the first of the light on the roof of that building and about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten. The man of fifty-six read one of the four figures off the boards at the end of the trestle table out loud over the mender's shoulder and got the other three, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing stood at that table with the cloth on his left hand and a knot in about nine inches of wire in the fingers of his right one.

"**Two hundred and eighty-nine days on that board and six hundred and five days on the train on the siding, and two hundred and eighty days separate the second of January and this morning, and the pool of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given is a hundred and ninety-nine days.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the lot book on the end of that trestle table has five ruled lines in it and one line written in them, and that a chair with a person in it for about a minute is a figure about a sitting and is not a figure about the person who sat in it, and that nobody in that yard asked her to enter anything about the chair and she has offered nothing.

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale came down that lane at about ten with her sleeves turned back and stood at the far end of that yard for about half an hour with her arms folded, and nobody in that yard asked her a question and she did not ask one, and what the book says about it is that a person standing in a yard who has not been asked anything has not refused anything and cannot be counted either way.

---

At about half past ten the man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up out of that ditch with his right arm held down at his side and the wet of that ditch standing in the legs of his breeches to the middle of his thigh, and he came across that yard to the end of that trestle table and sat down in the second chair.

Nobody said anything at all. About nine people in that yard were standing close enough to see it happen and not one of them said a word, and about four of them looked at the chair and then at him and then at the chair again.

He was in it about a minute.

Then he put his right hand on the edge of the table and got himself up out of it in one movement, which is not a thing he could have done out of the bottom of that ditch at six o'clock the night before, and he stood about nine feet off the end of that table with the water coming off him onto the ground and said one thing out loud, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got sixty-five.

"**I sat down because my legs went in that ditch at about half past nine and I am telling this yard that before about four of you tell it the other way, because I can hear about four of you saying it was the chair, and it was not the chair, and I have been digging since before this yard had a table in it.**"

And four of them had already said out loud, among themselves, that it was the chair. He heard all four of those. He stood there while the four of them said it a second time between themselves and he did not say one word about them and he went back down the bank into that ditch at about ten to eleven.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man sat in a chair for about a minute and got up again, and that a figure about a sitting is not a figure about the man who sat in it, and that two people in that yard said a thing about that chair out loud and not to him, and that the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about nineteen did not count the minute. Nobody asked him whether he was going to, and he wrote on his own page, in his own hand, that he did not count it and that he does not know why, and that is the second line he has put on that page this month that is not a figure.

---

The rest of that morning went the way that yard goes. The man of fifty-six read the four figures off those boards at about one and got all four, and four or five of them said the top one of the four to each other behind him and got it right. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing went up the lane to the fence end at about ten without stopping, which he does most mornings, and the yard got on with the day without him at that end of the table.

About four people in that yard asked him, when he came back at about half past two, whether the wire was for a gate, and he said out loud that he does not mend gates, he mends fences, and that a gate is a fence with a hole in it, and then he said that a man who cannot close his left hand has to be told the difference by somebody else, and went back to the boards.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a road came up that lane at about three with his boots black to the ankle, read the four figures as he went past, got all four, and went on up the lane, and nobody in that yard asked him about the eleven miles and nobody in that yard said one word to him about the chair, and what the book says about that is not asked.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in that ditch with his hook and his bundle from about nine until about half past four and came up the bank past that yard without saying one word about the building. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them had his hand-cart against the east wall with the tent still rolled on the back of it, and it has not gone up on any day since the thirtieth of the sixth month, and nobody asked him about it and he was given nothing, and two people in that yard said to him that a chair is not much of a thing to walk forty yards for, and he said that it was on the cart.

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against was in that yard from about ten until about five and looked at that chair twice, once at about half past ten while the man who digs loam was in it and once at about three with nothing in it, and said nothing at all about it either time, and a man looking at a chair is not a man deciding anything and is not a man who has been asked.

Two people in that yard started to say something to the woman of fifty-eight between about eleven and about four and did not finish it, and what they finished it with was the weather.

At about half past three the man of about thirty-one who digs loam was at the fence end of that lane on his way up out of it and the mender was at the end of that trestle table with about nine inches of wire and nothing to do with it, and the two of them said one thing each, in the ordinary way, and about four people heard both.

The man who digs loam said that a chair is a thing for a person who has been on their feet all morning, and that he was on his feet all morning, and that a chair is the only thing in that yard that has been put there for no reason at all. The mender said that is the first thing anybody has said out loud about that chair that he agrees with, and that he had been thinking the same and had not said it, and then he said that a person is not a thing a chair was put there for, and went back to the wire.

Neither of those is a finding. About four people in that yard said one of them was a better thing to say and about four said the other one was, and two men said two things in a lane and neither of the two things is a reason, and the reason a chair was put down is in the mouth of the man who put it down and in nobody else's.

---

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up the bank at about four and stood at the end of that trestle table for about two minutes with the water still coming off him, and looked at the second chair, and did not sit in it, and went back down and was in the bottom of that ditch again by about a quarter past four.

About four people in that yard said out loud that he would sit in it again later in the day. About four other people in that yard said that a man who has said he sat for his legs is not a man anybody is going to count, and neither of those is a finding, and a chair is not a job and a man in a chair is not a figure about the chair.

He was in the bottom of that ditch before nine and came up at about half past ten and went back down at about ten to eleven and was in it again from about two until about half past three, and was back down in it at about a quarter past four until the light went, and his right arm did not go above the level of that shoulder on any part of this day, and nobody in that yard asked him about that arm and he was not asked about it.

The man of about nineteen counted two things out loud in that yard this afternoon and both of them were figures, and he wrote both of them down and put nothing else on that page. The first of the two was the man of fifty-six reading the boards at one o'clock and he counted that and got forty-nine. The second was the road man reading the same four figures as he went past at three, and he counted that one too and got fifty-one, and three or four people standing near that table said that two counts of the same four figures two hours apart is not a check on anything.

He said out loud that it is not a check on anything and wrote that down as well.

---

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, carried them up the two goes herself, and read the one line in that lot book standing up on the way past, the way anybody can, and nobody said anything to her about it and she said nothing to anybody. She has read that one line on her way past more mornings than anybody in that yard has counted, and a figure about a run of mornings is a figure about mornings and is not a figure about the woman, and she has not been asked for a figure and does not have one.

The man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart was out on that road and did not come up that lane at all today. That is three days in this week he has not come up it, and on those days nothing in this district binds, and nobody in that yard said that out loud, and the record about the not saying says not asked.

Along that lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the eightieth night of that run and eighty days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands, and nobody in that yard said one word to him about them, and he was not asked to sit in either chair.

That bid is open thirty-nine days and was not run on this day and nobody proposed anything about closing it. That term on that reading is ten days past and did not renew. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud is ruled and was empty at about six and nothing went into it by anybody, and a second line was not asked for in that lot book and was not written in it.

By the time the light had gone in that yard the second chair was at the end of that trestle table with a wet patch on the boards beside it where a man had stood up out of it, and the man who had put that chair down was up the lane with his tent still rolled on the back of his hand-cart, and one sitting is a figure about one sitting and is not a figure about one person, and nobody has asked her how many there will be and she has not offered to know.
