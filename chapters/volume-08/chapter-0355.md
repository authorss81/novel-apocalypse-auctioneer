# Chapter 355: The Hand That Does Not Close

---

The fourteenth of the ninth month came in with a wind off the low ground that had grit in it and about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten, standing closer in than they had on any of the days before it. The three figures were on the end of the trestle table with a stone on the corner of the day's page. The man of fifty-six read the top one of the three out loud over the mender's shoulder and got the other two, and about four people in that yard were saying the figures to each other behind him and getting them right, and two of them were saying them wrong. Along the lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the fifty-fifth night of his run and fifty-four days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands.

"**Two hundred and sixty-three days on that board and the ninth of the nine printed nights a hundred and forty-six days back, and the table against that wall about four feet high eleven miles off has stood sixty-nine mornings, and the man of about sixty-four has slept on that low wall on fifty-three of the fifty-four nights of that run.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and that it has not moved on any day this month, and that the man of about sixty-four was not asked which of the fifty-four nights he did not sleep on, and that the record about the not asking says not asked, and that a clerk entered that night out of a ladder and did not ask.

---

The reading of that lot went back to the lot at about eleven in the morning, and about nine people went up the lane with it, and that is not a thing anybody called for.

The man who convened the bid had been saying the outside of that building out loud in a yard since the tenth of this month and had not got to the inside of it, and the inside of it is the part a person would want described, because the outside of it is nine feet of brick and a nail in a hasp. He had said out loud in that yard that he was going to describe the whole of it, and about nine people came to hear him finish.

The plank door in the end wall goes in about an inch on its own weight. He shut it on the first of this month and it has gone back in on its own weight since. He put his left hand flat on the face of it and pushed, and it went in about four inches and stopped on the nail in the hasp, which has not moved since the eleventh of the sixth month.

Then he changed hands. His right hand has not closed properly since the tenth of the eighth month, and it has not been any better since the first of this month, and about nine people in that lane were standing where they could see it.

He put his right hand on the edge of that door at the height of the nail and closed it, and it did not close. His fingers went about halfway round the edge of it and stopped, and stayed where they were, and the wood turned under them. He did not take it away. He stood in the lane with his right hand on the edge of the plank door of that building with his fingers stopped halfway round, in daylight, in front of about nine people, for about four seconds.

Nobody said anything in those four seconds. About nine people were standing in that lane and every one of them was looking at that hand and not one of them said a word.

He tried it again. The second time he put the heel of his right hand against the door and pushed with the arm and his fingers tried to hold the edge while the arm pushed, and the door did not move at all that time, and his hand came off the wood and left a mark on it in the grit, about the size of four fingers.

He did not say anything. He was in the middle of a sentence, because he had been describing the inside of that building in the ordinary voice to about nine people and he stopped in the middle of it, and the sentence stopped with him.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came about four steps up the lane and put his right hand on the edge of that door with the fingers of it, and his left hand is in a cloth and has not closed since the eleventh of the sixth month, and he got his fingers round the edge of the plank on the first go because the whole of the strength of that arm is on one side of him. He lifted the door off the hasp about four inches and held it there against the wind, which was coming up that lane at about the rate it does on a day with grit in it.

He did not say yes to anything. Nobody had asked him anything. He had taken hold of a door.

---

Then he read the rest of the lot out loud, in the ordinary voice, in daylight, to about nine people, and the count came to a hundred and seventy-six, and it is in the minute in his own words.

"**There is a bench along the far wall and a stool by the plank door and two buckets of water under the bench, and the front door is on the other side of that room with a handle on it, and the light that gets in here is the light that comes off the mud of that lane, and it does not get past the curtain on the nail. The floor is mud and it has been mud since the rain of the seventh month. That wall is cold enough in the middle of the day to make a man lean on it and be sorry. That is the inside of it and that is the whole of the lot, and I am not the man who called this bid and I have not been asked to read anything out. I am saying it because the man holding that door open has got one hand on it and cannot hold it much longer, and somebody has to say what is in there or nobody ever will.**"

He said that last part in the ordinary voice and about four people in that lane heard him say it. The man the figure of twenty-one years is against stood about four steps back with his right hand in his coat and his left at his side and did not say one word to him about it, before, during or after.

---

The clerk of nineteen years came up that lane about nine minutes after the two of them came back down it, and stood on the far side of that lane with the page under her arm, and wrote for about four minutes, and neither of the two men in that lane asked her what she was writing and she did not ask either of them anything while she did it. What she wrote down was that the man who convened that bid is not the man who read that lot, and that the reading of the lot is begun and not finished, and that the inside of it was said out loud in daylight by a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing. She wrote down that a person who took hold of a door is not a keeper of that building and is not a holder of that lot and is not a bearer of a term and is not a second reader, and that he did not say yes to anything and was not asked for anything.

And she wrote down that the column on that page with a word over it and nothing under it is ruled and empty and stayed empty this morning, and that nothing was put in it by anybody, and that a column with a word over it and nothing under it is a question this district asked itself and did not answer.

Nobody in that lane asked the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing to read anything back from the top. He said the inside of the lot out loud and there was no second half to it, because the second half is a different person and a different hour and there is no figure on any page in this district for who.

---

The rest of that day was the longest part of it.

The man who convened the bid was in that yard from about one until the light went and he did not stand at the end of that trestle table once. He went and stood about nine feet off it, the way he has stood for most of that month, and he put his right hand flat on the boards at about two and left it there, and it did not close on the edge of anything at any point in the afternoon, and he did not try.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came back down that lane at about half past two with grit on the front of his coat and sat down on the bench outside the east wall, which is the bench that is not the bench inside that building, and said one thing out loud to about four people who were still standing about. It came to a hundred and forty-one words and they are in the minute in his own words.

"**I held a door open for about four minutes and I want it written down that I did not take anything. That building is nine feet of brick and a man has been trying to describe it out loud for thirteen days and his hand gave out on the fourth day of it. If I put a mark on anything today it will be dirt on a plank, and there is a mark on that door now that neither of us put there. I am not the reader of anything. I am a man who mends fencing and I have nine feet of wire done and a knot in it and no work in this district since the sixth month, and nobody has asked me for any and I have not offered any and I would rather it stayed that way.**"

About four people in that yard heard that and one of them was the man who convened the bid and he said nothing back to it, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a man who has said out loud in a yard that nobody has asked him for work has not been given a post and has not refused one, and that nine feet of wire with a knot in it is not a lot and is not a toll and is not a figure about anybody, and that the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about three the man of fifty-six read the three figures out loud on his own at the end of that table, with nobody over his shoulder, and got all three, and about nine people heard him do it. A man reading three figures out loud on his own is a man saying a thing out loud and is not a reading, and getting three figures right is not a finding and is not a habit and is not a figure about the man who read them.

At about four the man who digs loam came into that yard and stood at the end of that trestle table for about ten minutes and did not ask anybody anything, and his right arm has not gone above the level of that shoulder since the first of the seventh month, and he did not raise it, and nobody in that yard asked him to and nobody in that yard said a word about it.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past four with two empty buckets and filled them at the trough and put them inside that building and carried the second one up the bank, on her own, and about nine people in that yard watched a man with one hand hold a plank door open for about four minutes in a wind with grit in it and then let it go when his arm gave out, and the door came back in on its own weight and stood at four inches, and the nail in that hasp has not moved since the eleventh of the sixth month and did not move this afternoon.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man whose hand will not close on the edge of a thing is not a figure about that thing, and that the mark his hand left on the plank door is not a mark on a page and carries no job and is not a signature, and that a printed line may not carry a job and a hand may not either, and that the door of that building is not a lot and a lot is not a door and the two are not added together.

At about half past seven the yard was down to about four people. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat beside him, and the man who convened the bid sat down on the low wall about nine feet from him and stayed there, and neither of them said one word to the other, and nobody in that yard said one word to the man of about sixty-four about what he was going to do with his hands.

The light went off that yard at about half past eight. The number on that sheet was still four hundred and eleven and it will be the same figure in the morning. The reading of the lot is begun and not finished. The bid in that yard has been open thirteen days and nobody has offered anything at all for it, and a reading is not a document and is not a fourth of the three documents this district does not own and is not a seventh of the six instruments it has built and not named.

**A HAND THAT HAS NOT CLOSED SINCE THE TENTH OF THE EIGHTH WENT ONTO THE EDGE OF A PLANK DOOR IN A LANE IN DAYLIGHT AND STOPPED HALFWAY ROUND IT, AND A MAN WITH ONE HAND IN A CLOTH TOOK THE DOOR OFF ITS HASP AND SAID THE INSIDE OF THE LOT OUT LOUD, AND THE COLUMN FOR THE NAME OF WHOEVER READ A THING OUT LOUD IS STILL RULED AND STILL EMPTY.**
