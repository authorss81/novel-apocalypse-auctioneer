# Chapter 342: A Lot With Two Doors

---

The first of the ninth month came in warm and the yard of Lot Seventeen had about nineteen people in it by ten, the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing had the three figures on the end of that trestle table and the man of fifty-six read the other two out loud over his shoulder and got them right, and the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of the low wall along the lane with his coat folded beside him for the forty-second night and has been in this district forty-one days.

"**Five hundred and sixty-six days on the train and two hundred and eighty days that nobody has entered anything, and two hundred and forty-one days from the second of January, and the first day of the eighth month is thirty days past this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and has not moved, and that its own age as a figure about the figure is ninety-one days, and that it is not a day-count for the sheet. She entered that a man of about sixty-four has slept on that low wall on forty of the forty-one nights of that run and that nobody asked him about the one he did not, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

He spent the morning of this day on the thing itself, which is nine feet of brick at the near end of the lane behind that bank. There is no window in it. There is a plank door in the end wall and a nail in a hasp that has not moved since the eleventh of the sixth month. There is a curtain on a nail, a bench, a stool, two buckets of water, and a table with four legs against the outside wall. Behind it there is a ditch about three feet deep with about a foot of standing water in it, and the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds out of that ditch was in the ditch with a hook and a bundle until about one and came up the bank past both of them without saying anything about the building.

He said it out loud twice, standing at the top of that ditch, in the ordinary voice, to nobody, and both times he got as far as the buckets and then went on to the table with four legs and lost the thread, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, who has had a cloth on his left hand since the eleventh of the sixth month, stood about nine feet off him and let him finish both times without helping him.

The second time he got it out whole. He said it to himself and then he said the first nine feet of it again to check that it came out the same, and it did, and he stood there in the sun for about four minutes afterwards looking at the ditch and not writing anything down, because he is not a clerk of a house and is not a clerk of anything and there is a clerk of nineteen years in this district and she is the only person who may write in either of the two books four hundred yards from that gate.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man describing a lot out loud to himself twice in a morning is not a reading, and that a thing read back from the top by a second person is not a thing said twice by one person, and that a man who has said a thing out loud to himself has not made a finding and cannot be counted either way, and that nobody asked him to say it a third time and nobody has asked him since.

At about two the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up that lane with nine sacks in the bed of it and stopped in that yard for about four minutes to shift two of them, and the man the figure of twenty-one years is against was about nine feet off the end of that trestle table and did not ask him whether he would be in that yard in the evening, and did not ask him for anything at all, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that he did not ask, and that a man who came into a yard for four minutes to shift two sacks is not a man anybody has put a question to, and that this is entered and is not a finding.

---

At about four he asked one question in that yard, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people. Nobody answered him.

He asked whether anybody in that yard had a figure for what that building was worth for a month. About nine people looked at the ground and one man of fifty-six said that he had a figure for what his own fence had cost him and that it was not the same thing, and the yard was quiet after that and stayed quiet. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man who has asked a question in a yard and got no answer has not been refused and cannot be counted either way, and that a figure a man names out loud about his own fence is a price and is not a rate.

He said one thing out loud at about half past four, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people, and a man of about nineteen counted what he said and got one hundred and twenty-seven, and it went in the minute in his own words.

"**I have asked that yard a question this afternoon and I did not get an answer, and I am not going to ask it again tonight, and I am not going to stand here and pretend to anybody that a question nobody answered is a figure somebody gave me. I told this yard yesterday evening that I would ask about nineteen people one question at about six tonight about a building with two doors. I am going to do that and I am going to do it with nothing in my hands, because I have not got a figure for that building and I am not going to invent one at ten past six to save myself the look of a man who has not got one.**"

About nine people came in off the lane after that and stood at the back of that yard, and one woman asked the man of about nineteen whether this was the thing that had been going on for a fortnight, and he told her it was not, and nobody in that yard said the word out loud.

---

At about six the light had not gone yet. The west side of the wall was still warm and the shadows of about nineteen people had got long enough to touch across the middle of that yard, and the man the figure of twenty-one years is against stood at the end of that trestle table and described the lot out loud. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was nine feet off his left shoulder with his right hand flat on the boards. The woman of fifty-eight came down the bank at about half past five with two buckets and put them down by the trough and stayed. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of the low wall with his back to the lane.

The man of about nineteen counted it and got two hundred and three, and it went in the minute in his own words.

"**A lot is a thing a bid is for and I am describing one. It is about nine feet of brick at the near end of the lane behind that bank, and there is no window in it. There is a plank door in the end wall and there is a nail in a hasp and that hasp has not moved since the eleventh of the sixth month. There is a curtain on a nail. There is a bench, and a stool, and two buckets of water, and a table with four legs against the outside wall. Behind it there is a ditch about three feet deep with about a foot of standing water in it and a man cuts reeds out of it and knows in four seconds what that building is, and he said so once on the eleventh of this last month in that yard and I am not going to say it for him. That is the lot. I have not got a figure for it and I am not going to read one off it, and a figure about a lot is a figure about a lot and is not a figure about anybody standing in that yard.**"

He stopped there. Nobody said anything back. A clerk of nineteen years entered that the term on it is a month, and entered the reason and not only the figure: **that a bell rung when there is nothing to answer it cannot be rung again for a month, and that a month is therefore the shortest interval this district has ever had to keep a thing true across, and that a term shorter than the shortest interval this district has evidence for is a term this district cannot check, and that nobody in that yard has asked for a longer term and that a clerk has not proposed one.**

Nobody asked for a longer term. The man of fifty-six said one sentence out loud, which was that a month was a strange length to pick for a building and that he was not going to be the one to argue with it, and a clerk entered that a man who has said out loud that he is not going to argue with a figure has not agreed with it and cannot be counted either way.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a bid needs a reader, and a holder whose name goes in a column, and a toll, and that on this evening it had none of the three, and that a bid that has not got any of the three has not failed and has not been entered anywhere, and that the reader is somebody and there is no figure on the page for who.

---

At about a quarter past six his right hand closed on the edge of that trestle table and did not open again until about ten to seven, and about nine people in that yard saw it happen and not one of them said anything about it. He kept the other hand at his side and finished the sentence he was in the middle of, and then he put both hands in his coat, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a man describing a lot out loud with a hand that has not closed since the tenth of the eighth month is still describing the lot, and that a hand is not a figure about a building and is not a price on it, and that nobody in that yard asked him about the hand and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall for the whole of it and did not stand up, and nobody said one word to him about what he was going to do with his hands, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a clerk may not say that a man whose trade is over is a failure and may not say the other thing either.

No offer stood. The bid did not close. A bid in a yard on one day is not a market, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that, and nobody in that yard said it out loud.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud at about seven, in the ordinary voice, to about nine people who had not gone home, and a man of about nineteen counted what he said and got one hundred and fifty-two, and it went in the minute in his own words.

"**A bid cannot close in this district for three reasons and I have said all three out loud in this yard and nobody has said one word back to me about any of them. It needs a reader, and a reading is a thing said out loud and then read back from the top by a second person, and a thing read back by nobody binds nobody. It needs a holder, and the holder's name goes in a column, and that column is ruled and empty and it was ruled before I was in this basin. And it needs a toll, and a toll is a thing a holder pays under a term, and there is no holder in this yard to pay one and there is no way in this district to pay a person for holding a thing. That is not me being hard. That is the shape of it.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a thing said out loud in a yard at about seven in the evening is a thing somebody said and is not a reading, and that a reading is read back from the top by a second person and that there was nothing to read back on this evening, and that a second reader is nobody in particular and is not a post and is not a column and is not anybody anybody has appointed.

**AND ON THE FIRST OF THE NINTH MONTH, AT ABOUT SIX IN THE EVENING, IN THE YARD OF LOT SEVENTEEN, IN FRONT OF ABOUT NINETEEN PEOPLE, THE FIRST BID IN THIS DISTRICT WAS OPENED ON A BUILDING WITH TWO DOORS AND NO FIGURE WAS READ OFF IT, AND THE TERM ON IT WAS SET AT A MONTH WITH THE REASON ENTERED, AND NO OFFER STOOD AND THE BID DID NOT CLOSE, AND THE FIGURE ON THE SHEET AT THAT GATEPOST WAS STILL FOUR HUNDRED AND ELEVEN.**
