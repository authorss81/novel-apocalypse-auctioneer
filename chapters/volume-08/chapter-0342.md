# Chapter 342: A Lot With Two Doors

---

The first of the ninth month came in warm, earlier than any day of that run, and by ten the yard of Lot Seventeen had about nineteen people in it and the lane was busy both ways. The three figures were on the end of the trestle table. The man of fifty-six came in behind the mender and read two of the three out loud over his shoulder that morning and got them right and the mender did not turn round. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of the low wall along the lane with his coat folded on the stones beside him. Forty-two nights of his run, and forty-one days in this district, and nothing in his hands.

"**Five hundred and sixty-six days on the train and two hundred and eighty days that nobody has entered anything, and two hundred and forty-one days from the second of January, and the first day of the eighth month is thirty days past this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and has not moved, and that its own age as a figure about the figure is ninety-one days, and that it is not a day-count for the sheet. She entered that a man of about sixty-four has slept on that low wall on forty of the forty-one nights of that run, and that nobody asked him about the one he did not, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

He spent the morning of this day on the thing itself, which is nine feet of brick standing at the far side of that bank, where the lane turns and goes on toward the low ground.

There is no window in it. He put his left hand flat on the plank door and pushed it, and it went in about an inch on its own weight, and he shut it again. Inside, the light that got in was the light that came off the mud of the lane, and it did not get past the curtain on a nail. The bench and the stool and the two buckets of water and the table with four legs set against the outside wall were all of the furniture there was, and the wall on the far side from the sun was cold enough in the middle of that morning to ache if you leaned on it. Behind the building the ground drops away into a ditch about three feet deep, and there has been about a foot of standing water in the bottom of it since the rain of the seventh month, and the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds out of that ditch was in there with a hook and a bundle until about one, and came up the bank past the door without saying one word about the building or about anything else.

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against had rehearsed it twice in his head on the way up the bank and it was not the same thing on the page as it was in his hands. That was the whole of his trouble and it was the whole of everybody's trouble in that yard, and it did not have a name in it, and he had spent a fortnight not putting a name to it.

He said it out loud twice, standing at the top of that ditch, in the ordinary voice, to nobody. Both times he got as far as the buckets, and both times he lost the thread there and carried on to the table with four legs as though the buckets were a doorway into the sentence after them. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing stood about nine feet off him with his right hand hanging and let him finish both times without helping him, which was a thing he had decided about at six in the morning and had not told anybody he had decided.

The third time he got it out whole. He said it to himself, and then he said the first nine feet of it again to check that it came out the same, and it did, and he stood there in the sun for about four minutes afterwards looking at the water in the ditch and writing nothing down, because he is not a clerk of a house and is not a clerk of anything, and there is a clerk of nineteen years in this district and she is the only person who may write in either of the two books four hundred yards from that gate.

A man describing a lot out loud to himself twice in a morning is not a reading. A thing read back from the top by a second person is not a thing said twice by one person. A man who has said a thing out loud to himself has not made a finding and cannot be counted either way, and nobody asked him to say it a third time and nobody has asked him since.

---

At about two the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up that lane with nine sacks in the bed of it and stopped in that yard for about four minutes to shift two of them, and the two he shifted were not the two he came for. The man the figure of twenty-one years is against was about nine feet off the end of the trestle table with a thing he had been saying in his head all morning still unsaid, and he did not ask the man with the cart whether he would be in that yard in the evening. He did not ask him for anything at all. A man who came into a yard for four minutes to shift two sacks is not a man anybody has put a question to.

At about four he asked one question in that yard, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people. He asked whether anybody there had a figure for what that building was worth for a month.

Nobody answered him. About nine people looked at the ground. The man of fifty-six said that he had a figure for what his own fence had cost him and that it was not the same thing, and the yard was quiet after that and stayed quiet. A man who has asked a question in a yard and got no answer has not been refused, and cannot be counted either way. A figure a man names out loud about his own fence is a price and is not a rate.

He said one thing out loud at about half past four, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people, and a man of about nineteen counted what he said and got one hundred and twenty-seven, and it went in the minute in his own words.

"**I have asked that yard a question this afternoon and I did not get an answer, and I am not going to ask it again tonight, and I am not going to stand here and pretend to anybody that a question nobody answered is a figure somebody gave me. I told this yard yesterday evening that I would ask about nineteen people one question at about six tonight about a building with two doors. I am going to do that and I am going to do it with nothing in my hands, because I have not got a figure for that building and I am not going to invent one at ten past six to save myself the look of a man who has not got one.**"

About nine people came in off the lane after that and stood at the back of that yard, and one woman asked the man of about nineteen whether this was the thing that had been going on for a fortnight, and he told her it was not, and nobody in that yard said the word out loud.

---

At about six the light had not gone yet. The west side of the wall was still warm to the back of the hand and the shadows of about nineteen people had got long enough to touch across the middle of that yard, and the man the figure of twenty-one years is against stood at the end of that trestle table and described the lot out loud. The mender was nine feet off his left shoulder with his right hand flat on the boards, the way a man stands at a thing he is being careful of. The woman of fifty-eight came down the bank at about half past five with two buckets and put them down by the trough and stayed. Along the lane the man of about sixty-four had his back to everything and his coat on the stones.

He had said it to himself three times that morning and none of it had held still, and now there were nineteen people in front of him and it had to come out in that order or not at all. It came out in that order.

The man of about nineteen counted it and got two hundred and three, and it went in the minute in his own words.

"**A lot is a thing a bid is for and I am describing one. It is about nine feet of brick at the near end of the lane behind that bank, and there is no window in it. There is a plank door in the end wall and there is a nail in a hasp and that hasp has not moved since the eleventh of the sixth month. There is a curtain on a nail. There is a bench, and a stool, and two buckets of water, and a table with four legs against the outside wall. Behind it there is a ditch about three feet deep with about a foot of standing water in it and a man cuts reeds out of it and knows in four seconds what that building is, and he said so once on the eleventh of this last month in that yard and I am not going to say it for him. That is the lot. I have not got a figure for it and I am not going to read one off it, and a figure about a lot is a figure about a lot and is not a figure about anybody standing in that yard.**"

He stopped there. Nobody said anything back, and he had not expected them to, and the not expecting was the only preparation he had made.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the term on it is a month, and entered the reason and not only the figure: **that a bell rung when there is nothing to answer it cannot be rung again for a month, and that a month is therefore the shortest interval this district has ever had to keep a thing true across, and that a term shorter than the shortest interval this district has evidence for is a term this district cannot check, and that nobody in that yard has asked for a longer term and that a clerk has not proposed one.**

Nobody asked for a longer term. The man of fifty-six said one sentence out loud, which was that a month was a strange length to pick for a building, and that he was not going to be the one to argue with it. A man who has said out loud that he is not going to argue with a figure has not agreed with it and cannot be counted either way.

A bid needs a reader, and a holder whose name goes in a column, and a toll, and on this evening it had none of the three, and a bid that has not got any of the three has not failed and has not been entered anywhere. The reader is somebody. There is no figure on the page for who.

---

At about a quarter past six his right hand closed on the edge of that trestle table, all at once, the way a hand does when it stops being told what to do, and it did not open again until about ten to seven. About nine people in that yard saw it happen. Not one of them said anything about it. He kept the other hand at his side and finished the sentence he was in the middle of, and then he put both hands in his coat, and a man describing a lot out loud with a hand that has not closed since the tenth of the eighth month is still describing the lot. A hand is not a figure about a building and is not a price on it. Nobody in that yard asked him about the hand, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Along the whole of that evening the man of about sixty-four stayed where he had put himself at the foot of the low wall and never once got up off it, and nobody in that yard said one word to him about what he was going to do with his hands. A clerk may not say that a man whose trade is over is a failure, and may not say the other thing either.

No offer stood. The bid did not close. A bid in a yard on one day is not a market, and nobody in that yard said it out loud.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud at about seven, in the ordinary voice, to the nine people who had not gone home, and the minute took one hundred and fifty-two words of it, in his own words.

"**A bid cannot close in this district for three reasons and I have said all three out loud in this yard and nobody has said one word back to me about any of them. It needs a reader, and a reading is a thing said out loud and then read back from the top by a second person, and a thing read back by nobody binds nobody. It needs a holder, and the holder's name goes in a column, and that column is ruled and empty and it was ruled before I was in this basin. And it needs a toll, and a toll is a thing a holder pays under a term, and there is no holder in this yard to pay one and there is no way in this district to pay a person for holding a thing. That is not me being hard. That is the shape of it.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a thing said out loud in a yard at about seven in the evening is a thing somebody said and is not a reading, and that a reading is read back from the top by a second person, and that there was nothing to read back on this evening, and that a second reader is nobody in particular and is not a post and is not a column and is not anybody anybody has appointed.

**THE FIRST BID IN THIS DISTRICT WAS OPENED AT ABOUT SIX IN THE YARD OF LOT SEVENTEEN, IN FRONT OF ABOUT NINETEEN PEOPLE, ON A BUILDING WITH TWO DOORS AND NO FIGURE WAS READ OFF IT. NO OFFER STOOD. THE SHEET FOUR HUNDRED YARDS UP THE LANE STILL SAID FOUR HUNDRED AND ELEVEN.**
