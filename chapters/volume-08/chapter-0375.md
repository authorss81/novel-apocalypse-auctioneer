# Chapter 375: A Question Asked About a Person

---

The third of the tenth month came in flat and cold with the mud of that lane gone hard again and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was at the end of that trestle table with his arms folded and did not read the four figures out loud, and the man of fifty-six read them over nobody's shoulder and got all four.

"**Eighty-nine mornings that table against a wall about four feet high eleven miles off has stood, and the ninth of the nine printed nights is a hundred and sixty-six days back, and nobody has put a line on a board in a hundred and eighty-six days counting the first of the fourth month in, and the pool of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given is a hundred and ninety-three days.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that a line ruled with a straight edge in a lot book in a yard is a line about a lot and is not a line on a board, and that the count of days on which nobody has put a line on a board did not move this morning and no line was put on one.

---

The reading of that lot stands where it stopped. It has got to the fourth of the five things a document that sets a thing out has to say, twice, and the fourth of the five is a person, and the fifth of them is a remedy and there is nobody in this district who can use one. About nineteen people in that yard know that the fourth of those five is a person, and about four of them have known it since the twenty-third of this month, and every morning since then the same four people have looked at the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing standing at that table and about four of them have looked at the man the figure of twenty-one years is against.

At about eleven the man of about nineteen who counts what people say in daylight yards put his own page under his arm, walked round the end of that trestle table, and asked the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing a question in that yard in the ordinary way, in front of whoever was standing there, and the count of what he asked came to a hundred and fourteen.

"**Is the fourth of those five things you? Because the fourth of them is a person and there is one person in this yard that everybody looks at when that reading gets said, and I am asking you in a yard and not in a room, and I am a man who counts what people say and I am nobody's clerk and you are not going to be one either, and if the answer is no then I will write down no and if the answer is yes then I will write down yes and I will write down which one you said, and that is all I am going to do with it.**"

About nine people in that yard went quiet at that, and about four of them did not, and nobody said anything.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing looked at the boards at the end of that table for a while, and then he said one thing out loud in that yard in front of about nine people, and the boy counted what he said and got a hundred and fifty-one.

"**It is a person and I am not going to say whether it is me, and I am not going to say it is not me, because either of those would be me standing in a yard telling about nineteen people that a thing this district has made is mine, and it is not mine, and I could not carry it if it were. The fourth of those five is whoever is standing at that table when the thing gets said, and that is a different person most mornings of a run, and you have all watched it be a different person, and today it would be him if he said the fifth one, and he is not going to say it, and neither am I. Write down that it is a person. That is the whole of it and it is not a no and it is not a yes.**"

The boy wrote the question and the answer down before the mender had finished the second half of his sentence, which nobody asked him to do, and about four people in that yard watched a man of about nineteen put two lines on a page in his own hand about a man of about thirty-four standing about nine feet away from him.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a question asked in that yard this morning was a question and is not a bid and is not an offer and is not a ninth of anything, and that a man who has answered a question in a yard has not agreed to a thing and cannot be counted either way, and that the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud is ruled and is still empty and no name went into it at any point in this month.

---

At about half past eleven the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing took his arms down and said that he was going to the fence end of the lane for a while, and he went, and he was not at the end of that trestle table at any point in the rest of that day.

The four figures were read out loud twice more after that, once by the man of fifty-six at about one and once by the man who digs loam at about half past three with his right arm held down at his side, and about four people in that yard said the top one of the four to each other behind them both times and got it right both times, and nobody said where the man who used to be at that table had gone, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man who keeps a road came up that lane at about two with his boots black to the ankle and read the four figures off the boards as he went past and got all four and went on up the lane, and nobody in that yard asked him about the eleven miles, and about four people in that yard said afterwards that a man who keeps a road is not a bearer of it and a man who walks a road is not a keeper of it, which is true and which that road has not heard said out loud in that yard before.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in the bottom of that ditch with his hook from about nine until about four. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them had his hand-cart against the east wall with the tent still rolled on the back of it, and it has not gone up on any day since the thirtieth of the sixth month, and nobody in that yard asked him about it.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally was not in that yard today and nobody in that yard said where he was, and the record of it says not asked.

The man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up that lane at about five with his shafts down, stopped at the head of it, and did not come into that yard, and about four people in that yard looked at the head of that lane and then looked away again, and about nine people in that yard watched him not come in and nobody sent anybody out to him and he was not asked to do one thing or say one thing. He sat on the front bench of it with his hands on his knees and then backed out of that lane and went up the road.

---

At about one four people in that yard, one after another and in the ordinary way, said some version of the same thing to the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing about the end of that lane, and asked him in four different words whether he was coming back to that table, and he gave them four answers and none of them was yes, and he said out loud that a man who has answered a question in a yard does not have to stand in the same place afterwards, and that he would be at that table in the morning if there was something at it.

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against said one thing about that at about two, out loud, in that yard, to whoever was near him, and nobody counted it and nobody has claimed it since.

"**He is not a holder of anything and he has not taken anything and nobody has given him anything, and if I say one word to him about where he is standing then I have made it a post in about four sentences and there is not a post in this district. He will be at that table or he will not be at that table. That is a thing about him and not about me and I am not going to stand in that yard and manage a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing.**"

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against said one thing out loud in that yard this afternoon that nobody counted, and a thing said in a yard and not counted is a thing that was said and is not a figure, and a man who has said out loud that a thing is not a post is not a holder of it and is not a registrar of it either, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was empty at about two this afternoon and nothing went into it by anybody.

The boy wrote the question and the answer on his own page, two lines, in his own hand, and then stood there with the page in his hand for a while, and did not put it on the end of that trestle table, and did not take it to a book, and did not hand it to the clerk of nineteen years, and nobody in that yard asked him what he was going to do with it. A clerk entered that two lines on a boy's own page are two lines and are not a record of a finding and are not a document this district does not own and did not become a fourth of the three, and that a page a person can walk up to and read is a page, and that the boy was not asked to do anything with it and nobody was asked and the record says so.

Along that lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the seventy-fourth night of his run and seventy-four days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands, and nobody in that yard said one word to him about that at any point today.

The woman of fifty-eight was at the trough at about half past five with two empty buckets and carried them up the two goes of that bank herself. She read the top line of the page a boy put on that table two days ago standing up on the way past, and she said nothing about it, and nobody in that yard said anything to her about it, and about four people in that yard who were still standing there said afterwards that a page anybody can walk up to and read is worth two of the ones that are not.

A woman of fifty-eight has asked three questions in nineteen years and every one of them has been about a bucket, and she was not asked anything today, and what she said out loud on the way past was said to two or three people who were not listening to her and is not a finding.

Nothing was decided in that yard today and nothing needed to be. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was still at the fence end of that lane at about seven, on his own, with his right hand round a length of wire he had not started on, and half of them who could see him from the end of that lane said nothing about that either.

At about seven, at the fence end of that lane, the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud to nobody in particular and then went home, and the words were that he would be at that table in the morning if there was something at it.
