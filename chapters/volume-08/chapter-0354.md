# Chapter 354: A Rule in a Room

---

The thirteenth of the ninth month came in with the sky low and the light coming off the low ground in a straight line under the cloud, the way it does about two days in that month, and by ten the yard of Lot Seventeen had about nineteen people in it. The three figures were on the end of the trestle table. The man of fifty-six read one of the three out loud over the mender's shoulder and got the other two and went back to the fence end of the lane without stopping. Along the lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the fifty-fourth night of his run and fifty-three days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands.

"**Five hundred and seventy-eight days on the train on the siding, and two hundred and ninety-two days that nobody has entered anything since the twenty-fourth of November, and two hundred and fifty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move this morning, and that the rival record has been in force a hundred and three days and is unanswered, and that its eighth reading was refused out loud with a reason on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month, and that a refused reading is not a reading, and that there are seven readings and not a ninth.

---

He went up that bank at about two in the afternoon. He did not knock, because the door was standing open on its latch the way it stands when a clerk is in the room, and a clerk of nineteen years was in there with a page under her arm and the first of the two books open on the shelf at a page with a ruled line on it and a word over the line and nothing under the line.

The woman who keeps those two books was not in that room. He did not ask anybody where she was, and that was the arrangement between them and it was his own and it was not on a page, and it was not the first time in ten days that he had arrived at a door standing open and said nothing about the fact that it was.

The second of the two books was on the shelf with its cover shut and it stayed shut, and nothing in that room that afternoon touched it, and a clerk did not enter that it was not touched, and neither of them said anything about it.

He put his right hand up to take the first of the two books off the shelf and could not do it. His fingers would not go under the edge of it. He tried it twice, the way a man tries a thing he has done a hundred times, and the clerk of nineteen years came about nine feet over and took it off the shelf with one hand and set it down open in front of him, and then stepped back and did not say anything about having done it.

A person who has had a book taken down off a shelf for him has not been given a reader and has not been given a post, and a hand that will not go under the edge of a book is not a figure about a book.

Then it came.

It did not come on the shelf and it did not come out of either book, and it was not on the board outside under the fanlight that lights nothing, and there was no sound of anybody saying it. The clerk of nineteen years had her eyes on the page in front of her the whole of the time it was there, and when he asked her afterwards whether she had seen anything come into that room she said that she had been looking at a page and had not, and that she believed him and would not put it in a book.

> A use ends when its term ends. A person who held it may not hold it again without a new reading.

He read it twice. It was the same both times and it did not change in the room, and there was nothing in it that this district had not said out loud in a yard or put on a page four hundred yards from a gate at some point since the first of the sixth month, and it was not a document, because it was not on anything, and nobody could put it in a pocket, and nobody could read it back from the top.

He said one thing out loud in that room, in the ordinary voice, to the one other person in it, and the clerk of nineteen years wrote it on the day's page in one line.

"**That is a rule. It is not a document and it is not a column and it is not a page and it did not ask anybody anything and it did not wait to be asked. And it is not a seventh of anything this district has built and not named, because a thing that arrives in a room without a man carrying it in is not an instrument a person has built. I am not writing it down and I am not asking her to.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a rule is a rule and is not a document, and that it is not a seventh of the six instruments this district has built and not named, and that a person in the room is not a bearer of a rule and is not a keeper of one, and that she did not write it on the page and was not asked to.

He stood in that room for the best part of an hour after that and did not read anything else. The light through the fanlight over that door does not get past the first two feet of the floor and never has, and the rest of the light in that room came off the mud of the lane through the open door, and by about half past three the mud-coloured light had gone flat and there was no way of telling what time it was in there except by the noise of the lane.

The first of the two books lay open on the shelf where the clerk of nineteen years had put it down, at a page with a word over a ruled line and nothing under the line. He looked at that page for a while without touching it and then said, out loud, to nobody, that he had worked out in the lane coming down that there is no figure on any page in this district for who a second reader is, and that there is a rule in a room that says a thing only holds while somebody reads it again, and that between those two facts there is a gap with nothing standing in it, and that he was not going to say what is on the far side of that gap out loud to a woman of fifty-eight or to any of the people in that yard, and that he had said it out loud in a room instead.

The clerk of nineteen years did not look up from the page and did not say anything, and a clerk did not enter it, because what a man says to a room that has one other person in it is not a figure about either of them.

---

He came down that bank at ten past three with it in his head and put what it said in front of about nineteen people in that yard, and he argued with it out loud in the ordinary voice, and he lost.

What he said is what a man says when he has found out in a room that there is a rule and has worked out in the lane coming down that the rule is the only thing in this district that could make what he has been doing for six days hold without his name going anywhere. The rule is not a name. The rule does not put a person on a figure. A second person reading a thing back from the top is what binds it, and a rule that says a use ends with its term is a rule that says a reading has to be made again, and he has been the only person making one.

He said it out loud in that yard at about a quarter to four, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got two hundred and thirty, and it went in the minute in his own words.

"**I have been in this basin since the first of the sixth month and I have said out loud in this yard that a use is a thing a holder has under a term, and I have said it as though that were mine to settle. There is a rule in a room four hundred yards up that bank, and it says a use stops when the term on it stops, and it says nobody picks one up again without somebody reading it afresh. I did not agree to it. Nobody in this yard agreed to it and I was not asked and I am not refusing it either, because you cannot refuse a rule the way you refuse a person. But I am going to say the one thing I can see wrong with it out loud in daylight in front of all of you, and then I am going to stop. If a rule says a thing only holds while somebody reads it again, and there is one man in this district who reads things out loud, and that man cannot ask anybody to be the second one, then the rule has handed the whole of it to a man who cannot keep it. That is what I am arguing. That is all of it. And it does not answer me, and I did not expect it to.**"

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud at about four, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people. What he said was counted and got a hundred and sixteen, and the man of about nineteen had it in his own words.

"**I have stood at the end of that table every morning of that run with three figures on it and a man reading them out loud over my shoulder for nothing. I am not saying he is the only one who can. I am saying that a rule that came into a room about two o'clock this afternoon with nobody carrying it in is not one of the six things this district has built, and it is not a seventh of them, and I am not going to be a man in this yard who stands about in a seventh thing. Write that down or do not. I have not got anything to add to it.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a rule is not a document, that it is not a fourth of the three documents this district does not own, and that a man who has argued with one has not refused it and cannot be counted either way, and that the record about the not asking says not asked, and that nobody in that yard was asked to agree to the rule and that agreement is not consent and is not a signature and is not a name.

The rest of that afternoon went like the rest of that afternoon. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing bent about nine feet of wire and put a knot in it about the length of a finger, and about four people in that yard asked him what the wire was for, and he said it was for nothing, four times, in the ordinary way, and the same answer given four times in an afternoon is a shape noticed four times and is not a finding and is not a habit.

At about five the man of about thirty-four who keeps a road came through that yard on his way out of the district for the day, and stopped at the end of that trestle table and read the three figures off the boards on his own without saying anything to anybody, and got them, and went out again, and nobody in that yard asked him to read anything and nobody told him that he had just done a thing.

A man who knows where a road goes is not the holder of it, and a man who reads three figures off a table he was not asked to read is not a reader and is not a second reader and has not been given a post, and a clerk entered that nobody was appointed to anything this afternoon and no column was filled.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets and stood at the foot of it for about four minutes with about nine people in that yard, and she did not ask anybody anything, and nobody in that yard asked her anything about the figure the man of fifty-six had named the day before.

The light went at about half past eight. The man of about sixty-four was still at the foot of that low wall with his coat beside him and his hands where he keeps them, and nobody in any yard of this month has said one word to him about what he is going to do with them. The number on the sheet at that gatepost was still four hundred and eleven and the reading of the lot is begun and not finished and the bid in that yard has been open twelve days.

**A RULE CAME INTO A ROOM FOUR HUNDRED YARDS UP THAT BANK AT ABOUT TWO IN THE AFTERNOON AND NAMED WHAT A USE IS AND WHEN IT STOPS, AND THE MAN WHO ARGUED WITH IT OUT LOUD IN THAT YARD IN FRONT OF ABOUT NINETEEN PEOPLE LOST THE ARGUMENT, AND THE RULE DID NOT ANSWER HIM, AND NOBODY WROTE IT DOWN.**
