# Chapter 386: Four Hundred Yards Up a Bank

---

The fourteenth of the tenth month came in flat and grey and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten. The man of fifty-six read the four figures off those boards out loud over the digger's shoulder, since the digger was standing at that table for once with his bar hooked over the back of it, and he got all four.

"**Two hundred and ninety-four days on that board and six hundred and ten on the train on the siding, and nobody has entered anything for three hundred and twenty-four days, and a toll is a thing a holder pays under a term and this district has no holder of anything.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the rival record has been in force a hundred and thirty-five days and the age of the figure about that figure is a hundred and thirty-five days, and that the two of those are two figures and are not one.

---

At about eleven the man the figure of twenty-one years is against sat down in the second chair at the end of that trestle table for about a minute, and the yard looked at him and about four of them said nothing and two of them said that is the first one today.

He got up out of it on his own in one movement, which is not a thing the hand that does not close can always do, and what the book says about it is that a sitting is a figure about a sitting and is not a figure about the person who sat in it, and that a man who sat in it and got up in one movement has not been asked to sit in it and has not refused to and cannot be counted either way.

Two people in that yard said out loud, in the ordinary way, that they had all four of them expected the second chair to be empty for another week, and two more of them said that a chair somebody put down for anybody who will use them is a chair that anybody is going to use, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said that he had put nothing down and had been asked nothing and would like that written down, and he was not asked and did not offer and is not entered either way.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was asked by two people in that yard, in the ordinary way, whether he had ever sat in that chair, and he said that a man who has been in a ditch until half past four does not sit down in a yard, and two people in that yard said that was the best answer anybody had given all morning and three of them said it was not an answer at all.

The man of about nineteen wrote two lines on his own page before eleven that morning and put nothing else on it, and the first of the two was the four figures the man of fifty-six had read out at ten and the second of the two was the same four read out again at about eleven by the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, and two people in that yard said that two men reading the same four numbers in an hour is a thing this yard has never had before, and the man of fifty-six said that it was because the digger had been standing at the table and that it would not happen again.

---

He went up that bank at about half past twelve.

He has not gone up it since the thirteenth of the ninth month. About nine feet of bank, and the lane at the top of it, and about nine feet of brick at the near end of that lane with a shut door in it and a step of two stones and a fanlight over the door that lights nothing. He has asked for nothing on any day since the twenty-ninth of the ninth and he asked for nothing today.

He was out of breath at the top of it and he stood on the top step for about four minutes before he did anything at all, and two people in that lane went about what they were doing and did not look at him, and a man who comes up a bank out of breath and stands on the top of it for four minutes before he moves has not been asked to hurry and has not refused to.

He did not knock.

About four minutes later the door opened about the width of a hand and then the width of a shoulder, and the woman who keeps those two books was standing in it, and neither of them said anything for about a second and a half. There was a light behind her from further inside that did not reach the step, and the shelf with the two books on it was about nine feet back from where she was standing, and he did not look at it and did not go past her.

Then he asked her one question, and the man of about nineteen, who had come up that lane behind him about nine feet back and had stopped at the corner of the building and could hear all of it, counted what he asked and got fifty-six.

"**Does a thing in this district bind when the second person who reads it back is the only other person in the room, and does it bind in a book, and does it bind in a yard, or is it only ever a thing that happens in a yard with about nineteen people standing in it.**"

She said no in about four seconds, and gave a reason of her own, and the count of what she said was thirty-nine.

"**No. Nineteen years I have written down what people said in this room and I have never once written down what they ought to do, and I am not starting this week, and that is the whole of it.**"

He did not ask her twice. He said thank you and she shut the door and the fanlight over it went on lighting nothing. Neither of them said one word about anything else, and he did not ask her where anybody was and she did not offer him anything to sit on.

The second of those two books was on a shelf about nine feet in and it was not opened and nothing in that room touched it, and the one line in it in her own hand that nobody asked her to write was not read aloud by anybody and he was not told what it says, and a clerk of nineteen years did not enter that it was not read because she was not asked about it.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a door was opened and that a door being opened is not a question asked and not an answer given and not a consent to anything, and that one question was asked on this bank this afternoon and it was answered once, and that he did not ask it twice, and that the record about the not asking says not asked.

About a minute after the door shut he said one thing out loud on the step of two stones to nobody, in the ordinary way, and the man of about nineteen counted it and got fifty-five, and two people in that lane heard it and none of them said anything.

"**Thirty-one days. I have not been up this bank since the thirteenth of the ninth and I came up it today with nothing in my hand, and I am going back down it, and there are two sheets of print on this step and I did not move them and I am not going to.**"

Then he went back down that bank, and he was slower coming down than he had gone up, and he put his right hand inside his coat on the top step and it did not come out again that day.

The man of about nineteen walked back down behind him and did not catch up with him and did not say anything on the way, and at the foot of the bank he stood for about a minute and then went up the lane toward that yard on his own. He wrote nothing on his own page about it. Two people in that yard asked him in the ordinary way at about two in the afternoon what had been said at that door, and he said out loud that he had been nine feet back round a corner and that a man nine feet back round a corner is not a clerk and is not anybody, and one of the two said that was a good answer and the other said that he had heard all of it and could have said it and did not, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The clerk of nineteen years was at the foot of that bank at about twenty past one with her page under her arm and she saw him come down, and she said nothing to him about where he had been, and two people in that lane said nothing about it either, and she saw a man come down a bank and did not ask him and has not offered to.

---

The rest of that afternoon went ordinary and nobody in that yard said one word about a bank for the rest of it. The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in the bottom of that ditch from about two until about half past four and got his right hand above the level of that shoulder once, for about a second, putting a bar into a stone, and took it straight back down, and nobody in that yard asked him about the second it took.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a road came up that lane at about four with his boots black to the ankle, read the four figures as he went past, got all four, and went on up, and nobody in that yard asked him about the eleven miles and nobody in that yard said one word to him about a bank. The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in that ditch with his hook and his bundle until about half past four. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was at the east wall with his cart and was given nothing.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally came down that lane at about three with a flat book under his arm and stood at the far end of that yard for about twenty minutes, and nobody in that yard asked him anything and he asked nothing, and he did not go near the lot book and did not look at the second line in it, and about four people in that yard said out loud that he had come down that lane for something and he said that he had come down it because it is dry at that end, and the page says not asked.

Nobody sat in either chair after eleven o'clock. The bid stands at forty-four days and was not run today and nobody proposed anything about closing it. The term on that reading is fifteen days past and did not renew. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and was empty at about six, and a rule said out loud in a yard on the tenth of this month is not in it and is not going in it.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets and filled them at the trough and carried them up the two goes herself, and she read both lines in that lot book standing up on her way past, and nobody in that yard said one word to her about a bank or a door or a question.

Along that lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall on the eighty-fifth night of his run and eighty-five days in this district, with his coat on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody in that lane said one word to him about what two people said at a door four hundred yards from where he was sitting, and he was not asked.

That night the second chair was at the end of that trestle table with nobody in it, and the lot book was under the stone, and the man who put that chair there was up the lane with his tent still rolled on the back of his hand-cart.
