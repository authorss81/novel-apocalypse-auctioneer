# Chapter 352: Nobody Holds It

---

The eleventh of the ninth month came in hard and bright, with the frost still in the shadow of the east wall at ten and gone off the boards of that trestle table by eleven, and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen. The three figures were on the end of the table. The man of fifty-six read the top one of the three out loud over the mender's shoulder and got the other two without stopping on them, and there was no pay in it and there had never been any. Along the lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the fifty-second night of his run and fifty-one days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands.

"**Two hundred and sixty days on that board, and the days nobody has entered anything since the twenty-fourth of November stand at two hundred and ninety, and two hundred and fifty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and that it is a hundred and one days old as a figure about itself, and that nobody has put a line on a board on any of the hundred and sixty-third days since the first of the fourth month, counting that first day in, and that a line in a book and a line in a yard and a line on a board are three different things.

---

The question of the toll came up in that yard at about eleven and nobody called for it. The reading of the lot was standing where it had stopped yesterday morning, with the term said out loud and the three other things a document that sets a thing out has to say still not said, and about four people in that yard had gone home the evening before and come back this morning still thinking about it, which is not a finding and is not a habit and is not a figure about anybody.

The man of fifty-six raised it. He did not raise it out loud. He stood at the end of that trestle table for about a quarter of an hour with his hands behind him and said one thing to the mender about a length of wire, and then, without any change of voice at all, he said what his own fence along the trough had come to, which was eleven coppers a week, and he said it to the boards of the table and about nine people in that yard heard it.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that eleven coppers a week is a price a man named out loud about his own fence and is not a rate, and that a rate is a thing that is already true of you whether anybody says it or not, and that no offer was made on that figure and none was refused, and that the refusals of eleven coppers a week are nine and did not move this morning.

Then the mender took it, and he took it the way he takes a thing that has been handed to him, which is without any noise at all.

He said one thing out loud at about half past eleven, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got two hundred and eleven, and it went in the minute in his own words.

"**He has just said what a fence of his own costs a week and I want everybody in this yard to hear where that sits. A toll is a thing a holder pays under a term. Not a thing a person pays to be in a yard, and not a thing a person is given for turning up, and not a thing anybody can be told they owe because they are in a household. A holder. And there is no holder in this yard, because a bid has been open in it for ten days and no offer has ever stood and nobody's name is against anything, and the man who called that bid is the man standing at the end of this table with his hand in his coat. So there is no toll. Not a toll of nothing. No toll, with nothing after it to be filled in, because I am not going to let a clerk write a line under that word with a space for a figure in it, and if one of you writes a figure in it later, that figure is to be completed and a figure that is to be completed is not a rate, it is a hole with a number in it.**"

---

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against was standing about nine feet off the end of that trestle table through all of that with the day's page under his right arm, and about four minutes in, the wind came up that lane the way it does at about the turn of the middle of the day and took the corner of the page off his arm and turned it over.

He got his thumb under the corner of it to fold it down. He got it under the first time and it went over about an inch and sprang back. He got it under the second time and his thumb did not go down past the first joint, and the page stood up off his arm in the wind and the whole of it showed at once, in a yard, to about nineteen people.

He did not try it a third time. He stood there with it for about two seconds and then he said, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing and to nobody else, that he could not hold that page down and that he was sorry about it.

The mender put his own right hand flat over the corner of it, with the cloth still on his left, and held it while the clerk of nineteen years came about nine feet over with her own page and got the first one off him and folded it and put a stone on it. Neither of the two men who had just had hold of that page said one word to the other about it, and neither of them said one word about it afterwards, and about four people in that yard saw the whole of it.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a person who has asked another person to take hold of a page his own hand could not hold has not given that person a post and has not given up one, and that a person who has said out loud in a yard that he could not hold a page has not been given a job and has not refused one, and that the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past two with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, and put one of them inside that building and brought the other one back out and set it down by the door. She stood at the foot of that bank for about four minutes with her hand on the door frame, which is not a door she keeps and has never been asked about.

Then she said one thing out loud, to about nine people, in the ordinary voice, and nobody in that yard had asked her anything, and the man of about nineteen counted what she said and got fifty-five, and it went in the minute in her own words.

"**If that building had a term put on it and there was a toll on it, I would want to know whether the toll was for keeping it or for being in it, and I have never asked anybody a question about money in nineteen years, and I am asking it now about a bucket.**"

A question about a bucket is a question and is not an offer and is not a refusal and is not a bid, and a person who asks a question in a yard and gets no answer has not been refused and cannot be counted either way, and she has asked that woman about a bucket twice in nineteen years and both times about a bucket, and this was the third of them and it was about a bucket.

Nobody answered her. The mender said afterwards, to about four people and not to her, that he did not know, and the man of fifty-six said that he did not know either, and about four other people in that yard said nothing at all, and she picked the bucket up and carried it up the two goes of the bank herself.

The man of about nineteen asked one question out loud in that yard at about three, which is the second time in ten days he has said a thing in it instead of counting one. He asked the clerk of nineteen years, in the ordinary voice and in front of about nineteen people, what a clerk writes under a word when there is nothing at all to write under it.

She said that she writes that there is nothing to write under it, and that it takes a line, and that the line is about the word and not about anybody. He said that a line with nothing under it is a sentence and not a figure, and that he had been told a thing on the third of this month about a column and had not worked out until that evening whether the two were the same thing, and that a person who counts what gets said out loud in a yard is nobody's clerk and is not going to be.

A man who counts what people say in a yard in front of about nineteen people has asked a question and been answered and has not been given a post for either, and two questions a man has asked in ten days are two questions and are not a habit and are not a finding and are not a figure about the man who asked them.

The fifth of the five things this district does not have, which is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, was said out loud in that yard for the second time in four days and it is still not paid and the count is still five, and a woman who carries water to eleven houses is not a person in a household, and a clerk did not ask her about any of that and the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

The afternoon did what the afternoons of that run did. The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was at the top of that ditch at about four with a bar of iron in his right hand held down at his side, and his right arm has not gone above the level of that shoulder since the first of the seventh month, and it did not go above it this afternoon either, and nothing was made of that by anybody. The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds out of that ditch was in the bottom of it with his hook and his bundle until about half past four and came up the bank past the door without saying one word about the building.

At about five the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up that lane and stopped in that yard for about four minutes to shift two sacks out of the way of the ditch, and the man who convened the bid did not ask him whether he would be there in the evening, and the cart went out of that lane about twenty minutes later than it had gone out on any of the days before it, and nobody in that yard paid for those twenty minutes and the man who lost them did not say he had.

The reading of the lot is still begun and not finished. There is still no holder in that yard, so there is still no toll, and there is still no figure on one and none may be put there by anybody in this district, and a figure left with a space for it is not a rate and never was one. The offer that has been on a table in this district for seven months has four lines in it and the fourth of them is still not read out loud in this yard, the refusals to read are nine, the departure is unspent, and there is still no date in any ledger for the next reading, and a toll that cannot be paid is not that fourth line and is not a reading of it.

At about half past seven the yard was down to about four people and the man of about sixty-four was still at the foot of that low wall with his coat beside him, and the man the figure of twenty-one years is against stood about nine feet off the end of that table and did not go up that bank and did not knock and did not ask anybody to do anything for him.

The light had gone off that yard by about half past eight and the number on the sheet at that gatepost was still four hundred and eleven and the rival record has been in force a hundred and one days and has been read out seven times in that yard and had its eighth reading refused out loud with a reason on the twenty-fourth of the sixth month, and it is unanswered, and a toll that cannot be paid is not a reading of it and is not a ninth reading.

**A TOLL IS A THING A HOLDER PAYS UNDER A TERM, AND THERE IS NO HOLDER IN THAT YARD, SO THERE IS NO TOLL, AND A WOMAN WHO HAS NEVER ASKED ANYBODY ABOUT MONEY ASKED ABOUT ONE BY THE BUCKET SIDE AT HALF PAST TWO AND WAS ANSWERED BY NOBODY, AND A HAND THAT COULD NOT HOLD A PAGE DOWN IN THE WIND ASKED ANOTHER MAN TO HOLD IT.**
