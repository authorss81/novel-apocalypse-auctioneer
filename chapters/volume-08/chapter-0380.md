# Chapter 380: Somebody Put a Chair Down

---

The eighth of the tenth month came in with the first of the light on the roof of that building and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten. The man of fifty-six read one of the four figures on the boards at the end of the trestle table out loud over the mender's shoulder and got the other three, and the mender was at that table with his left hand in its cloth and a short length of wire in the fingers of his right one.

"**Two hundred and eighty-eight days on that board and six hundred and four days on the train on the siding, and two hundred and seventy-nine days separate the second of January and this morning, and the pool of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given is a hundred and ninety-eight days.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that this is the last day of the run of figures this district began at the end of the month before last, and that whether anybody is going to read them out loud in that yard tomorrow is not a thing she has been asked and is not a thing she has offered, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The morning went the way this yard goes. The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in the bottom of that ditch with a bar in his right hand held down at his side before nine and worked his way along it. Further along that same ditch the man who cuts reeds was working with his hook and his bundle, and neither of those two men said one word to the other all morning. The man who keeps a road went out of that yard at about ten and did not stop in it, and nobody in that yard asked him anything and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally was not in that yard today. Nobody in that yard said where he was, and nobody in that yard said anything about the six households, and the figure for those has not moved for a hundred and seventy days and came out of a ladder and not out of his mouth and he was not asked for it.

---

At about half past eleven the man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up for anybody who will use them came down that lane with a chair.

He could not carry it. He has had no use in either of his hands since before this district began keeping figures and nobody in that yard has ever asked him about it and nobody in that yard asked him about it today, and so he came down about forty yards of that lane with the chair going along the ground beside him, walking it on with the side of his right boot every few steps, and it went along in the mud and against the stones of that lane and twice it stopped and he got it going again with his hip.

About nine people in that yard watched a chair come down that lane. Not one of them said anything. About four of them said good afternoon to him and he said good afternoon back to those and to nobody else, and nobody in that yard asked him where the chair had come from and nobody in that yard asked him why he was bringing it down that lane at half past eleven in the morning.

The chair had been tied to the front of that hand-cart for as long as anybody in that yard could remember and it came off the cart when he untied it, and he untied it with his forearm against the frame of it and about nine feet of string came down into the mud with it, and he left the string in the mud and nobody picked it up.

He set it down at the end of that trestle table at the corner the other chair is not at, and pushed it square with his boot, and stood there for about a minute, and said nothing at all, and went back up the lane to his hand-cart.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a chair is a chair, and that a second chair put down at the end of a trestle table in that yard this morning is not a sixth of the five things this district protects and is not a seventh of the six instruments this district has built and not named and carries no job, and that a man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up for anybody who will use them is not a keeper of tables and is not a holder of anything, and that nobody in that yard asked him to put it down.

Nobody sat in it today. It stood at the end of that trestle table from about half past eleven until the light went with about nine people in that yard knowing where it came from, and about four people said that a person can read a book standing up and does not need a chair for it, and about four other people said that a chair is a chair, and neither of those is a finding.

There is no figure on any page in this district of how long the first of those two chairs has stood at the end of that trestle table, and nobody in that yard will say one out loud, and a clerk entered that the absence of a figure is not a zero and is not a finding and is not a way of saying that nobody has ever sat in one.

About four people in that yard said out loud, in the ordinary way and among themselves, that they had no idea where that chair had come from, and about four of them said that it did not matter, and four or five others said that it did matter and that they could not say why. An opinion is not a figure, and nobody in that yard was asked for an opinion about a chair.

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against looked at that chair for about a minute and a half at about half past twelve and said nothing at all about it, and four or five others there watched him look at it and none of them said anything to him about it, and a clerk entered that a man looking at a chair is not a man finding something and is not a man deciding anything and is not a man who has been asked.

The man who mends fencing read the four figures out loud at about one and looked at that chair once while he was saying the second one and went back to the boards without stopping.

At about half past four the man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up for anybody who will use them came back down that lane to his hand-cart and stopped at the end of that trestle table on his way past, and said one thing out loud to nobody in particular, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got seventy-seven.

"**I did not put that chair there for anybody. I put it there because it was on my cart and I have been carrying it since before this yard had anything in it worth sitting down for, and I am not asking anybody to use it and I am not going to ask anybody to use it, and if somebody sits in it one day that is a thing they did and not a thing I got.**"

About four people in that yard said, in the ordinary way and among themselves, that a chair is for a person who has been standing a long time, and about four of them said that a person can lean on a trestle table, and three or four more of them said nothing about it at all. None of the three opinions is a use, and a chair is not a job.

The man of about nineteen wrote one line on his own page about that chair at about half past three, in his own hand, and it is on that page today, and a clerk entered that a line about a chair on a boy's own page is a line about a chair and is not a document this district does not own and did not become a fourth of the three.

---

The rest of that day went the way the last day of a run goes.

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in the bottom of that ditch before nine and came up the bank at about ten and went out of that yard, and was in it again from about two until about half past four.

The man who keeps a road came up that lane at about three with his boots black to the ankle, read the four figures off the boards as he went past, got all four, and went on up the lane, and nobody in that yard asked him about the eleven miles and nobody in that yard said one word to him about the chair, and what the book says about it is not asked.

The man who cuts reeds was in that ditch with his hook and his bundle until about half past four and came up the bank past that door.

The man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up that lane late in the afternoon with his shafts down and stopped at the head of it and did not come into that yard, and about nine people in that yard watched him not come in, and he sat on the front bench of it with his hands on his knees and then backed out of that lane and went up the road.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, and carried them up the two goes herself, and she read the one line in that lot book standing up on the way past, and four or five of them said that she reads everything in that yard that is not nailed down and that she has never been asked to.

Along that lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the seventy-ninth night of that run and seventy-nine days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands, and nobody in that yard said one word to him about them, and the fifth of the five things this district does not have, which is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, is still not paid and the count is still five.

At about half past six the clerk of nineteen years folded the day's page and put it inside her coat and went up that lane past the end of that yard and up the bank, and she carried a page up that bank herself for the first time since the twenty-first of the ninth month, and she did not go into that room and she did not knock, and nobody in that yard saw her go up the bank and nobody in that yard asked her where she was going, and it is entered as not asked.

Two pages are still on the end of that trestle table under a stone, one of them with the twenty-first of the ninth month on it and one of them a boy put down there on the first of this month, and carrying one page up that bank this evening was not a finding and was not a habit and was a thing she did, and nobody asked her to.

The term on that reading is nine days past and did not renew. What the reading of that lot has got to is still the fourth of the five things and the fifth of them is a remedy and there is nobody in this district who can use one. That bid is still open and has not been run on one of its days. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud is ruled and was empty at the end of this day and nothing went into it by anybody, and the lot book has five ruled lines in it and one line written in it, and the rival record has been in force a hundred and twenty-nine days and was answered once out loud in a yard on the fourth of this month and is still in force.

By the time the light had gone in that yard, the chair was still at the end of that trestle table with nobody in it, and the man who had put it down was up the lane with his tent still rolled on the back of his hand-cart.
