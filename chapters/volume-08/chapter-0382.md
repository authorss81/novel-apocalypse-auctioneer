# Chapter 382: What a Rule Costs When It Is Free

---

The tenth of the tenth month came in with the wind off the low ground and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten. The man of fifty-six read the four figures off the boards at the end of the trestle table out loud over nobody's shoulder this morning and got all four, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came in that lane at about ten with the cloth on his left hand and about six feet of wire in the right pocket of his coat, which is what he has been bending all month.

"**Nobody has entered anything for three hundred and twenty days since the twenty-fourth of November, and the rival record has been in force a hundred and thirty-one days, and the age of the figure about that figure is a hundred and thirty-one days, and those two are two figures counted off one day and not one figure.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the term on that reading is eleven days past and did not renew, and that a rule said out loud in a yard is not a document and did not become a fourth of the three this district does not own.

Before ten the man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in the bottom of that ditch with a bar in his right hand held down at his side, and the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was about nine feet along the same ditch with his hook and his bundle, and neither of those two men said one word to the other all morning.

---

At about half past eleven the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing asked whether anybody had anything else to say out loud in that yard, and nobody in that yard had anything else, and he said the thing he had come down that lane to say, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got two hundred and two.

"**A thing in this district binds when a second person reads it back from the top and gets it right. It does not bind on a name and it does not bind on a signature and it does not bind on a mark and it does not bind on a person, and if a person puts their name on a thing it does not get them anything and they cannot take it off again either. A second reader is nobody in particular. It is a person standing in this yard who is not the person who said it and is not the man who counts, because the man who counts is the one who catches the mistake and a man cannot be catching the mistake and be the mistake. It is not a post and it is not a column and it is not a steward and it is not a keeper, and there has never been a keeper on a page in this district and a document that names a job that does not exist has found nobody. Whoever is standing here is it, and we will not find it the same way twice, and that is the whole of it.**"

About nine people in that yard listened to all of it. Nobody in that yard said one word for about four minutes afterwards, and about four of them looked at each other, and the lot book on the end of that trestle table was under the stone with the boy's page beside it and nobody touched either.

Then the mender said the price of it out loud, in the same voice, and nobody counted it and nobody asked him to.

"**And the price of it is that nobody can be made to keep it. There is no post to put in it and no name to write against it and no hand that has to hold anything, and the day one of us decides not to do it there is nobody above him to be told and nobody behind him to be asked, and the only thing that happens is that nothing binds that day. I have been in this yard five months and I have got exactly one hand that closes and I am telling you the price of this rule in front of about nineteen people because if I do not say it in here then somebody will say it in a month and it will sound like a discovery.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the rule was said out loud in that yard in the ordinary voice with nobody's name in it, and that the price of it was said out loud after it in the same voice, and that nobody was made to keep it and nobody was asked to keep it, and that a thing said out loud in a yard is entered on a page and is not printed on a sheet this district makes.

About four people in that yard said out loud, in the ordinary way and among themselves, that a district which has spent seven volumes arguing about what it may write down has just written down a rule with nobody's name on it. About four other people in that yard said that a rule with nobody's name on it is a thing that binds nobody, which is what a clerk said in this yard in the eighth month, and that the two of those are the same sentence and one of them is about a sheet and the other is about a yard.

---

At about ten past twelve the man of about nineteen asked the man who mends fencing one question, in a yard, in front of whoever was standing there, and the count of what he asked came to forty-two.

"**Then it cannot be me, and I want to know that now and not in a week, because I have been standing at this table for a month counting what everybody says and I thought that was a thing I could be.**"

The mender answered him in about four seconds, in the ordinary way, and nobody counted it and nobody has claimed it since.

"**Because you are the one who catches the mistake. You cannot be the one who is checked, or you are checking yourself, and a man checking himself has found nothing all the time.**"

The boy put his own page face down on the end of that trestle table at about ten past twelve and put the flat of his hand on the back of it and did not turn it over again until about four in the afternoon, and when he turned it over there was nothing on the back of it, and he did not write anything on it and did not say why.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man of about nineteen was told in that yard that he is not the second reader and with a reason, and that he was not asked for it and had not refused it and cannot be counted either way, and that the record about the not asking says not asked, and that a page with nothing on the back of it is a page and is not a record of hearers and is not a list of anybody.

---

The afternoon did not improve on the morning and nobody in that yard tried to make it. At about half past three two people said out loud that a rule gets said in a yard about once and then there is nothing to do until somebody needs one, and the mender said that was the correct length of life for a rule he had said out loud and he was not going to argue with two people about it.

At about four the boy turned his page over, looked at the blank side of it for about two minutes, and then said one thing out loud at the end of that trestle table to nobody in particular, and everybody within earshot heard it and two of them agreed with it and nobody argued.

"**I have been counting things in this yard for a month and I thought I was getting better at it. I am not going to be the one who checks anybody, and I would rather know that today with my hand on this page than find it out in a month when I have said yes to something.**"

The clerk of nineteen years wrote nothing on her own page about that and a clerk did not enter it and the boy did not ask her to, and he said out loud afterwards that it was his sentence and it was going on his page or it was going nowhere, and three people in that yard said that a sentence on a boy's own page is not a record of anything and he said that it is a page and that is all it is.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, carried them up the two goes herself, and on the way up she read the one line in that lot book standing up, the way anybody can, and she said nothing to anybody and nobody in that yard said one word to her about the rule or about the boy or about anything else.

---

Nobody sat in either chair at any point in that day, and that was said out loud twice, and neither time by anybody who had been asked. About four people in that yard said that a rule about a second person is not a thing you can watch happen, and about four of them said that a man who has said a thing out loud in a yard has not done anything yet and knows it, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said that both of those were true and that he would not mind either of them being said about him for the rest of the month.

At about half past three the man the figure of twenty-one years is against asked one question out loud, which was how anybody is supposed to know whether the second person got it right, and two people in that yard said that the second person is the one who knows, and a man who knows whether he got it right is a man doing a check and is not a man being one.

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a road came up that lane at about eleven and again at about four with his boots black to the ankle, read the four figures off the boards both times and got all four, and nobody in that yard asked him about the eleven miles on either occasion and the page in her own hand says not asked. The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up for anybody who will use them had his hand-cart against the east wall with the tent still rolled on the back of it, and it has not gone up on any day since the thirtieth of the sixth month, and he was given nothing and nobody asked him about the tent, and three people in that yard said that a man who cannot use either hand is the obvious one to ask and he said that a rule is not a pair of hands and that nobody had asked him for either.

The man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart was out on that road and did not come up that lane today. That is four days in this week he has not come up it.

Along that lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the eighty-first night of that run and eighty-one days in this district, and there was nothing in his hands, and about four people in that yard said good afternoon to him as they came down the lane and three of them said good afternoon to each other instead, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about five the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud at the end of that trestle table to nobody in particular, and the man of about nineteen counted it and got seventy-three, and everybody in that yard heard it.

"**I have said it and I cannot make anybody hold it and that is not a failure this morning, it is the price, and the day somebody finds a way to make somebody hold it then it stops being that and starts being a post, and I would rather have nothing that binds on two days in seven than a thing that binds every day and has got one man's name on it.**"

Nobody in that yard took the second chair. The bid stood at forty days open and was not run, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty and the clerk of nineteen years did not put a rule into it, and the boy said out loud that a rule is not a name and a name is not a figure and he was not going to write either one of them down tonight.
