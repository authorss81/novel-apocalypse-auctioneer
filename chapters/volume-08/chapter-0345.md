# Chapter 345: Sixty-One Days

---

The fourth of the ninth month came in with the light coming off the low ground earlier than it had any business coming, and by ten the yard of Lot Seventeen had about nineteen people in it and every one of them standing in the wrong place for the shade. The three figures were on the end of the trestle table. The man of fifty-six read the bottom one of the three out loud over the mender's shoulder and got the other two as well. Along the lane the man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of the low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on the forty-fifth night of his run and forty-four days in this district.

"**Two hundred and eighty-three days that nobody has entered anything, and two hundred and forty-four days from the second of January, and the pool of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given is a hundred and sixty-three days, and the first day of the eighth month is thirty-three days past this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and has not moved, and that its own age as a figure about the figure is ninety-four days, and that a figure about a figure is not a day-count for a sheet and is not a promise about a later day.

---

By about ten the man of about nineteen had put his flat stone down at the foot of that trestle table to keep the day off the wall, and about nine people in that yard had already asked him twice what he was going to count today, and he told both of them that he did not know yet. A man who counts what gets said out loud in a yard in daylight is nobody's clerk, and a question asked him about what he is going to count is not a question he has been asked to do anything, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him to count anything for them.

The mender had yesterday's page on the end of that table with a stone on the corner of it, because the wind on that lane takes a page off a flat table. He had the cloth still on his left hand and took the stone off with the other one, set yesterday's page down, and did not read it out again.

At about half past ten the man the figure of twenty-one years is against got to the end of that table with nothing in either hand and said the thing he had come to that yard to say, and it took two hundred and seventeen words out of him, in the ordinary voice, in front of about nineteen people.

"**There is a figure on that gatepost and it is four hundred and eleven, and it was arrived at by counting the people who answered a door, and I am not afraid of that figure. I have never been afraid of that figure and I do not think anybody in this yard is. What I am afraid of is sixty-one days. That is the interval between the day that sheet was found on that post, which was the first of the sixth month, and the day that sheet names, which is the first of the eighth. Thirty days to the end of the sixth and the thirty-one of the seventh. Sixty-one days is a figure about how often a body four hundred miles off that has never once shown its face in this basin decides to print, and a figure about how often a body prints is a figure about a habit and not about the body, and I am afraid of a habit four hundred miles off because I am the only person in this district who knows what a count of answerers is and what a list of hearers is and what the difference between those two things costs, and I am the only person in this yard who cannot learn it back from anybody.**"

The yard was quiet for about four seconds after that. About nine people looked at the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, and then at the sheet on that gatepost four hundred yards up the lane, and then at the ground.

A man who has said out loud in a yard that he is afraid of a figure has not refused and cannot be counted either way. A figure about how often a body is a habit is not a figure about a body and is not a promise about a later day, and the age of the figure on that post and the sixty-one are two figures and are not added together.

Then he said the other one, and he said it while there were still about nineteen people standing there, which was the only reason he said it at all. He had walked up the lane that morning with it in his mouth like a stone he had been carrying in a pocket, and the reason he was saying it now, ten days after a bid was opened on nothing, was that if he ever said it in a room it would be a document, and a document could be read back from the top by somebody, and there is no person in this district he would be willing to hand that to. So it had to go into a yard, in daylight, where it could not be taken down and could not be filed, and where the only record of it would be a boy of about nineteen with a flat stone and a very good memory.

"**And I am going to say the other thing now while there are about nineteen people standing here, and then I am not going to say it again. I have stood in this yard since the first of the sixth month and I have never given anybody one word to call me by, and about nineteen people have watched me not do it every morning of that time, and there is not one of you who could tell a stranger what to call me. That is not an oversight and it is not a mystery and nobody in this yard is going to find out today. I am not starting tonight. If a stranger walks up to that table in a year and asks who is in charge of it, the answer is going to be that there is nobody in charge of it, and that answer is true, and I am the one who will have said so. Write that down if you want it. I am not going to say it a second time.**"

That took one hundred and seventy-six words out of him and it went in the minute in his own words.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a name is not a figure, and that a man with no name in this district is not a person anybody has asked for one, and that no column was ruled and no page was marked and nothing was entered anywhere in this district this morning about a name, and that she may enter that a man said he has not got a word for anybody to call him by and may not enter what it is.

Nobody in that yard asked him anything about it. About nine of them looked away. Along the lane the man of about sixty-four did not turn round.

What actually happened in that yard in the minute after he finished was that about four people opened their mouths. The man of fifty-six got as far as a consonant and looked at the boards of the trestle table instead. The woman of fifty-eight, who was at the trough at that moment with a full bucket in each hand, looked up at the man the figure of twenty-one years is against for about two seconds, and then looked at the water, and carried on up the bank. Nobody asked him. That is a thing that happened in nineteen people all at once and not one of them did it, and it took a man of about nineteen with a flat stone three days to work out that he had a figure for it, and he had nowhere to enter it, and he did not say so in that yard.

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against heard all four of those mouths open and close. He had spent a fortnight building the sentence and he had not known, until the sentence was out of him, that the whole of it rested on nobody in that yard being willing to be the first one to put a name in the air. He went about nine feet up the lane after that and stood against the bank out of the wind for a while, and then came back and did not go up the bank to the room and did not knock.

---

At about eleven the wind came up the lane and the west leg of that trestle table lifted about an inch off the dirt and came down again, and then it lifted and stayed up. The man the figure of twenty-one years is against got his right hand on it and pushed it down, and it went up again under his hand. That hand has not closed properly since the tenth of the eighth month and it could not get round that leg an inch, and he tried it twice, and the table came up both times.

A clerk of nineteen years, who was about nine feet off with a page under her arm, came over and put her own hand flat on the top of that table on the other side and held it down while about nine people got their boots under it and wedged it. Neither she nor he said one word to the other while that was happening. He took his hand off about a minute after the leg stopped moving and put it in his coat.

A man whose hand cannot close on a table leg is not a figure about a table and is not a post and has not been given one, and a person who has put a hand on a table to hold it down has not taken on anything and has not been asked to, and the record about the not asking says not asked, and nobody in that yard asked either of them anything.

---

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud at about two, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people, and the minute took one hundred and eighty-eight words of it, in his own words.

"**A man of about sixty-four came into that gate on the morning of the twenty-first of the seventh month and has been in this district forty-four days and has slept on that low wall on forty-three of the forty-four nights of that run, and I have just been asked for that figure twice this morning by people who are not writing it down. He has nothing in his hands. He lost the ear on the left side in the sound of that bell and did not get it back, and he told about nineteen people about it in this yard eleven minutes after it stopped and named the day he did it on. A figure of nights and a figure of days are two figures about a man and they are not added together and the man is not a figure about either of them, and I am not going to be one of the men in this yard who starts finding jobs for a man of sixty-four because a bell went round twice. I have been in this basin long enough to know what that looks like.**"

Saying out loud in a yard that he is not going to find a job for another man has not refused the other man anything and cannot be counted either way. A man whose trade is over is not a failure and is not a tragic figure and is not redeemed, and a clerk may not say that about him and may not say the other thing either, and nobody in that yard said one word to him about what he was going to do with his hands.

---

The man of about thirty-four who keeps a road came up that lane at about two and went into that yard and stood at the end of that table for about a quarter of an hour and then went out again, and nobody asked him one question about eleven miles. At the foot of the east wall the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up for anybody who will use them had his hand-cart against the brick and the tent still rolled on the back of it, and it has not gone up on any day since the thirtieth of the sixth month, and about nine people in that yard looked at the cart and looked away again.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds out of that ditch was at the top of it at about four with a bundle on his shoulder and a hook in his hand, and the thing he said in about four seconds on the eleventh of the eighth month is not being said again in that yard and has not been asked for again, and nobody in that yard has asked him to repeat it.

The woman of fifty-eight went down that bank with two buckets at about half past five and came back up it at about six with two full ones, and stopped at the foot of it for about four minutes with nobody there, and she was not asked about anything that afternoon. Four minutes is a figure about four minutes and is not a figure about a woman.

The light was off that yard by about half past eight. The bid was not called again and the end of that trestle table had nothing on it for the third day running, and the number on the sheet at that gatepost was still four hundred and eleven.

**A MAN SAID OUT LOUD IN THAT YARD THAT HE WAS AFRAID OF SIXTY-ONE DAYS, AND IN THE SAME YARD HE SAID HE HAD NEVER GIVEN ANYBODY ONE WORD TO CALL HIM BY AND WAS NOT GOING TO START, AND NOBODY ASKED HIM FOR ONE. NOBODY HAS PUT A LINE ON A BOARD ON ANY DAY SINCE THE FIRST OF THE FOURTH MONTH, AND A STICK OF CHALK AGAINST A WALL IS NOT A LINE ON A BOARD.**
