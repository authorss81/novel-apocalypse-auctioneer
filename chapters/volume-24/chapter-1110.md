# Chapter 1110: The Three Hundred And Ninety-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Quarter Of An Inch Of Grit

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and nobody in this basin has ever done one thing with it. It is eight hundred and forty-nine days old.

That board carries one thousand and eight marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and twenty-four days and no wagon has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and thirty-eight days. Nine hundred and ninety-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run, so it is not a lot and not open this morning. Seven hundred and thirteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and eighteen days is how long that rule said out loud in there has stood. Seven hundred and eighty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and that body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and twenty-seven days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and ninety-one days back. Six hundred and sixty-seven marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and fifty-three marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing in it that will carry that. Those four documents have never been in this district at any point in its life. Three of those lines are readable in four places here and not in a fifth place in it. No end is set on any of the four conditions in this district. Fourteen is what this basin has made, and nothing has been made in it since. Five of those fourteen never came in here at all, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page took no line this morning and nothing at all went onto it, and it is at sixty-five lines. The first asking made in this district is not written on that page and it is not written anywhere else either. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and nothing written on that page before the two hundred and eighth morning was ever a line for one, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and twenty-fourth mornings and the near rail on the near side of it three hundred and seventy-fourth mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and ninety-fourth mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and ninety-third morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and forty-ninth night of that run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and forty-eight of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and thirty-fifth of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and fiftieth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the two hundred and eighty-third time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

There has never been anything written at the back of that lot book and it lies open along those boards with the back of it as bare as it has always been. Five lines are ruled across a page of that book, and a column stands on the page after it with a name in it under a heading which still says whoever. The second space on that form is ruled and empty, and nobody in this district has ever been asked to put anything in it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages, in the hands of the people who brought them, and nothing came down that lane today. That record has stood three hundred and nine days. About nine people were on that ground at ten to eight and that lot has been public since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and thirty-ninth afternoon running and one word was all there was in it. No person in this district has ever had either of those two given to them, and nobody here has ever been up to either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and none of it is left. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district, and nobody has ever heard one of them read in it.

---

He came down that slope at about a quarter to eight with the cloth over his arm and the light just off the top of that bank.

What he wanted off that morning was his own table sitting square by about ten so that he could work the length of it, and his table up that bank with the first load.

---

He put it up the way he puts it up, and then he put the back of his heel under the near end of it.

The table did not come up.

That is the thing about it. There is nothing loose under that near end to lift against. The frame of that table is sound and the top of it is twisted, and what is under the foot of that near trestle is not loose stuff at all but grit out of his own bin packed hard into a hollow under the foot of it, and the near end of that table has been standing on it a quarter of an inch lower than the other end for long enough that the dust has settled into the shape of it.

He knows what it is. He has known what it is for about as long as that table has stood on it.

Nobody in that yard saw him know it.

---

He got his hand flat down beside that foot of the trestle and brought out what was loose at the front of it, which was about as much as a thumbnail of it, and it came out as a smear and not as grit.

Then he put that smear back where he got it from.

The near end of that table went on standing a quarter of an inch low on that hollow for the whole of the rest of that morning, and that table was out of level from about ten until the light went off those boards.

---

"That corner is lower this morning than it was yesterday," said the man of about thirty-two, who had the far end of that table and a wedge in his hand. "It is lower than it was yesterday."

"I know what is under it," said the man of about thirty-four.

"Then get it out from under it."

"It is not under it. It is packed into the hollow at the front of that foot and there is nothing to get at it from where I am stood."

The man of about thirty-two looked at the near end of that table for a while and then went back to his own end of it with his wedge and did not say one word more about it.

Nobody asked him anything about it.

---

He squared the top with the back of his heel anyway, and then he worked the far half of that table, which is the half that is over sound ground, and he got a good deal done on it.

The foot and about four inches of sound pallet lay where it had lain all that morning, on the boards at the far end of his own table with the square cut end pointing at the near edge, and he did not go near it.

---

Nothing was turning on that frame at the near end of that first table. The turner was at it from about six and a half and kept his heel off it, and nobody in that yard started it.

At ten to eight the boy was at those boards and said thirty, and nothing came down that lane behind it.

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight was at about four feet off the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand until about four, and nobody spoke to him there.

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table, and she said nothing out loud that day.

His number went out into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one, in the voice the man of about forty-eight keeps for it, and about four voices in that yard took it up out of step with one another.

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and four figures came off the top of it and the flat answer went to that stone after them, and it was the two hundred and eighty-third time running, and nobody in this district has ever asked him about any part of it.

---

The clerk came down those boards at about four with the pen in the holder and read that morning out loud, and then she wrote the three hundred and ninety-fourth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and she took no line for it.

She has read a morning number out loud four hundred mornings and more, and this one was the last of ten she has had to put through that band of thin paper, and she read it the same as the rest of them, and nobody on those boards looked up at it.

---

At about half past four the light went off those boards and that day's boards came down off the cart face down and went up that bank on about nine pairs of shoulders, and about nineteen people went up with them.

He was the last of those men off that ground and the near end of his own table was still a quarter of an inch low on that hollow, and that table was still out of level.

---

When the covers came over it that page stood at sixty-five lines and it did not carry a sixty-sixth. Nothing came down that lane today, and it is thirty pages behind it. Three of the first thirty-three lines came through this district and none of them has been read. That fifth of those five is unpaid at five. Seven months is counted here and nothing in this district holds that. Four hundred and eleven will be on that gatepost sheet in the morning and no more than that.

He took the grit out of that hollow with the flat of his hand and put it back where he got it, and that table was out of level for the rest of that morning.
