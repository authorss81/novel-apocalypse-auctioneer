# Chapter 1136: The Four Hundred And Twentieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Boy Stood On That Near Rail And Found A Hollow Worn In The Top Of Its Last Two Feet

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and no one in this basin has ever done anything with that sheet. That figure is eight hundred and seventy-five days old.

That board carries one thousand and thirty-four marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and fifty days and no wagon has come off it in that whole time. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and sixty-four days. One thousand and twenty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was not run, and it is not a lot here and is not open here this morning. This yard stands seven hundred and thirty-nine days behind the figure on the second line of that book. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood seven hundred and forty-four days. It has been eight hundred and fourteen days since the first day of the eighth month, and that body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and fifty-three days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is nine hundred and seventeen days back. Six hundred and ninety-three marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and seventy-nine marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and nothing in this basin is built to take that count. Those four documents have never been in this district at all. Those three lines are readable in four places here and not in a fifth place in this basin. Four conditions are set out here, and not one of them carries an end of any kind. Fourteen is the whole of what this basin has made and nothing else has ever been made in it. Five of those fourteen have never been brought to this district, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page is at sixty-six lines and it took no line onto it at any hour of that morning. No paper in this district carries the first asking of anybody, that page least of all. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and no line ever written on that page before the two hundred and eighth morning was a line for one, so it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and fifty mornings. That near rail on the near side of it has stood there four hundred mornings, and nobody in that yard looked up at it and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and twenty mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the four hundred and nineteenth morning, and it is neither that second table nor that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and seventy-fifth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and seventy-four of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and sixty-first of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and seventy-sixth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the three hundred and ninth time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

That lot book is open along those boards with the back of it bare. One page of that book has five lines ruled across it, and the column on the page after that one holds one name in it under a heading which still says whoever. There is a second space ruled on that form and it stands empty. There are thirty pages behind that lane and every one of them is in the hands of the people who brought them, and nothing came down that lane today. That record has stood three hundred and thirty-five days. About nine people were on that ground at ten to eight and that lot has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones that afternoon for the three hundred and sixty-fifth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of those two, and nobody has ever gone up over the far end of either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank.

That toll came to ninety-eight and is paid and spent and none of it is left here. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page three came through this district and no voice in this district has read one of them aloud.

---

He was at that end of that stack at ten to eight with his hands in his pockets, which is where he has said that number every morning he has stood there.

What he wanted off that morning was that stack squared off against those boards before the clerk's hand went anywhere near that book, and a straight run of light on them while he did it.

---

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "And there is not one thing behind that this morning and I am not saying a figure after it."

He said that out loud as well, which he does.

There is an order in his head for the way those four voices come out after that number at half past one, and nobody in this district has ever asked him for it, and nobody has ever looked to see whether he has it right.

---

At about half past nine he came down that slope with his bag against his foot and the whole of that day in front of him, and he crossed that ground the way everybody crosses that ground.

He put his right foot on that near rail to step over into the width of that second table.

The rail took his weight and it did not feel like a rail under his heel.

---

He stopped.

He put his weight off it and came back to it and stood looking down at the last two feet of it from about a foot above, and he did not put his hand on it yet.

The rail is straight as a length of iron for about thirty feet of it and then, over the last two feet of it, it is out of straight by about the thickness of a coin, and there is a hollow worn in the top of it in that last two feet, and the hollow is about the width of two fingers and it is smooth inside the way that a thing is smooth that a heel has been going into for a long while.

Nobody in this district has ever said one word about that rail.

---

He put two fingers in that hollow and moved them along it, and there was no grit in it and no rust in it and nothing in it at all, and the wood under it was sound and warm with the sun that was already on it.

He took his fingers out and looked back along the rail from where it comes off the boards to where it stops, and he could see the whole thirty feet and the last two feet both at once, and it was half past nine and the cart was not in yet and nobody else was near that end of the boards.

---

He went and did the stack.

He squared it off with the side of his boot and got his back to it, and he kept his weight off the near rail for the rest of that day because the hollow in the top of it is where his heel had gone wrong once, and he did not tell anybody that he had found it and nobody in that yard asked him anything about it at any point of that day.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came out and stood at about four feet off the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand until about four, and not one person in that yard said anything to him in any of it.

From about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale had her own pan up about four feet off the near end of that second table with her basket down beside her, and she said nothing out loud in that morning.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight had his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for that and for nothing else, and about four voices in that yard took it up behind him out of step with one another, and nobody in that yard put one word out loud about any of it.

That frame stood at the near end of that first table with nothing turning on it and the turner was at it from about six and a half with his heel off it, and nobody in that yard ever started it.

The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall all morning and nobody asked him anything.

At about four the hand went up on that wall and four figures came off the top of it one after another and the flat answer went to that stone after them, and it was the three hundred and ninth time running for it.

The clerk read that morning out loud at about four with the pen in the holder and wrote the four hundred and twentieth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

There is about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert at the end of it and nobody in this basin has ever been given that far end either.

---

About half past four the light came off those boards and that day's boards came off that cart with their faces to the ground and went up that bank on about nine pairs of shoulders, and about nineteen people went up beside them.

He went up with the near end of that stack on the boards, the way he goes up, and he stepped over that rail both times instead of putting his heel on it.

---

That page stood at sixty-six lines when the covers came over it and it took no line onto it, and it carried no sixty-seventh. Nothing came down that lane and it stands at thirty pages behind it as it has stood through that whole run. Those three came through this district and no voice in here has read one of them out loud. That fifth of that five is five and it is unpaid. Seven months is counted in this district and no thing in this basin will take that count. That gatepost sheet will be carrying that same four hundred and eleven in the morning and the morning after that.

That near rail is out of straight over its last two feet by about the thickness of a coin, with a hollow worn in the top of it about the width of two fingers, and nobody in that yard said one word about it.
