# Chapter 1143: The Four Hundred And Twenty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And He Cut The Shoulders Off That Batten And Took One Shaving Off It In One Piece

Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet at that gatepost, and nobody in this district has ever done a thing with it. That figure is eight hundred and eighty-two days old.

That board carries one thousand and forty-one marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and fifty-seven days, and no wagon has come off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and seventy-one days. One thousand and thirty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run. It did not come to this ground as a lot, and it is not open here this morning. Seven hundred and forty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard is. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood seven hundred and fifty-one days. Eight hundred and twenty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and that body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and sixty days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is nine hundred and twenty-four days back. Seven hundred marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and eighty-six marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing in this basin it is sitting on. Those four documents have never been in this district. Three of those lines can be read at four places in this basin, and at no fifth place anywhere in it. Four conditions are set out in this district, and not one end has ever been put in writing to any of them. Fourteen things is the whole of what this basin has made, and there has never been a fifteenth of anything made in it. Five of those fourteen never came as far as this district, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page took nothing onto it at any hour of that morning, and it stands at sixty-seven lines, where it stood when that morning began, and there is no total of those lines anywhere. No first asking has ever been on that page, or on any other paper in this district. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and nothing that was written on that page before the two hundred and eighth morning was a line for one, so it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and fifty-seven mornings. That near rail on the near side of it has stood there four hundred and seven mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank four hundred and twenty-seven mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the four hundred and twenty-sixth morning, and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and eighty-second night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and eighty-one of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and sixty-eighth of those mornings, and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and eighty-third morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the three hundred and sixteenth time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

That book has lain open along those boards with nothing at all on the back of it. Five lines are ruled across one page of it. The column on the page after that one holds one name in it under a heading which still says whoever, and a second space is ruled on that form and it has stood empty every morning of this run. Behind that lane there are thirty pages, and not one of them is on this ground, because the people who brought them down are holding them, and that lane came down empty today. That record has stood three hundred and forty-two days. About nine people stood on that ground at ten to eight, and it has been a public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones this afternoon for the three hundred and seventy-second afternoon running, and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Nobody here has ever been given either of those two distances, and nobody in this district has ever been up over the far end of either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank.

That toll came to ninety-eight and there is none of it left in this district. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three came through this district, and nobody in it has ever heard one of them read.

---

He came down that slope at about half past seven with his bag against his foot and that whole day in front of him, and he crossed that ground the way everybody crosses that ground.

What he wanted off that morning was that top of his own squared with the back of his heel, a batten fitted to the back of it, and the shavings off those boards before the sun comes round off that bank.

---

His own table stands at the far end of that ground, and it is his own, and there is about a foot of clear ground on the near side of his own frame.

He put his own top across his own two trestles and squared it with the back of his heel, and then he went back to the bag for the batten and his plane and his two wedges.

---

The batten was an inch and a half off square and about the length of his own two trestle feet. He cut one shoulder off it and then the other one, and off that second shoulder the whole shaving came away in one piece, thin as a leaf and longer than the batten.

He looked at it a while, and then he threw it in with the rest of that morning's offcuts.

The wedges went in at the near trestle foot and at the far one. The felt was in the bag where it belongs and not on that top, and the cloth went along his own top across the grain, because that is the only way that cloth goes along anything.

---

At ten to eight the boy was up at those boards and gave that number out loud in the way he gives it. There was nothing at all behind the number that morning, and not one page came down that lane.

About half past nine the man of thirty-eight came out to about four feet off the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand, and he was at about those four feet until about four, and nobody in that yard said a word to him.

About half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale had her own pan up at the near end of that second table with her basket down beside her, and she said nothing out loud that day.

The woman of about fifty-two came down that bank with her hands empty at about half past ten and stood in about four feet of sun with them behind her, and nobody said good morning to her.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight had his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it and nothing else, and about four voices in that yard had it behind him out of step with one another, and nobody in that yard put one word out loud about any of it.

That frame stood at the near end of that first table with nothing turning on it all that day, and the turner was at it from about six and a half with his heel off it. The turner said nothing out loud at any hour of that morning.

The man of about sixty-four stood at the foot of that low wall, and nobody asked him anything.

---

At about ten he was at his own two trestles at the far end of that ground when the man of about thirty-four came along the far half of that first table and stopped at his own near corner.

"Your wedges are in the wrong trestle."

"They are holding."

"They are holding because that trestle foot has gone down into its own bed since the spring, and it will go down another half inch by the winter."

"Then I will take them out in the winter."

He went along his own top with the cloth after that, and not one word of it was about anything else on that ground.

---

At about two that cart came through the gap and the near wheel went down into the same place it always goes down at the second bend. Those boards came off it face down and about nine pairs of shoulders took them up that bank at about half past four, with about nineteen people going up beside them.

---

At about four a hand went up on that low wall, and four figures came off the top of it one after another, and the flat answer went to that stone after them. It was the three hundred and sixteenth time running for it, and nobody in this district has ever put one question to him about any part of it.

The clerk read that morning out loud at about four with the pen still in the holder and wrote the four hundred and twenty-seventh morning under the last line in her own small careful hand. She took no line for it.

There is about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert at the end of it, and that far end has never been given to anybody in this basin.

The light came off those boards at about half past four, and that ground was empty after that.

---

That page stood at sixty-seven lines when the covers came over it and carried no sixty-eighth on it. That lane brought nothing down it today and it stands at thirty pages behind it, as it has stood for that whole of it. Three of them came through this district, and not one has been read out loud in it. That fifth of that five is five and it is unpaid. Seven months is counted in this district and this basin has nothing that will carry it off. That gatepost sheet will go on carrying the same four hundred and eleven in the morning and in the morning after that.

He fitted his own batten to his own top and squared it with the back of his heel, and not one person in that yard said one word to him about any part of it.
