# Chapter 1102: The Three Hundred And Eighty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Wheel With Nothing Turning On It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and this basin has never put it to a use of any kind. It is eight hundred and forty-one days old.

That board carries one thousand marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and sixteen days and no wagon has come off it in any of them. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and thirty days. Nine hundred and ninety-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and was not run, and it is not a lot and not open this morning. Seven hundred and five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood seven hundred and ten days. Seven hundred and eighty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and nineteen days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and eighty-three days back. Six hundred and fifty-nine marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and forty-five marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months a clerk has counted, and there is nothing in this district it sits on. Those four documents have never been got inside this district. Three lines are readable in four places here and there is no fifth place in it with any of them on. Four conditions have no end set on any of them. Fourteen things is what this basin has made and there has never been anything else made in it. Five of those fourteen never came, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page is at sixty-five lines and it took no line on it this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it. On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and nothing on that page before the two hundred and eighth morning could have been a line for one, so it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in this district for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and sixteen mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and sixty-six mornings, and the man who fitted that rail is not to be thanked. Three hundred and eighty-six mornings is how long a man has stood at that foot of that bank on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood there for the three hundred and eighty-fifth morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and forty-first night of his run, having slept on seven hundred and forty of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twenty-seventh of those mornings and had all four of them. That is the two hundred and forty-second morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the two hundred and seventy-fifth time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

Nothing is at the back of that lot book and nothing ever has been, and it lies open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the page after that holds a single name under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form, and it stands empty, and nobody in this district has ever been asked to put anything in it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down it this morning. That record has stood three hundred and one days. About nine people were stood on that ground at ten to eight, and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and thirty-first afternoon running, and one word was all that afternoon had in it. Neither of those two has ever been handed to anybody in this district and nobody here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight has been paid and spent and nothing of it is left. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and not one of them has ever been read out in it.

---

He was at that frame at about six and a half and the stove had been in a good while.

What he wanted off that morning was the whole of the day's load off that cart and up that bank while the light was still on those eleven feet of ground, and the near wheel kept off the place at the second bend where yesterday's boards had been lying.

---

Nothing was turning on that frame. He had not put his heel on it and he was not going to, and nobody in that yard had come over and started it, and the casting was standing there in the cold with the belt sitting in the groove between the two marks he had put on it himself.

He put his hand flat on the casting the way he does, to feel whether there is any warmth left in it.

There is not, and there has not been for days, and his hand came away the same temperature it went on, and he wiped it down the front of his coat instead of using the rag because the rag is dirty and the coat is not.

---

At about seven he came along that ground and said the thing out loud to nobody in particular, the way he says things.

"That load is two days in one and whoever is standing on the ground at the soft place is going to find out about it about ankle deep. It is not soft anywhere else. It is soft in one place and it is that same place it was soft at in the spring, and there is no way to take a load over it and not go through it, and that is the whole of what I know about it."

Nobody put a question to him about that or about anything else.

He put his hands in his pockets and stood at that frame and looked at the face of that bank for a while without saying anything at all.

---

About a quarter past ten he came along the boards a second time and said the other thing.

"The boards go up while the light is on them and not one minute after that. Half past four is the hour and it has been the hour for thirty-one years and it is not going to be a different hour this morning, and if anybody is standing at the top of that bank waiting on me they can stand there until the light is off the boards, because I am not carrying them up in the dark."

Then he went back to that frame and stood with his hands in his pockets again.

---

About a quarter to ten the man of about thirty-two and the man of about thirty-four were at the far end of that first table with their cloths out and a short length of batten between them, and the man of about thirty-eight was at about four feet off the near end of that table with his cloth in his hand, and none of the three said one word to the other two.

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table, and she said nothing out loud after her own business that day.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air, and about four voices in that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.

---

At about ten he got to the front of that one sentence of his.

He has got to the front of it most mornings for a long time now and he has never once got past the front of it, and he has never told anybody in this yard that he gets that far, and he stood there this morning with the casting cold under his hand and the words of it as far as they ever get.

Then he let it go where it always goes back to and went and looked at the ground at the second bend instead.

---

At about four a hand went up on that low wall and four figures came off the top of it, one after another, and the hand lay flat on the stone under the lowest one of them.

"The four, one more time today," said the man of fifty-six, "and I have had you both times already."

Then he said his other thing to the stone, and it was the two hundred and seventy-fifth time running, and nobody in this district has ever asked him about any part of it.

---

The clerk came down those boards at about four with the pen still in the holder and read that morning out loud before her hand went near any part of it, and then she wrote the three hundred and eighty-sixth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

---

About half past four the light left those boards and the whole of that stack came down off that cart face down and went up that bank, the ninth space and all, and about nineteen people went up with it.

He stood at that frame and watched the last of them go, and the belt was sitting in the middle of the groove between the two marks, and nothing was turning on it, and he had not put his heel on it once in that whole day.

---

That page stood at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and did not take another. That lane brought nothing down it and it is at thirty pages behind it. Not one of those three has ever been read out in this district. That fifth of the five is five and is unpaid. That count of seven months is on nothing whatever. Four hundred and eleven will be on that gatepost sheet in the morning.

He said two things out loud in that yard today, and both of them were about the boards, and neither of them was about that frame.
