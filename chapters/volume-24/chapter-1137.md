# Chapter 1137: The Four Hundred And Twenty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And That Table Stood Level From About Ten Until About Four

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and nothing has ever been done with that figure or with that sheet. That figure is eight hundred and seventy-six days old.

That board carries one thousand and thirty-five marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and fifty-one days without a wagon ever coming off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and sixty-five days. One thousand and twenty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and was not run, and it is not a lot on this ground and it is not open here this morning. This yard is seven hundred and forty days behind the figure on the second line of that book. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood seven hundred and forty-five days. It has been eight hundred and fifteen days since the first day of the eighth month, and that body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and fifty-four days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is nine hundred and eighteen days back. Six hundred and ninety-four marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and eighty marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing in this basin that will take it. Those four documents have never been in this district at all. There are three of those lines that can be read in four places here, and in no fifth place in this basin. Four conditions are set out in this district and no end has ever been put on any of them here. Fourteen things is the whole of what this basin has made and nothing else has ever been made in it. Five of those fourteen are not in this district and never have been, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page is at sixty-six lines and nothing went onto it at any point of that morning. There is no first asking on that page and no first asking on any other paper here either. There was a reason said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and nothing that was written on that page before the two hundred and eighth morning was a line for one, so it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and fifty-one mornings. That near rail on the near side of it has stood there four hundred and one mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and twenty-one mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the four hundred and twentieth morning, and it is neither that second table nor that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and seventy-sixth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and seventy-five of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and sixty-second of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and seventy-seventh morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the three hundred and tenth time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

That lot book lies open along those boards with the back of it bare. Across the top of one page of that book five lines are ruled, and the column on the page after that one holds one name in it under a heading which still says whoever. That form carries a second ruled space and it is empty. Behind that lane there are thirty pages, and every one of them is in the hands of the people who brought it down, and nothing came down that lane today. That record has stood three hundred and thirty-six days. There were about nine people on that ground at ten to eight, and that lot has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones that afternoon for the three hundred and sixty-sixth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Nobody here has ever been given either of those two, and nobody has ever been up over the far end of either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank.

That toll came to ninety-eight and has been paid and spent. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three came through this district and nobody in it has read one of them out loud.

---

He came down that slope at about twenty past seven with the cloth over his arm, and the light was lying along the whole length of that ground while the near end of his own table was still in the shadow off that bank.

What he wanted off that morning was that top along with the cloth from the far end to the near end of it, all in one morning, with the sun still off the near end of it while he was on it.

---

He put his table up the way he puts it up and squared that top with the back of his heel.

The near end of that top stood a quarter of an inch low on the hollow of grit at the foot of the near trestle, and the top of it is twisted and not dipping, and he put his hand flat on that near corner and pushed on it once and it is not loose and it is not square.

The foot and about four inches of sound pallet was lying out on those boards at the far end of that table, square cut end toward the near edge, in the way of anybody coming the length of that yard.

---

He got the cloth and started at the far end of that top and came back toward the middle, and the cloth went along that half of it without the board dipping anywhere under his hand.

At about a quarter to ten he stopped where he always stops, which is two feet short of the near end of that top, and he stood there with the cloth in his hand and the sun coming up off that bank behind him.

---

At about ten he went to the far end of his own table and picked that piece up where it had lain since the light came.

He carried it the length of that table and set that square cut end down into the near corner of his own top, into the corner where the two boards of that frame come together, and he leaned on the end of it with the heel of his hand until it was all the way in.

It went in the whole way.

The near corner of that top came up off that hollow of grit and that table stood level on its two trestles, and the daylight that had been standing under that corner all morning was not standing there any more.

---

Not one person in that yard said one word about it, and nobody asked him why, and nobody asked him what the piece was for, and nobody thanked him for it either.

He took his hand off the piece and stood a while looking down the length of that table.

There was no light standing under any part of that top. It is twisted and it is not dipping, and it is a table, and there is nothing in it but the top.

Then he picked the cloth up off his arm and started at the far end again and came all the way back, and he did the near two feet as well, and the cloth went along them without the board going down anywhere under his hand.

---

At ten to eight the boy had said thirty at those boards and said no figure after it out loud.

About half past nine the man of thirty-eight came out to his four feet off the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand, and he was there until about four, and no one in that yard said a word to him.

---

From about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale had her own pan up about four feet off the near end of that second table with her basket down beside her, and she said nothing out loud at any hour of that morning.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for that alone, and about four voices in that yard had it behind him out of step with one another, and nobody in that yard put one word out loud about any of it.

That frame stood at the near end of that first table with nothing turning on it all day and the turner was at it from about six and a half with his heel off it, and nobody in that yard started it.

The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and he was asked nothing.

---

At about two he moved his bin out from under the trestle at the far end because the shade had come round far enough to make him look at the far half of that top twice, and he put his hand flat on that top twice at about three to see whether it was still where he had left it.

It was.

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and four figures came off the top of it one after another and the flat answer went to that stone after them, and it was the three hundred and tenth time running for it.

---

At about four he took that piece out of that corner.

It came out of that corner as easy as it had gone in. The near end of that top went down onto that hollow the moment it was gone, and the daylight came back under that corner about the width of a finger, and the twist came back into the top of it with it.

He laid that piece out on the boards at the far end of his own table with the square cut end toward the near edge, and he got his cloth and folded it over his arm.

---

The clerk read that morning out loud at about four with the pen in the holder and then wrote the four hundred and twenty-first morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

There is about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert at the end of it, and nobody has ever been given the far end of that in this basin.

About half past four the light came off those boards and that day's boards came off that cart face down and went up that bank on about nine pairs of shoulders, and about nineteen people went up with them.

He went up with the last of them that day, and the near end of his own table stood a quarter of an inch low on that hollow when he got back to it, and there was no light anywhere under the middle of it and none under the far end either.

---

That page stood at sixty-six lines when the covers came over it, took no line onto it, and carried no sixty-seventh. Nothing came down that lane and it is at thirty pages behind it as it has stood through that run. Those three came through this district and no voice in it has read one of them out loud. That fifth of that five is five and it is unpaid. Seven months is counted in this district and nothing at all in this basin will take it. That gatepost sheet will go on carrying four hundred and eleven in the morning and the morning after that.

That table sat level from about ten until about four, and he got the near two feet of that top along with the cloth while it was, and then he put that piece back on the boards at the far end of his own table, and nobody in that yard said one word about it at any point of that day.
