# Chapter 1104: The Three Hundred And Eighty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Piece Against That Corner

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it has been on that sheet through everything this basin has done with itself. It is eight hundred and forty-three days old.

That board carries one thousand and two marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and eighteen days and no wagon has come off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and thirty-two days. Nine hundred and ninety-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot. Seven hundred and seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and twelve days is how long that rule said out loud in there has stood. Seven hundred and eighty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and that body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and twenty-one days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and eighty-five days back. Six hundred and sixty-one marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and forty-seven marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing here that it sits on. Four of those documents have never come into this district at all. Three lines are readable in four places here and there is no fifth place in it with any of them. Four conditions are set out in this district with no end upon any one of them. Fourteen is what this basin has made and nothing else has been made in it. Five of those fourteen never came here and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page took no line this morning and it stands at sixty-five lines. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and nothing written on that page before the two hundred and eighth morning was ever a line for one, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in this yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and eighteen mornings and the near rail on the near side of it three hundred and sixty-eight mornings, and the man who fitted that rail is not to be thanked. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and eighty-eight mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and eighty-seventh morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and forty-third night of that run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and forty-two of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twenty-ninth of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and forty-fourth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the two hundred and seventy-seventh time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

Nothing is at the back of that lot book and it lies open along those boards with nothing in it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after that holds one name under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form and it stands empty and nobody has ever been asked to fill it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down it today. That record has stood three hundred and three days. About nine people were stood on that ground at ten to eight and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and thirty-third afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Neither has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody here has ever been up to either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight, it has been paid and it is spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and nobody in it has ever read one of them out.

---

The light was over the top of that bank before he got his own table up, and he had the frame across the two trestles by about a quarter to eight.

What he wanted off that morning was to find out how much gap there was at the near corner of his own top, and to be up that bank with the first load.

---

The top is twisted and not dipping and the near end of it stands on grit that came out of his own bin, so the near corner of that table is a quarter of an inch nearer the ground than the corner behind it, and a man who works a table for years can see that in his sleep.

He squared the top with the back of his heel. He went along it with his cloth. He put his hand flat on the near corner and pushed down on it the way you push to find out whether a thing is loose, and it is not loose, and it is not square either.

---

He got the piece off the boards.

He has carried it a long time and it is a foot and about four inches of sound pallet with a square cut end, and the square end is the end he cut and the other end is the foot of it.

He put the square cut end against the near corner of his own top, right down into the corner where the two boards of that frame come together, and pushed.

It went in about half an inch and then it stopped.

That is a thing about a corner and not about a board. He knows what stopped it and it was the same thing that has been stopping him at that corner every morning he has come to it.

He took it away again and looked at the square end of it for a second, and then he laid it back down on the boards at the far end of that table with the square cut end pointing at the near edge, where anybody walking past can see it.

---

He got on with the cloth.

"You have got the daylight under that table about the width of a finger at the near end," said the man of about thirty-two, who had the far end of it and a wedge in his hand. "If you take that corner down a bit more you will be working all morning on a table that is going somewhere."

"I know what it is doing," said the man of about thirty-four.

"Put a wedge under it and be finished."

"The wedge goes in the frame and the frame is sound. It is not the frame."

Then the man of about thirty-two got on with his own end of that table and neither of them said one word about that corner again.

Nobody asked him what the piece was for. Nobody asked him why he had it out, or whether he was going to do anything with it, or whether it fitted anything. Nobody thanked him for it.

---

The frame stood at the near end of that first table with nothing turning on it, and the turner was at it from about six and a half and did not put his heel on it, and nobody in that yard started it.

At ten to eight the boy was at those boards and said the lane count before the clerk's hand had moved anywhere, and it was thirty, and nothing came down that lane behind it.

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came out and stood at about four feet off the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand, and nobody spoke to him there for the whole of that day.

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table and said nothing out loud that day.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four voices took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.

---

At about a quarter to eight the hand went up on that low wall and four figures came off the top of it, and at about four it went up again and four more came off the same four.

"The four of you, and I do not need anybody to tell me what a corner is for," said the man of fifty-six out loud to that stone. "I have had you twice today and got you twice and that is the whole of my trade."

Then the other thing, which was the two hundred and seventy-seventh time running, and nobody in this district has ever put one question to him about it.

---

The clerk came down that row of boards at about four with the pen in the holder and read that morning out loud, and then she wrote the three hundred and eighty-eighth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

---

About half past four the light came off those boards and the day's boards came down off that cart face down and went up that bank on about nine pairs of shoulders, and about nineteen people went up with them.

He was at the far end of his own table when the last of them went, and the foot and about four inches of sound pallet was lying on the boards at the far end of it with the square cut end pointing at the near edge of that table.

---

That page stood at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it took no line on it today. Nothing came down that lane and it is at thirty pages behind it. Those three have gone through this district unread. That fifth of the five is five and is unpaid. Seven months is counted in this district and nothing in it holds that. Four hundred and eleven will be on that gatepost sheet in the morning.

He put that piece against that corner this morning and it went in about half an inch, and he took it away again and laid it back on the boards, and nobody in that yard asked him what it was for.
