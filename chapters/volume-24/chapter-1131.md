# Chapter 1131: The Four Hundred And Fifteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And He Worked With His Knee In Four Inches

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and nothing in this basin has ever been done with that sheet. That figure is eight hundred and seventy days old.

That board carries one thousand and twenty-nine marks. The train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and forty-five days and no wagon has come off it in any of them. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and fifty-nine days. One thousand and twenty days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, was not run, and it is not a lot on this ground this morning. This yard stands seven hundred and thirty-four days behind the figure on the second line of that book. The rule that was said out loud in that yard has stood seven hundred and thirty-nine days. The first day of the eighth month was eight hundred and nine days ago. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and forty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is nine hundred and twelve days back. Six hundred and eighty-eight marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and seventy-four marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and no thing in this basin will take that count. Those four documents have never been in this district at all. Three of those lines are readable in four places here and there is no fifth place in this basin that has any of them. Four conditions are written out in this district, and no end has ever been put on any one of them. Fourteen things is the whole of the making in this basin and there has never been a fifteenth made in it. Five of those fourteen never came to this district at all, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page stands at sixty-six lines and nothing went onto it at any point of that morning. The first asking of anybody is not on that page and it is not on any other paper in this district. On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and nothing written on that page before the two hundred and eighth morning has ever been a line for one, so it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and forty-five mornings. That near rail on the near side of it has stood there three hundred and ninety-five mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and fifteen mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the four hundred and fourteenth morning, and it is neither that second table nor that near rail. On the seven hundred and seventieth night of that run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and sixty-nine of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and fifty-sixth of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and seventy-first morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the three hundred and fourth time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

That lot book is open along those boards and the back of it is bare. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after that one holds one name in it under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form and it is empty. There are thirty pages behind that lane, every one of them in the hands of the people who brought it, and nothing came down that lane today. That record has stood three hundred and thirty days. About nine people were on that ground at ten to eight, and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones this afternoon for the three hundred and sixtieth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of those two, and nobody here has ever gone up over the far end of either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank.

That toll came to ninety-eight, it was paid, and it is spent. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three came through this district and no voice in this district has read one of them out loud.

---

He came down that slope at about a quarter to eight with the straight edge under his arm and the wedge in the pocket of his coat, and the light was on the whole of that ground except the last four feet of it.

What he wanted off that morning was his own top squared and the cloth along it before the sun came round off that bank and put his eye in at the near end of the last four feet.

---

He put his own top across the two trestles the way he puts it up and squared it with the back of his heel, and there was about a foot of clear ground on the near side of that frame.

There was about a foot and no more, because the near rail comes along that side of that ground from about the middle of it to the boards, and a man who wants a knee at the near end of his own top has to put that knee in what is left.

Four inches is enough if a man is careful with the cloth and careful with his weight.

---

At ten to eight the boy was stood at those boards with his hands in his pockets, which is where he says that number.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "There is nothing behind that this morning and I am not saying one word after it."

He said out loud that he was not saying a word after it, which is what he does most mornings.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came out and stopped at about four feet off the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand, and he was at those four feet until about four, and not one person in that yard said one thing to him in any of it.

He has never been asked in this district what those four feet are for, and there is a man who stands in them every morning of that run and has never been asked.

From about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale had her own pan up about four feet off the near end of that second table with her basket down beside her, and she said nothing out loud that morning, and nobody in this district has ever asked her what she weighs things for.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight had his own number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for that alone, and about four voices in that yard took it up behind him out of step with one another, and nobody in that yard put one word out loud about any of it.

That frame stood at the near end of that first table with nothing turning on it and the turner was at it from about six and a half with his heel somewhere else all morning, and nobody in that yard started it.

---

At about half past nine he and the man of about thirty-four had the far edge of that first table between them and they said six things about it.

"That corner wants the cloth across the grain and not with it."

"With it takes the dirt off and puts it back in the same place."

"Then it wants the block and not your thumb."

"That felt in the bag is for the second board and not the first."

"The near wheel of that cart drops at the second bend and it drops on the same side every time it comes in."

"You can wedge that trestle or you can stand on it. I would wedge it."

Not one word of that was about the piece of sound pallet lying on the boards at the far end of that other man's own table, and neither man looked at it while the other had his hands full.

---

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and four figures came off the top of it one after another, and the flat answer went to that stone after them, and it was the three hundred and fourth time running for it.

The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall through the whole of that, and nobody said one word to him, and nobody has ever said one word to him.

At about four the clerk came down that row of boards with the pen in the holder and read that morning out loud, and then she wrote the four hundred and fifteenth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

There is about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert at the end of it, and nobody in this basin has ever been given the far end of that.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four, that day's boards came off that cart face down, and they went up that bank on about nine pairs of shoulders with about nineteen people going up beside them.

He carried the last four feet of his own top up on his shoulder and did not stop at the far end of that other man's table on his way past, and that piece was lying on the boards there with the square cut end pointing at the near edge of it, where anybody walking the length of that yard has to pass it.

He said nothing about it and nobody said nothing to him about it, and nobody has asked him anything about it in this district.

---

That page stood at sixty-six lines when the covers came over it and it took no line on it and it carried no sixty-seventh. Nothing came down that lane and the thirty pages behind it are the same thirty that were behind it yesterday. Three of those lines came through this district and no voice in here has read one of them aloud. That fifth of that five is five and it is unpaid. Seven months is counted in this district and nothing in this basin is built to hold that count. That gatepost sheet will be carrying the same four hundred and eleven in the morning and the morning after that.

He worked that near end of his own top with his knee in the four inches between that rail and that trestle leg, and he squared that top with the back of his heel before he picked his trestles up, and he has never once said one word to anybody about the width he had to work in.
