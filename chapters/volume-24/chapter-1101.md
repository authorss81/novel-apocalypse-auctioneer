# Chapter 1101: The Three Hundred And Eighty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Piece Of Sound Pallet In The Open

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and nothing has ever been done with it in this basin. That figure is eight hundred and forty days old.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety-nine marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and fifteen days and no wagon has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and twenty-nine days. Nine hundred and ninety days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, it was not run, and it is not a lot. This yard is seven hundred and four days behind the figure on the second line of that book. That rule said out loud in here has stood seven hundred and nine days. It is seven hundred and seventy-nine days since the first day of the eighth month, and that body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and eighteen days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and eighty-two days back. Six hundred and fifty-eight marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and forty-four marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and nothing in it holds that. Four documents have never come into this district at any time. Three lines can be read in four places in this basin and in no fifth place in it. Four conditions are set out in this district and none of them has an end put on it. Fourteen is what this basin has made, and it has made nothing else since. Five of those fourteen never reached here, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

That page took no line this morning and it stands at sixty-five lines, and the earliest asking of anybody's is not written on that page, nor on any other paper in this district, and there is nobody here who can produce it. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and that page was not able to take a line for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning came, so that nothing written on it earlier than that was ever a line for one. It is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in this yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and fifteen mornings and that near rail on the near side of it three hundred and sixty-five mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and eighty-five mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and eighty-fourth morning, and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and fortieth night of that run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and thirty-nine of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twenty-sixth of those mornings, and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and forty-first morning running with nobody having asked him anything. It was the two hundred and seventy-fourth time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

That lot book lies open along those boards with the back of it bare, as it has always been. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the page after that holds one name under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form and it stands empty. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in the hands of the people who brought them, and it brought nothing down it this morning. That record has stood three hundred days. About nine people were on that ground at ten to eight, and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones this afternoon for the three hundred and thirtieth afternoon running, and one word was the whole of it. Neither has ever been given to anybody in this district: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight and it has been paid and spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district, and nobody in it has ever read one of them out.

---

He came down that slope at about a quarter to eight with the light just off the top of the bank and the whole length of that ground still lying in it.

What he wanted off that morning was his own top squared and the cloth along it before the man of about thirty-two came to that end of the table, and his own table carried up the bank with the first load and not with the last.

---

He put it up the way he puts it up. Two trestles, the frame across them, the top onto the frame, and then the back of his heel against the far edge to bring it round.

The top is twisted and it does not dip. The near end of that table stands a quarter of an inch on grit that came out of his own bin and not on sound wood, and that is why the far end rides when a weight goes on the near end, and it has done that long enough that he does not put a weight on the near end.

He squared it anyway, because the back of his heel is what he has for that and there is nothing else in that yard to square a top with.

---

In the middle of that table there is a foot and about four inches of sound pallet with a square cut end. The square end points at the near edge of that table and the foot of it lies on the boards at the far end of it, where anybody walking past can see it.

He has carried that piece a long time. It came off a pallet with the rubbish end broken away from it, and what is left is square at one end and sound the whole length of it, and it is the shape of the thing a man needs when a corner is out of square.

The near corner of his own top is out of square. He can see it from where he stands with his cloth over his arm.

He went along the top of that table with the cloth instead, from the far end toward the near, and he did not put his hand out for anything.

---

"You have got a cloth and a piece of pallet and about two hours of light on that ground," said the man of about thirty-two, coming along with a short length of batten under his arm. "And that corner is the one thing on that table you have not laid a hand on."

"I can see the corner," said the man of about thirty-four.

"I know you can see it. I am saying it is still there and it is still out."

Then the man of about thirty-two went and set his own frame up at the far end of that ground, which is what he does every morning and has done for a good many years, and neither of them said one word about that corner again for the rest of that day.

---

The frame stands at the near end of that first table with nothing turning on it. The turner was at it at about six and a half. He did not put his heel on it this morning and nobody in that yard started it.

At ten to eight the boy was at those boards with his hands in his pockets and the light lying along the front of that stack.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "There is nothing behind that number this morning and I have stopped waiting for there to be."

Nobody asked him where the number came from, and nobody asked him what the other part of it was, and he put the stub of pencil behind his ear and went back down to the foot of that stack.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came out and stopped at about four feet off the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand.

Those four feet are the four feet where a man can work without being spoken to, and that is the whole of what they are for. Nobody said one word to him at any point of that day, and he did not look round at anybody, and he got on with what he was doing.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table with her basket down beside it and the bar wanting the wind.

She let it want it until it stopped wanting it, and then she did the first thing of that day on that pan and said nothing out loud after it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight had his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices in that yard took it up behind him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.

Not one word about it was said out loud in that yard.

---

At about a quarter to eight a hand had gone up on that low wall and taken four figures off the top of it one after another, and at about four the hand went up again and took the same four off it.

"The four of you," said the man of fifty-six out loud to that stone. "And I will be back for the second lot."

Then the other thing he says to it.

"Two hundred and seventy-four mornings," he said. "I have had the two of you twice today and got them both times, and the light came off those boards at half past four and went down the face of that bank while my hand was on this stone, and I have not put one question to one person in this yard today and I am not going to, and this stone gets what I say and nothing that comes off anybody else."

That was the two hundred and seventy-fourth time running, and nobody in this district has ever asked him a question about any part of it.

---

The clerk came down that row of boards at about four with the pen in the holder and read that morning out loud in the way she reads it, and about four people on those boards stood still through the whole of it with a foot on a board and did not move until she had finished.

Then she wrote the three hundred and eighty-fifth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

---

About half past four the light came off those boards and the day's boards came down off that cart face down and went up that bank on about nine pairs of shoulders, and about nineteen people went up with them.

He was the last of those men to come away off that ground. The foot and about four inches of sound pallet was still lying in the middle of his own table with the square cut end pointing at the near edge, and the foot of it was still on the boards at the far end of it, and the near end of that table was still a quarter of an inch down on grit out of his own bin.

The frame stood at the near end of the first table with the turner at it and nothing turning on it.

---

That page was at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it did not carry a sixty-sixth. Nothing came down that lane today and it is at thirty pages behind it. Those three have gone through this district without one of them being read. The fifth of that five stands at five and is unpaid. Seven months is counted here and nothing in this district carries it. That gatepost sheet will still be carrying four hundred and eleven in the morning.

The piece was on the boards at the far end of his own table when he went, and nobody in that yard had said one word to him about it.
