# Chapter 408: What a Stranger Cannot Tell

The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was standing at the end of that trestle table at the distance a stranger stands at, with his back to the east wall and his hand-cart behind him, and he had been standing there about a minute before he said anything at all.

It was the sixth of the eleventh month and the light was thin and there was no wind in the yard. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen, and about four of them noticed where he was standing, and about four of them had noticed it on the twenty-fifth of the tenth month and had not noticed it on the other eleven days since.

The man of fifty-six read the four figures out loud at about half past nine and got all of them, and said the word after each of them, and got the word four times, and nobody standing at that wall read the word with him.

"**The board carries three hundred and sixteen, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and thirty-two days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and forty-six, and three hundred and seven days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the age of the figure about that figure is a hundred and fifty-seven days, and that the figure is not a figure about a man and is not a figure about a door and is not a figure about anybody who came down that lane.

---

He stood at the stranger's distance for about a quarter of an hour and then said one thing out loud, in the ordinary voice, in front of about nineteen people, and the man of about nineteen counted it and got one hundred and sixteen and put it down.

"**A man walks up to that table from that gate and he sees a stone and two lines in a book and a page with nine columns on it and a word in the margin, and he cannot tell which of the figures in that book is current, and now he cannot tell what the word is for either, because it is not over a column and there is no column it is over, and it is in a clerk's hand and it is the only thing on that page he could check and he has got no way of knowing that, and a thing being clear is not the same as a thing being right.**"

"That is three things now," the mender said from the end of that table. "On the twenty-fifth of the tenth it was one. You have added two in twelve days and the first one is still there."

"I have added two and I have not taken one away and that is the whole of my report."

Then somebody at the near end of that table said the ordinary thing, which is that there are three things that can be done with a book a stranger may walk up to and read, and the man who puts tables up nodded, and about four other people nodded with him, and he opened his mouth to say the third one and did not.

The yard waited. Nobody finished it for him. About nine people stood there at the end of that trestle table and looked at a man who had stopped in the middle of a thing he had said on the twenty-fifth of the tenth month, and the clerk of nineteen years wrote nothing on her page, and the man of about nineteen counted nothing, and the silence went on for about eleven seconds, which is longer than any silence anybody in the yard has sat in since before this district began keeping figures.

"You have said the first two before," somebody said at last, kindly.

"I have."

"And you are not going to say the third one."

"No," the man who puts tables up said, "and it is not because I have forgotten it, and about four of you know what it is, and I am not going to have it said out loud in this yard this afternoon by anybody including me, because the last time it was said out loud in this yard the man who said it said in the same breath that he had not said it, and that is how a thing becomes a habit instead of a finding."

He put his forearm on the table where it had been for six months, and about four people said that the stone was on the corner it had been on since he moved it an inch on the twenty-fifth of the tenth month and put it back, and he said the stone was where he had put it and he was not going to touch it again this month.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down to that table with his right hand in his coat and stood about nine feet off it, which is the same gesture in a different direction, and about four people in the yard said so out loud, and he said he knew.

Then the man who puts tables up asked him a question, which is the first question anybody in the yard has asked him since the second of the tenth month, and about four people looked up when it was asked.

"You have read those two lines out loud more times than anybody in this district," the man who puts tables up said. "How many figures are on the end of that table that a man standing where I am standing could not tell the truth of?"

The man in the coat looked at the stone, and at the page, and at the boards behind him, and stood there for a while.

"**Two, and neither of them is a figure of a lot.**"

"The ditch is a figure of a lot."

"The ditch is a figure of a lot that stopped being true and nobody wrote down when. And the word is not a figure of a lot at all, it is a figure of how the other eleven got here, and a man standing where you are standing cannot tell which of the two he is looking at."

That is two figures and neither of them is a person. The man of about nineteen wrote them down on his own page, both, under a line, and about four people said that a count of what a stranger cannot tell is a count of the stranger and not of the figures, and the boy said it was a count of both. The two of them were the same figure standing in two places, and nobody worked out how.

At about two he went back to the east wall and got his hand-cart and did not put the tent up and did not take the table down, and the tent is still rolled on the back of that hand-cart where it has been since the thirtieth of the June, and he was given nothing and nobody asked him about it.

Then a woman at the near end of that table said that a man standing at a gate who cannot tell what a figure in a book means is not going to be a man who bids on anything in it, and that the fifth of the five things this district does not have is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and that a man who cannot tell what a lot is worth cannot be paid for holding it either.

"Nobody is going to pay anybody for anything in that book," the mender said, "and that is not because the figure is out of date. That is the fifth of the five and it has not been paid since before this district began keeping figures, and a figure that is out of date and a district that cannot pay anybody are two different holes and this yard has both of them and is not going to be given a sixth by anybody this afternoon."

At about half past two the child of about eight came down that lane with a woman he belongs to and stopped at the gap in the low wall, and put his hand through it, and stood there for about a minute, and nobody in the yard asked him one thing. He was not asked whether anything works or does not work, he was not asked about the water, and he was not asked about a book on a table. When he had been standing there long enough the woman he belongs with said his name once, and he took his hand out of the gap and went on up the lane with her.

Nobody in the yard knows whether that wall keeps anything off the weather. Nobody has written it down. Nobody is going to write it down in this month either, and a document signed anyway is what a convening is.

At the far end of that yard, which is the only part of it that is dry, the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, and has said of himself that he is fifty-one, stood from about eleven until about two with a flat book under his left arm, and the woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale stood a few feet off him with her arms folded, and neither of them said one word to the other or to anybody else. His six households have not moved for a hundred and ninety-eight days and there is a word in the margin of a page under a stone on that figure, and he was not asked about it, and about four people said out loud that they were not going to ask him about it, and he heard them say so.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a lot book this district makes is not a document it does not own, that there are three of those and no fourth, and that a stone on a book is not a figure and carries no job. The figure on the second line of that lot book has been out of date for twenty-one days and was not altered and has nothing written beside it and is not going to get anything written beside it. The bid has stood open sixty-six days and was not run today. The first day of the eighth month went past ninety-six days ago. The ninth of the nine printed nights is a hundred and ninety-nine days back and no bell was pulled today. The rule said out loud in the yard on the tenth of the tenth month is twenty-six days standing, and the number of days it has been kept is a figure about sittings. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and was empty at about six, and the fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid and the count is still five.

Between about two and the light going, that yard did what it does at that hour, and the wall got its two readings and the man of about sixty-four got asked about neither of them. The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in that ditch before nine and came up out of it about ten and read the four figures at the end of that table, and that arm of his did not go above the level of that shoulder at any hour of that day, and nobody said one word to him about it and he said nothing. The road keeper came up that lane twice and was right both times and was not asked about the eleven miles. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of the low wall from about ten with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and he was not asked to sit in the second chair and nobody proposed it and nobody sent him on nine days.

At about half past five she came down that bank with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, carried them up the two goes, and read the two lines standing up on her way past, the way anybody can, and there was not one word out of her and not one word to her.

By the time the light had gone the stone was square on that book at the corner nearest the boards, and the tent was still rolled on the back of a hand-cart against the east wall, and about four people said good evening to each other on their way out and about four of them said good evening to nobody in particular.

Nothing in the yard was better than it was at ten o'clock in the morning and nothing in it was worse, and about four people said that out loud at the end of the low wall, and that one of them said it was the first time anybody had said a thing like that about a day instead of about a week, and nobody gave a reason.

"Twelve days," the mender said, from the end of that table, "and I have said three things in the yard in twelve days and two of them were a man standing at a stranger's distance and one of them was me, and none of the three of them has moved a single figure, and a thing that does not move a figure is a thing a man said."
