# Chapter 397: The Thing on the End of That Table

---

The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up for anybody who will use them got the figure off the top board at about twenty past eight on the twenty-fifth of the tenth month. He was standing at the near end of that trestle table on the side away from the boards, where nobody usually stands, and he got it. Then he stood there and looked at the boards for a while, as though the boards were a thing he had not seen before.

By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen, and two of them were looking at the table from the wrong side.

The man of fifty-six read the four figures out loud at about half past nine and got all four, and the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up read them again at ten and got all four. About four people said out loud that two men have now got the same four numbers off that wall, and a third of them has not looked at the wall at all this morning, and nobody there said who the third one was.

"**The board carries three hundred and five and the train on that siding has stood six hundred and twenty-one days, and one hundred and forty-six days is how long that record has been in force, and the figure on the figure on that gatepost is a hundred and forty-six days old, and those are two figures and not one figure.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the rival record and the age of the figure about that figure are two figures counted off one day and one exclusion, and that they land on one interval, and that that is not a slip and is not a finding.

---

At about half past ten the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up put his forearm on that table, where it had been for six months, and looked down at the end of it.

The stone was on the lot book at the corner nearest the boards. The clerk's page was under the same stone. The boy's page was under that one. There were two chairs at the table, and the man of fifty-six was standing at the near end of it with his hand flat on the boards, and the lot book had two lines in it, and the second of the two said about a foot of standing water in a ditch this morning, and the page had nine columns ruled on it with nothing written in one of them.

He took his forearm off the table and stood back about four feet and looked at all three of those things from where a stranger would be standing. Then he said one thing out loud in the yard, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people heard it, and the man of about nineteen counted it and got eighty-six and put it down in the margin, and read it back to himself without saying it out loud.

"**A man walks up to that table from that gate and he sees a stone and two lines in a book and a page with nine columns on it and nothing in them, and he cannot tell which of the figures in that book is current, and it is not because the book is unclear. It is because the book is clear. That is the whole trouble and it is on the end of that table in the open where anybody can walk up to it.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man looked at the end of that table from about four feet back and said what a person walking up to it can and cannot tell, and that what he cannot tell is which of the figures in that book is current. A lot book is a lot book. It is not a sheet, and not a column of not-askings, and not a column of people, and not a document this district does not own, and not a fifth of the five things this district protects, and not a seventh of the six instruments it has built and not named. A book a stranger may walk up to and read is a document, and a book in a room with a shut door is not one. A thing being clear is not the same as a thing being right.

Then about four people said the ordinary thing, which is that a stranger could go and fetch somebody.

"A stranger four hundred miles off is not going to come here."

"A book a stranger may walk up to is a book for a stranger who is standing in front of it, and not a book for a man on a road."

The mender said out loud that a man who can be fetched is a man in this district, and a man who cannot be fetched is a man four hundred miles off with a sheet nailed to a gate.

"About four of us," somebody said. "He was right."

"And the two of those have never been in the same room," somebody else said, which nobody had thought of until it was said, and about four of them said so too.

A count of the people who answered a door is a count of the answerers and not a count of the people, and the difference between a number made by knocking and a number made by walking is the whole of what a removal is. No figure on that page today and none on those boards was made by knocking, and none was made by walking. A name is not a figure.

---

At about two the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up put his hand on the stone and moved it about an inch, which is the first time anybody has touched that stone since it was put there. He put his hand back on it and pushed it square again, and said one thing out loud, and about four people heard it, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got seventy-one.

"**I have moved that stone an inch to see if it was loose and it was not loose and I have put it back where it was, and I want it said in whatever page you keep that I did that and not a clerk of nineteen years and not a boy of about nineteen, and I am not a keeper of that book and nobody has asked me to be.**"

The stone on that lot book was moved about an inch and put back, and the man who moved it is a man who puts tables up, and not a clerk of a house and not a clerk of nineteen years, and is not a keeper of anything. The two lines in that book were not altered, nothing was written beside them, and no figure was added to that book. A stone on a book is not a figure and is not a mark and carries no job.

At about half past two the man of about nineteen read the five headings on that clerk's page out loud, one after another, standing at the end of that trestle table, and then read the four words over the four columns under them. The man of fifty-six stood about nine feet off with his hand on the boards and listened to all nine of those and did not say one word.

"That is nine things a person can say a word about," somebody said, "and not one of them has a name in it."

About four people in the yard then asked him, in the ordinary way, which of the two lines in that lot book is the current one.

He looked at the stone. Then he looked at the two lines. Then he looked at the four of them.

"I do not know," he said. "And I am not going to guess in front of about nineteen people."

"That is the second time in three days a man in this yard has said he does not know."

"One of them said it about a month. This one said it about a book."

"A man who does not know a thing and says so in a yard is doing the only thing there is," the man of about nineteen said.

"Nobody asked you what to say."

"I was not waiting to be."

Then about four people said out loud that a stone on a book is not a market, and neither is a book on a table, and one of them said that a market is a place where a person is asked a question, and that the last time anybody in this district was asked one at that table was the thirteenth of this month.

"The thirteenth of this month was the day a toll was read out," the mender said.

"That was a reading and not a question."

Nobody there said one word about the difference, and that was true, and the day went on the way days in the yard went.

Then about four people said out loud that a page with nine columns ruled on it and nothing in them is the same as a page with nine columns ruled on it and something in them, and about four other people said that it is not.

"It is not," the mender said. "The difference between them is what somebody chose to put in, and the reason they chose it. One of those two is on a page a clerk keeps in her own coat and the other one is on a page under a stone in the open."

About four people said that a page under a stone in the open with nothing in it is a page a stranger can walk up to and read and get nothing out of, and about four of them said that is not a column of people and is not a record of anybody and is not a fourth of the three documents this district does not own.

Nine columns with nothing in them is a figure about nine columns and is not a figure about nine people and is not a finding about anybody. A page of a clerk and a page under a stone in the open are two pages and are not one page. The whole of what this district has made in six months is the one under the stone, and the clerk of nineteen years is not going to say so in her own hand on a page nobody can walk up to.

Then the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came to the end of that table at about three and read the two lines in that lot book out loud from the top in the ordinary voice, both of them, and put his right hand flat on the boards beside the stone while he read the second one.

It did not close.

About four people in the yard watched his hand on the boards, and none of them said a word about it, and he took it back into his coat when he had finished.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man read the two lines in that lot book out loud in the yard this afternoon and that nobody read them back from the top, and that a thing read back by nobody binds nobody. The second of those two lines is ten days out of date. It was not altered, and it has not been corrected, and it has not been replaced, and it has no second figure beside it, and it is not going to get one from anybody in this yard. A line which has stopped being true is not a wrong line and is not a mistake anybody made.

---

The rest of that day went ordinary. The man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up that bank at about ten with his legs wet to the knee and read the four figures off the boards at the end of that table, and got all four. The road keeper came up that lane twice, once near eleven and once near four, and read the four figures off that wall both times and got all four both times, and nobody there said one word to him about the eleven miles and nobody said one word to him about a stranger.

The reed cutter was in that ditch until the light went, with his hook and his bundle, and came up the bank about ten minutes after everybody else had left the wall. The man who puts tables up was at the east wall with his cart for the rest of the afternoon and was given nothing and nobody asked him about the tent, which is still rolled on the back of that hand-cart and has not gone up on any day since the thirtieth of the June.

That bid has stood open fifty-five days and was not run today. The first of the eighth month went past eighty-five days ago. The reading of that lot is where it was on the twelfth of this month, which is begun and not finished, and it stands at the fourth of the five things a document that sets a lot out has to say, and the fourth is a person, and there is nobody in the yard who has been asked for one. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and was empty at about six, and no name went into it by anybody.

By the time the light had gone the stone was square on the lot book at the corner nearest the boards, and the two lines in it were the two lines that were in it this morning, and a page with nine columns on it was under the same stone with nothing written in one of them. A stranger walking up to that table in the morning would see exactly what about nineteen people saw today, and would be able to read every word of it.
