# Chapter 391: The Length of the Month After This One

---

The man of fifty-six had his palm flat on the boards at the end of that trestle table and had put it there at about half past eight and took it off it about twenty minutes later and put it back, and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten.

The nineteenth of the tenth month came in dry and cold along the top of that bank, and nobody in that yard said one word about a chair. The man of fifty-six read the four figures off those boards out loud over the mender's shoulder the way he has read them over somebody's shoulder every morning for as long as he has been reading them and got all four, and the man of about nineteen wrote all four down and got the same figure off every one of them, and then the man of fifty-six did not go up that lane to the fence end the way he does most mornings and stayed at that table with his hand where it was.

"**Nothing has been entered for three hundred and twenty-nine days, the board carries two hundred and ninety-nine and the train on the siding carries six hundred and fifteen, and the pool of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given is two hundred and nine days, which is seven months back, and the first day of the eighth month is seventy-nine days past.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the bid has stood open forty-nine days and was not run today, and that the figure on the second line of that lot book is four days out of date and was not altered.

---

At about half past ten the man of fifty-six said the thing out loud.

He had not been asked it. Two people in that yard were doing the thing they do, which is to stand at the near end of that table and look at those boards, and about half past ten he lifted his hand off them, turned round to the yard instead of to the boards, and said it out loud in the ordinary voice in front of about nineteen people, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got fifty and wrote it on his own page in his own hand, in ink, in the margin, with the figures he had got that morning underneath it.

"**I have read the figures off those boards every morning for six months and I have got them every morning, and I cannot tell you how many days the month after this one has, and I am not going to stand here and guess at it in front of you.**"

The whole of that yard went quiet in a way it has not been quiet on a morning since before this district began keeping figures, and about four people in it looked at the boards, and the boards had not changed, and about four other people looked at the man of fifty-six, and he had said a thing about himself and not about a figure.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the length of the month after this one is not a figure this district has, and that no figure on any page in that yard this month uses one, and that a month nobody has counted is a month nobody has checked, and that a figure somebody has entered because they had to enter one is not a promise about a later day. She wrote that sentence on the eighteenth of the sixth month of this year and she is writing it again this morning, and that the second time a person writes a sentence is a habit and a habit is a finding and she has entered that too.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came to the end of that table and put his right hand in his coat before he got there, the way he has put it in his coat every morning since the thirteenth of this month, and stood about nine feet off the boards and looked at all four figures without touching anything.

About four people in that yard said out loud that a man who reads the figures should have something on a page somewhere with his name against it, and that there are nine columns on that clerk's page and a page of the boy's own beside it, and that a column of a thing with a word over it is not a column of people, and that one of them could say that this man checks the boards, and about four of them said that is the least anybody could put down, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud that he has been in this yard five months and has watched two people check those boards and neither of them has been asked to and one of them has said out loud this morning that he does not know a thing, and that a thing a man says about himself in a yard is not a thing anybody can write down, and he was right and about four of them said he was right.

Then the man the figure of twenty-one years is against said one thing out loud himself, in the ordinary voice, to nobody in particular, and about nineteen people heard all of it, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got fifty-seven and wrote it on his own page, and then looked at it for a while without saying anything.

"**I am not going to be called anything. I have not been called anything for six months and I am not going to start today, and none of you has asked me for one this morning and I want that in whatever page you are keeping, and I am not going to tell you what to write.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man who has said out loud in a yard that he is not going to be called anything has not refused and cannot be counted either way, and that the record about the not asking says not asked, and that no name went into any of the nine columns on that page and none was going to.

Then about nine people in that yard said out loud, in the ordinary way and all at once, that somebody ought to know the length of that month, and that it is a figure like any other figure, and that there are boards on that wall with days on them going back to the twenty-fourth of December and one of them could have it, and the man of fifty-six said that he had said he was not going to guess and he was not going to guess on a board either, and about four of them said that was not what they meant, and nobody in that yard wrote the length of a month on anything at all for the rest of that day.

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in the bottom of that ditch before nine and came up the bank at about ten and read the four figures standing at the end of that table and got all four, and his right hand did not go above the level of that shoulder at any point in this day. The man of about thirty-four who keeps a road came up that lane at about eleven, said the four figures out loud from the middle of them as he went past without stopping at the table at all, got all four, and went on up, and nobody in that yard asked him about the eleven miles and nobody in that yard said one word to him about a month.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in that ditch until about half past four and came up the bank with his hook in his hand and his bundle under his arm, and the man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his cart, and he was given nothing and nobody asked him about the tent. The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale was at the far end of that yard with her arms folded for about half an hour and nobody asked her a question and she did not ask one and her four empty houses are four figures about four houses and are not a figure about her.

---

At about four somebody in that yard said the ordinary thing, which is that if a man does not know how many days a month has then the figures on those boards are no good, and about four other people in that yard said that the figures on those boards are the figures on those boards and have been right every morning for six months and that a man not knowing a thing about a month is a fact about a man and not about a board, and about four people in that yard then asked the man of fifty-six, in the ordinary way and all at once, what he thought this district should do about a bid that has stood open forty-nine days, and he said one thing out loud and about nine people heard it and nobody counted it.

"**I have read the figures on that board for six months and I do not know how long a month is and I am not going to be told by anybody in this yard, and a bid that has stood open forty-nine days is a thing that has not been run on one of forty-nine days, and that is a figure about a bid and not about anybody standing here.**"

About four people in that yard said that is the second time in two days he has told them what he is not going to be told, and about four of them said that a man who reads figures every morning and does not know what to do with a bid is a man who has run out of one thing and not out of both, and the man of fifty-six said that he had not run out of anything and that he was going to be at that table tomorrow, and about four of them said good, and about four of them said nothing.

It took them the better part of an hour. Four of them stood at the near end of that table counting back the marks off the top board in twos and threes and got to a figure that was nine days out, and one of them counted off the second board and got a figure that was four days out the other way, and one of them said out loud that the boards count days and do not count months and that a month is not on any of them, and about four of them said that a man who keeps figures for six months ought to be able to count a month on a board, and the man of fifty-six said that a board counts days and he has been counting days on it since the twenty-fourth of December and has never once counted a month on it and was not asked to, and a figure that is not on a board has to come off a list, and there is one list in this district and it is not a board and it is not in that yard.

The fifth of the five things this district does not have, which is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, is still not paid, and the count is still five, and no sixth is proposed, and a day's work went into an outlet at the low end of that ditch on the fifteenth of this month and has not been paid for.

That bid stands at forty-nine days and was not run today and nothing has been said about closing it. The first day of the eighth month is seventy-nine days past and a ladder is spent as a ladder and a ladder is a figure about a ladder. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and was empty at about six, and no name went into it by anybody.

A little before that the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up the bank at the end of that ditch with his bundle over his shoulder and the water running off him onto the boards, and he put his right boot on the end of that trestle table to get the last of it off, and about four people in that yard watched him do that and none of them said one word about it, and he went up the lane.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, carried them up the two goes, and said nothing to anybody on her way past that table and nobody in that yard said one word to her.

By the time the light had gone, the man of fifty-six was still at the end of that trestle table with his hand back flat on those boards, and the four figures on them were the same four figures they had been at about half past eight, and the yard was the same yard, and a man in it had said out loud that he did not know a thing, and about nineteen people had heard him say it, and a month nobody has counted is a month nobody has checked.
