# Chapter 391: The Length of the Month After This One

---

Frost still in the shade of the east wall at half past eight and gone off the boards by the time the man of fifty-six got his palm flat on them. He put it there at half past eight. At nine he lifted it off to rub the ball of his thumb where the cold had got into it, and then he put it back, and it was the hand of a man who had nowhere else to put it.

By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen, and the four figures on those boards were the four figures that had been standing on them since before this district began keeping figures.

The nineteenth of the tenth month came in dry and cold along the top of that bank. Nobody there said one word about a chair. The man of fifty-six read the four figures out loud over the mender's shoulder, the way he has read them over somebody's shoulder every morning for six months, and he got all four. The boy of about nineteen wrote all four down and got the same figure off every one of them. Then the man of fifty-six did not go up that lane to the fence end the way he goes most mornings. He stayed at that table with his hand where it was and looked at the marks.

"**Nothing has been entered for three hundred and twenty-nine days, the board carries two hundred and ninety-nine and the train on the siding carries six hundred and fifteen, and the pool of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given is two hundred and nine days, which is seven months back, and the first day of the eighth month is seventy-nine days past.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that the bid has stood open forty-nine days and was not run today, and that the figure on the second line of that lot book is four days out of date and was not altered.

---

At about half past ten the man of fifty-six said the thing out loud.

Nobody had asked him it. Two people in the yard were doing the thing they do, which is to stand at the near end of that table and look at those boards over their own shoulders, and about half past ten he took his hand off them, turned round to the yard instead of to the boards, and said it in the ordinary voice where anybody standing there could hear it.

The boy counted what he said and got fifty, and put it on his own page in his own hand, in ink, in the margin, with the four figures he had got that morning underneath it.

"**I have read the figures off those boards every morning for six months and I have got them every morning, and I cannot tell you how many days the month after this one has, and I am not going to stand here and guess at it in front of you.**"

The whole of that yard went quiet in a way it has not been quiet on a morning since before this district began keeping figures.

Somebody at the back said, to the person beside him and not loudly, that the man does not know.

They looked at the boards. The boards had not changed.

"He said he isn't going to guess," somebody else said. "That's a different thing."

"That is the same thing," said a third, and stopped there, and about four people agreed with him and about four said it was not, and not one of the six offered a reason.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the length of the month after this one is not a figure this district has, and that no figure on any page in the yard this month uses one. A month nobody has counted is a month nobody has checked. A figure somebody has entered because they had to enter one is not a promise about a later day. She had written that sentence on the eighteenth of the sixth month of this year and she was writing it again this morning. The second time a person writes a sentence is a habit, and a habit is a finding, and she entered that as well.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came to the end of that table with his right hand in his coat before he got there, the way he has put it in his coat every morning since the thirteenth of this month, and stood about nine feet off the boards and looked at all four figures without touching anything.

Nine feet was the distance he had settled on a week ago. Six feet was being asked something. Nine feet was near enough to read a page and far enough that a person could say what he liked about it, and he had not told anybody that and was not going to.

The people at the near end of that table were working the boards the way they always did, and one of them had it in his head that a man who reads figures ought to have his name against them on a page somewhere.

"There are nine columns on her page," the first man said. "Nine."

"And a page of the boy's own beside it."

"He checks the boards. Somebody could put that down. That is the least anybody could put down."

"Nobody's asked him."

"Nobody's asked him," the first man agreed, and the two of them stood looking at the boards a while longer without saying anything else, which was normal, and which was the whole trouble and nobody there had the sentence for it yet.

The mender said it out loud, with a reason, and about four people heard all of it. He had been in the yard five months. In that time he had watched two people check those boards and neither of them had been asked to, and one of them had said out loud this morning that he did not know a thing. A thing a man says about himself in a yard is not a thing anybody can write down.

"He was right," somebody said.

"About four of us think so," somebody else said, and about four of them said he was right, and it went no further than that, because arguments in the yard went no further than that.

Then the man the figure of twenty-one years is against said one thing out loud himself, in the ordinary voice, to nobody in particular, and about nineteen people heard all of it. The boy counted what he said and got fifty-seven and put it on his own page, and then looked at what he had put there for a while without saying anything.

"**I am not going to be called anything. I have not been called anything for six months and I am not going to start today, and none of you has asked me for one this morning and I want that in whatever page you are keeping, and I am not going to tell you what to write.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man who has said out loud in a yard that he is not going to be called anything has not refused and cannot be counted either way. The record about the not asking says not asked. No name went into any of the nine columns on that page and none was going to.

Then about nine people said out loud, in the ordinary way and all at once, that somebody ought to know the length of that month. It is a figure like any other figure. There are boards on that wall with days on them going back to the twenty-fourth of December and one of them could have it.

The man of fifty-six said that he had said he was not going to guess and he was not going to guess on a board either.

"That is not what we meant," somebody said.

"No," the man of fifty-six said. "It is not."

Nobody in the yard wrote the length of a month on anything at all for the rest of that day.

---

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in the bottom of that ditch before nine and came up the bank at about ten and read the four figures at the end of that table and got all four. His right hand did not go above the level of that shoulder at any point in this day, and he did not explain that to anybody, and nobody asked him about it.

The road keeper came up that lane at about eleven, said the four figures out loud from the middle of them as he went past without stopping at the table at all, got all four, and went on up. Nobody in the yard asked him about the eleven miles. Nobody said one word to him about a month.

The reed cutter was in that ditch until about half past four and came up the bank with his hook in one hand and his bundle under his arm. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his cart, and he was given nothing and nobody asked him about the tent. The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale stood at the far end of that yard with her arms folded for about half an hour, and nobody asked her a question and she did not ask one, and her four empty houses are four figures about four houses and are not a figure about her.

---

At about four somebody said the ordinary thing, which is that if a man does not know how many days a month has then the figures on those boards are no good.

"That is not what a board is," the man of fifty-six said.

"Nobody said what a board is."

"You did. You said the figures on those boards are no good. That is the first thing anybody has said to me this month about a board, and it is not true." He took his hand up off them and put it back down. "The figures on those boards are the figures on those boards, and they have been right every morning for six months. A man not knowing a thing about a month is a fact about a man. It is not about a board."

Then about four people in the yard asked him, in the ordinary way and all at once, what he thought this district should do about a bid that has stood open forty-nine days. He said one thing out loud. About nine people heard it and nobody counted it.

"**I have read the figures on that board for six months and I do not know how long a month is and I am not going to be told by anybody in this yard, and a bid that has stood open forty-nine days is a thing that has not been run on one of forty-nine days, and that is a figure about a bid and not about anybody standing here.**"

"That is the second time in two days he has told them what he is not going to be told," somebody said, to somebody else.

"A man who reads figures every morning and does not know what to do with a bid is a man who has run out of one thing," somebody else said. "Not out of both."

"I have not run out of anything," the man of fifty-six said. "I will be at this table tomorrow."

About four of them said good, and about four of them said nothing, and both of those were the ordinary ending to it.

It took them the better part of an hour. Four of them stood at the near end of that table counting back the marks off the top board in twos and threes and got to a figure that was nine days out. One of them counted off the second board and got a figure four days out the other way.

"Numbers do not agree," somebody said.

"The boards count days," said the man who had been counting the second one. "They do not count months. A month is not on any of them."

"A man who has kept figures for six months ought to be able to count a month on a board."

"A board counts days. I have been counting days on that wall since the twenty-fourth of December and I have never once counted a month on it and was not asked to." The man of fifty-six turned round from the boards. "A figure that is not on a board has to come off a list, and there is one list in this district, and it is not a board, and it is not in this yard."

The fifth of the five things this district does not have, which is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, is still not paid, and the count is still five, and no sixth has been proposed. A day's work went into an outlet at the low end of that ditch on the fifteenth of this month and has not been paid for.

That bid stands at forty-nine days and was not run today and nothing has been said about closing it. The first day of the eighth month is seventy-nine days past, and a ladder is spent as a ladder, and a ladder is a figure about a ladder. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and was empty at about six, and no name went into it by anybody.

A little before that the reed cutter came up the bank at the end of that ditch with his bundle over his shoulder and the water running off him onto the boards. He put his right boot on the end of that trestle table to get the last of it off. About four people in the yard watched him do it and none of them said one word, and he went up the lane.

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank at about half past five with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, carried them up the two goes, and said nothing to anybody on her way past that table. Nobody there said one word to her.

By the time the light had gone the man of fifty-six was still at the end of that trestle table with his hand back flat on those boards, and the four figures on them were the same four figures they had been at about half past eight. A man there had said out loud that he did not know a thing, and about nineteen people had heard him say it, and the boards had not moved all day. A month nobody has counted is a month nobody has checked.
