# Chapter 394: The Rule, Twelve Days On

---

Hard frost on the boards at first light on the twenty-second of the tenth month, the kind that comes up off the bank and goes off the boards before anybody has washed at the trough. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen. The man of fifty-six read the four figures off those boards out loud over the mender's shoulder and got all four, and the mender read them after him and got all four, and the man of about nineteen put all four down.

"**The board carries three hundred and two, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and eighteen days, the pool of refusals with no reason a clerk of a house has given is two hundred and twelve days, and a table against a wall about four feet high eleven miles off has stood a hundred and eight mornings.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. The rule said out loud in the yard on the tenth of this month has now been standing twelve days, and twelve is a figure about a rule and is not a figure about a lot and is not a figure about any person in the yard. The price of it is that nobody can be made to keep it. She entered that price on the day it was said and has entered nothing this morning that takes it back.

---

At about eleven about four people in the yard asked the mender, in the ordinary way and all at once, to say the thing he said on the tenth of this month again, because about four of them wanted to know whether it had been said properly.

He said it again standing at the end of that trestle table with his left hand in its cloth and his right hand flat on the boards. It took him about nine seconds. It was not the same words.

The man of about nineteen counted what he said and got ninety-five and put it down, and read it back off his own page to nobody, which is a thing he does and which is not a reading.

"**A thing in this district binds when a second person reads it back from the top and gets it right, and it does not bind on a name and it does not bind on a signature and it does not bind on a mark and it does not bind on a person, and a second reader is nobody in particular and is found by whoever is standing there, and the price of it is that nobody can be made to keep it, and I am not going to say that again in a softer voice.**"

Nine seconds later he said one more thing out loud and about four people heard it, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got seventy-two. His left hand stayed in the cloth through both of them, and about four people in the yard could see the shape of a hand in a cloth and could not see the hand.

"**It was not kept on the fourth day after I said it, and the day it was not kept there was a man in that yard reading out loud who was not the man who opened the book, and nobody read any of it back from the top, and a clerk wrote that down, and I have not asked anybody since whether they have kept it and I am not going to.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the rule was said out loud in the yard on the tenth of this month by the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing. A person is named in that entry as having said it, and that is a description and not a name, and no name of his is in any column on any page in this district and none is going to be. The rule was not kept on the day four days after it was said, and a clerk entered that too, and neither half of that is revised.

Then about four people said out loud that the rule has been working.

"We would know whether it had been working," somebody else said, "if anybody had read a thing back from the top since the tenth of this month."

The whole of that yard looked round at itself for about four seconds.

About nine people there had read four figures out loud in the ordinary way that morning, over each other, and had not read them back from the top, and had not claimed to have.

"That is what a yard is," somebody said.

A rule which costs nothing to keep is a rule nobody can be made to keep, and the clerk of nineteen years entered that on the day it was said and is entering it again this morning. A sentence entered twice in a district is a habit, and a habit is a finding, and the finding is about her and not about the rule.

---

At about one the man of about nineteen said one thing out loud at that table, on his own, without being asked and without anybody having asked him anything. About four people heard it, and the man of about thirty-four who keeps a road counted it and got seventy-nine and said so out loud, and about four of them put it down wrong.

"**There is a column on that page with a word over it and the word is the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it has been ruled since the thirtieth of the June, and it has been empty every day since including today, and I have not written a name in it, and nobody has asked me to, and I am not going to, and I am not going to say out loud that anybody should.**"

About four people said out loud that a woman of fifty-eight read a line back from the top on the eleventh of this month, and that her name could go in that column in about four seconds.

"About four seconds," somebody said, and put a hand flat on the page to show how few.

"A person who has said out loud she is not going to do it again has said so to the yard and not to a column."

Nobody in the yard asked the clerk of nineteen years to put a name in it. She was not asked, and she did not rule a new column, and she did not touch the old one.

The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud is ruled and is empty, and nothing went into it. A column of a thing with a word over it is not a column of people, and a person is not a column, and four columns of not-askings is a figure about four columns, and no fifth of that shape was ruled today. A name said out loud in a yard is a consent the person it belongs to did not give, and nobody in the yard asked her about it, and she has not been asked.

Then about four people said out loud, in the ordinary way and all at once, that the reading of that lot ought to be finished. It stands at the fourth of the five things a document that sets a lot out has to say, and the fourth is a person, and there is no person in the yard who has been asked.

"There is one more of those five after the one that is a person," the mender said. "I am not going to say what that one is."

"You could say it."

"No." He had his right hand flat on the boards and he took it off. "A document which names a thing nobody in this district can use has found nobody."

About four of them said he was right, and about four of them said that is the same answer twice in three days.

The reading of that lot is begun and not finished and stands where it stood, and a document which names a remedy nobody can use has found nobody. Nobody in the yard advanced it today, and nobody is going to advance it by standing at a table saying out loud that it ought to be.

---

The rest of that day went ordinary. The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in that ditch before nine, came up out of it about ten, and read the four figures off the boards standing at the end of that table with the water running off him, and got all four. The road keeper came up that lane at about half past three, said the four figures out loud without stopping, got all four, and went on up, and nobody asked him about the eleven miles.

At about half past one the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart went up that lane and did not come into that yard and did not stop at the low wall.

"He is out on that road four days in a week," somebody said.

"Whatever a rule costs, it does not cost a man his week."

"Nobody in this yard has ever asked him what a week is worth."

"Nobody is going to."

A clerk of nineteen years entered nothing about that at all, because nobody asked her a question, and because a week a man with a cart spends on a road is a figure about a man and not a figure about this district.

At about four about four people in the yard began, in the ordinary way, to work out how many days it has been since a woman of fifty-eight read a line back from the top on the eleventh of this month. Two of them got to twelve. One of them got to eleven. They did not get a third figure.

"It is eleven," the man of about nineteen said. "The eleventh to the twenty-second is eleven. Whoever has the other one has counted a day that has not happened."

"A boy who writes figures down for nothing is the last person anybody in this yard should take a count from."

"That is fair," he said. "I would rather be the last person than the only one."

Then the clerk of nineteen years shut her page at about half past four and did not put that count on it, and about four people in the yard asked her why not, and she said one thing back to them in about four seconds, and nobody counted it.

"**That is a figure about how many days ago a person stood in a yard, and it is not a figure about whether she read it right, and if it goes on that page it is going to sit there next to a figure that is out of date and somebody is going to read the two of them as one thing, and I have enough of those.**"

A count of the days since a person read a thing back was arrived at out loud in the yard, and it was not written on the clerk's page, and it is not a finding. A figure about a person is not a figure about a reading, and a page of a clerk is not a board and does not have to carry every figure anybody says in a yard.

The reed cutter was in that ditch with his hook and his bundle until about half past four and came up the bank wet to the chest. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was at the east wall with his cart, the tent still rolled on the back of it, and he was given nothing.

That bid stands at fifty-two days and was not run today. The first day of the eighth month is eighty-two days past. The figure on the second line of that lot book is seven days out of date and has not been altered. The ninth of the nine printed nights is a hundred and eighty-five days back and is open, and it was closed in nothing.

At about half past five the cloth on the mender's left hand came loose at the wrist, where he had been leaning on that table with it. He re-tied it with his teeth and his right hand, which took him about two minutes, and about four people in the yard watched him do that and not one of them said one word about it, and his hands were not touched and nothing was asked about them.

About half past five the road keeper came back down that lane with his shafts over his shoulder and stopped at the end of that trestle table. The boards were still there with the marks on them. The light had gone off them by then, and he got all four by touch as much as by looking.

About four people in the yard saw him do it, and none of them said anything.
