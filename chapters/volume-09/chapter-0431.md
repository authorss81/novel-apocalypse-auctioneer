# Chapter 431: Two Lines And No Third

There was a name in the air in the ground of Lot Seventeen on the twenty-ninth of the eleventh month and there was nothing on the ground in it with that name in it, and some people who had been standing at that trestle table since about nine had worked out between them that a thing can be in a place and not be in a book there.

The light came up hard behind that bank and the frost stayed in the shadow of that ditch all morning. By ten there were about nineteen people in it.

The man of fifty-six put his palm flat on those boards and read the four figures off them in the ordinary voice, and got all four, and said the word after each one and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and thirty-nine, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and fifty-five days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and sixty-nine, and three hundred and thirty days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and eighty days, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a figure about a sheet, and that there is no day-count for the sheet itself, and there never has been one.

---

The boy of about nineteen was at the end of that trestle table at about half past nine with his own page out and his pencil in his hand, and he was not writing on it.

"Two days," he said.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was at the far end of the table with his right hand flat on the boards and his left hand inside a cloth against his chest, and he had been there since before the light came off that bank.

"Two days," said the boy, "and there is a name in this yard and it is not on anything, and there is no rule that says it has to be, and if there is not a rule then nobody is breaking anything, and if nobody is breaking anything then this yard is exactly where it was on the twenty-sixth, and about four of us have spent two days finding that out."

"Put that on your page," somebody said from the middle of the ground.

"I am not going to."

"Why not."

"Because the second half of that is a sentence and the first half of it is a figure, and I have been told by three people this month not to put a sentence and a figure in the same line." The boy did not look up. "I have also been told by three people this month that a page is a page and not a column and not a job, and I would like it noticed that those two things have never once been the same instruction and I have had to obey both of them for about a fortnight."

The clerk of nineteen years said one thing out loud at about ten without looking up from her own page, and a few people heard it and the rest of that ground did not, and the boy of about nineteen counted what she said and got seventy-nine and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**He is right and neither of those two things is mine to rule on. I have nine columns on this page and none of them is for what a person says in a yard, and I have been asked to rule a tenth four times this month and I have said no four times, and a clerk who says no four times has a habit and a habit is a finding about the clerk and not about the columns.**"

Nobody wrote that down. She entered that a man asked a question there and was answered and not answered in the same breath, and entered no figure, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

At about half past ten the man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up out of that ditch with the water to his thigh and got the four figures at that end of the table and said the word with them four times. That right arm of his did not go above the level of that shoulder at any point in that morning, and two people noticed it and neither of them said a word.

He said one thing up that bank at about eleven and it was not addressed to anybody.

"Two days ago somebody said a name in that ground and one of you put a hand over your mouth about a second after it, and I have been in that ditch for both days and I have watched four of you do a thing that all four of you think nobody saw." He did not stop work. "I am not going to say the name and I am not going to say whether it was mine. I am saying that the hand went up and came down again in about a second and that a hand going up and coming down is not on anybody's page either, and that about four of you have got a second thing in the open out here and no rule about it at all."

Nobody said that out loud. The clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud there and entered the thing, and entered that the thing was not a name and not a figure, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about a quarter to eleven the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down that bank and took up the nine feet he always took off that table with his right hand in his coat. He had said nothing out loud since about one on the twenty-seventh, and about nine people had looked at him four or five times each to see whether he was going to start, and he was not going to start for another half hour.

---

The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up came off that east wall at about ten and put his hand-cart down against the low wall and came and stood at the end of that table, and about nine people were within four feet of him.

"You have got nothing in your hands," said the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing.

"I have got nothing in my hands."

"I have said a thing to a man two days running and both of those things were about what he was going to do, and I am not going to say it a third time."

"I know what you are not going to say and I have been waiting two days to find out whether you were going to notice that I have nothing in my hands." The man who puts tables up turned his two hands over at that table, palm up, and about nine people looked at them. "That is the whole of what I have got. I have carried a thing in my mouth for twenty-four days and it is not on this ground in any form, and I would like somebody here to tell me what a man is supposed to do with his hands when there is nothing coming."

Nobody told him. The clerk of nineteen years entered that a man held his hands out at that table, and entered that a man said he had nothing in them, and entered no figure, and two people said afterwards that it was the first time anybody had asked that question and the first time that ground had heard that there was one.

She entered one more thing on that page, and it was in her own hand lower down and away from the other entries, and a few people at that end of the table leaned over to see whether she was going to stop.

She did not stop. She entered that a figure somebody has entered because they had to enter one is not a promise about a later day, that she wrote that on the eighteenth of the sixth month of this year, and that she is writing it again, and that the second time is a habit and a habit is a finding.

---

At about twelve the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up that bank with the water coming off his coat and stood at the end of that table for about four seconds. Four people were within four feet of him and nobody said a word to him and nobody asked him for anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked. He was in that ditch on the fifteenth of the tenth month until about half past four and came up the bank on his hands and his knees, and nobody there has asked him about that morning, and nobody asked him this morning either, and some people at that table wanted to and did not.

At about one the road keeper came up that lane, got the four figures off that wall as he went past, and was not asked about the eleven miles. At about half past one the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up that lane and went on up it and did not stop, and the near wheel on that cart was still dragging, and he got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm, and about nine people watched him and not one of them said a word about it.

At about two the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing put his right forearm on the boards of that table and left it there, and at the end of the hour it had gone dead from the elbow down and the hand at the end of it did not move when he told it to, and nobody said anything about that either.

At about three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given.

At about half past three a voice at the near end of that table said out loud that a ground with a name in it and no page under it was the most honest thing in this district, and that it had taken two days for a few of them to work that out, and nine people agreed with it, and one of them said that agreeing with a sentence did not put it on a page, and nobody wrote it down.

---

A clerk of nineteen years read her own page out loud at about half past four, from the top, to the boy of about nineteen and not to the yard behind him. The bid has stood open eighty-nine days and was not run this day and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and nineteen days past, and a ladder is spent as a ladder. The rule said out loud in that ground on the tenth of the tenth month is forty-nine days standing and has not been kept on any day anybody has counted. The figure on the second line of that lot book is forty-four days out of date, was not altered, and has nothing written beside it. The figure entered off a list on the thirtieth of the tenth month is thirty days old and has not been checked by anybody and cannot be checked by anybody in this district. The word unchecked is in the margin of her own page over that figure and is over nothing else, and she read that out loud too, and then said that a clerk reading her own page to one other person is not a thing read back from the top and binds nobody.

She put the page down and the boy of about nineteen copied the whole of it onto his own, in his own hand, in one column, and then read his own column back to her from the top. She did not correct one word of it, because there was nothing in it to correct, and a couple of people at that table said afterwards that a boy reading a clerk's page back to that clerk is not a second of anything and that it is a thing two people did because neither of them had anything else to do.

Two lines in a book under a stone that has been there two months, on the face of a book in the open all afternoon, where a stranger could have walked up to that table at half past four and read both of them. Both of them were true when they were written. One of them is not true now. Neither of those two things was on the page in front of him.
