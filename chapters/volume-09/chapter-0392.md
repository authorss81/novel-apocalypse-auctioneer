# Chapter 392: The Stone and the Second Line

---

The stone on that lot book had gone green down one side of it in about a week of rain and nobody there had turned it over, and the corner of the second page was standing up about a quarter of an inch clear of the boards where the damp had got into it. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

The twentieth of the tenth month came in with the ground hard underfoot and the light coming up behind that bank. The man of fifty-six read the four figures off those boards out loud over the mender's shoulder and got all four. Then he went up that lane to the fence end for the first time in two mornings, and was not in the yard for about an hour, and came back down it at about eleven and read them again and got all four.

"**Nobody has entered anything for three hundred and thirty days, the board carries three hundred and the train on that siding has stood six hundred and sixteen, and a hundred and ninety-one days separate the second of January and this morning, and nobody has put a line on a board since the first of the fourth month.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and that a line in a lot book is a line about a lot and is not a line on a board, and that no line was put on one today.

Between about ten and about half past ten, about four people said out loud that two evenings ago the man of fifty-six had sat down in the second chair at the end of that table and got up out of it again, that nobody had counted how long he was in it, and that nobody had said one word about it since. About four other people said that a man sits in a chair and gets up out of it, and that is a figure about a sitting and not a figure about a man.
"Nobody counted," somebody said.

"Nobody ever counts," somebody else said. "That is the whole of it. Say it once in six months and it is not a thing that happened, it is a thing somebody did."

The man of fifty-six came back down that lane at about eleven, read the four figures again, got all four, and said nothing whatever about the chair. Nobody asked him about it.

One of the people who had been standing at the near end of that table said one more thing about the boards, and it was the ordinary sort of thing, which is that the record set against four places four hundred miles off has been in force a hundred and forty-one days, and that the age of the figure on that figure is a hundred and forty-one days as well. Two figures counted off one day and one exclusion land on one interval, and that is not a mistake and is not the same figure twice. The rival record and the age of the figure about that figure are two figures and not one figure. They stand on the same number today for the reason two figures do, and no column in this district is going to be moved to break the equality, and none was moved today.
---

At about half past ten the man of about thirty-four who digs loam came up out of that ditch wet to the thigh, the way he comes up most mornings, and came to the end of that trestle table and read the two lines in that lot book standing up. He is the man whose mouth the second of those two lines came out of on the thirteenth of this month. Nobody there had asked him to read anything.

He got to the second line and stopped there.

Then he went about nine feet to the left and looked over the low wall at the water in that ditch, and stood at it a while with his arms down at his sides, and came back to the table and stood there.

Then he said one thing out loud in the yard, in the ordinary voice, in front of about nineteen people, and the man of about nineteen counted what he said and got sixty-one and put it on his own page in his own hand, and put a line under the sixty-one the way he puts a line under a figure he is keeping.

"**That second line is out of date and I am the one whose mouth it came out of, and it stopped being true on the fifteenth of this month, and it is five days out of date this morning, and nobody wrote down the day it stopped and I did not say the day because nobody asked me for the day.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the second line of that lot book has been out of date five days, counted from the fifteenth of this month with the day it stopped being true not counted in. Five is a figure about a figure and is not a figure about that lot. A line that has stopped being true is not a wrong line and is not a mistake anybody made.

Then about four people said the thing anybody would say, which is that a man could go down into that ditch with a stick and put it in and bring out a figure and write it on a piece of paper, and not on the book.

"And on the piece of paper it is his," somebody else said. "A figure on a piece of paper in a coat is a figure four people can argue about. A figure in a book a stranger may walk up to is a thing anybody can read."

The mender said out loud that a wrong count is not struck by writing a correct one beside it. Putting the correct figure on a piece of paper in a yard in daylight in front of about nineteen people is putting the correct figure beside it in every way that matters.

"He was right," somebody said.

"Nobody has said anything this morning that was worth the paper," somebody else said, and about four of them agreed, and about four of them said that it had been about a piece of paper, and both of those were true and nobody worked out how.

No figure was written on any paper in the yard today and nobody measured the water in that ditch. The second line of that lot book has not been altered and has not been replaced and no figure has been written beside it. The old charter in the book four hundred yards from that gate is still wrong on its face in a book a stranger may walk up to, and a wrong count is not struck by writing a correct one beside it, and the two are the same rule standing in two places and neither of them was repaired today.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came to the end of that table, put his right hand in his coat before he got there, and read the two lines in that lot book out loud from the top in the ordinary voice, both of them, without stopping, and put the book back down under the same stone.

He put the stone on the corner it had been on all week. He put his hand on the top of the stone first to be sure of it, the way a man checks a thing he is going to be asked about.

Then he said one thing out loud, and the man of about nineteen counted it and got sixty-nine and put the count down, and then asked him to say the last sentence of it again, and put that one down too, in different words, because it was a different sentence.

"**Nobody wrote down the day that figure stopped being true, and the only man in this district who knows it is the man who cuts reeds out of that ditch, and he has not been asked for it, and I am not going to ask him for it in this yard, and a figure I have to keep is not the same as a figure I am paid for.**"

What he said out loud in the yard was a description and not a name, and the man it belongs to was not asked, and saying out loud in a yard who a person is does not make it a reading and does not bind anything, and it did not bind anything this morning.

About four people stood at the low wall after that and looked down into that ditch without saying a figure. The water was there and it was standing, and it was deeper than it had been on the thirteenth of this month and shallower than it had been on the fifteenth. Nobody put a stick in it. Nobody went down into it. The road keeper stood at that wall for about ten minutes and then went up the lane without saying anything at all.

The man of about nineteen went down the bank at about half past two on his own, without saying he was going and without anybody asking him to. He got to the boards at the side of that ditch with his left hand first and his right hand after, and his right hand, which is the one hand in the yard that has not closed properly since the tenth of the eighth month and did not close today either, was on the wet wood, and it slipped. He got his knee in and sat down on the bank with his feet in about four inches of water for about a minute and a half.

He did not pick anything up. He did not put anything in.

He came back up that bank on his hands and his knees, the way a man comes up that bank, and water came off both knees.

About nine people watched him do it from the top of that wall and not one of them said a word to him. When he got to the top he said one thing out loud to nobody in particular, and nobody counted it.

"**I have been in the water in that ditch and I am not going to be the one who brings a figure back out of it, because the moment I do there are two figures about that lot and a stranger walking up to that table cannot tell which of them is the one.**"

He went into the water in that ditch this afternoon and came back out of it and did not measure it and did not refuse to. Nobody had asked him, so he has not refused and cannot be counted either way, and a figure he could have brought back and did not bring back is not a figure this district is holding and is not a figure it has lost.

The rest of that day went ordinary and the ordinary part of it was longer than a morning. The road keeper came up that lane at about half past three, read the four figures, got all four, and went on up. About four people said out loud that a man who has been in that lane every day this month and got all four every time has not once looked at that ditch from the low wall.

"I looked at it the first day I came down that lane," the road keeper said. "Six years ago. It was water then as well."

"A ditch is a ditch," somebody said.

"I keep a road."

He went on up. About four of them said he was right and about four of them said that is two things about him and neither of them is a figure.

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam went back into that ditch at about two and was still in it at about half past four and came up the bank wet to the chest. The reed cutter was in that ditch until the light went and then was not, and nobody there said one word to him when he came up the bank at the end of it, and his hands were not touched and nothing was asked about them and he was given nothing. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his cart, and the tent was still rolled where it has been since the thirtieth of the June.

That bid stands at fifty days and was not run today and nothing has been said about closing it. The first day of the eighth month is eighty days past. The ninth of the nine printed nights is a hundred and eighty-three days back and is open, and it was named once in the yard this morning by a clerk and closed in nothing. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and was empty at about six, and no name went into it by anybody.

By the time the light had gone the stone was back on that book at the same corner it had been on all week, and the second page was still standing up about a quarter of an inch clear of the boards, and nobody turned the stone over. The man of fifty-six was at the end of that table with his hand flat on those boards again and the four figures on them were the same four figures they had been at about half past eight. The fifth of the five things this district does not have, which is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, is still not paid, and a man who went into a ditch this afternoon and came out of it without a figure is not a man anybody has paid.
