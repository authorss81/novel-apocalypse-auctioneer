# Chapter 422: Two Kinds Of Figure

Two men there said the same word on the same morning, and one of them had said it to a woman at a trough and one of them had said it to a table. By about eleven about nine people had worked out that the two of those were not the same thing, and had not said what the thing was.

It was the twentieth of the eleventh month and there was rain in the night that had stopped before the light came. The ground at the south end of the yard of Lot Seventeen was soft to the boot and about nineteen people were in it by ten.

The man of fifty-six said the four figures off that wall with his palm flat on the boards, got every one of them, said the word with them, and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and thirty, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and forty-six days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and sixty, and three hundred and twenty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and seventy-one days, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a figure about a sheet and that there is no day-count for the sheet itself.

---

He was at the top of that bank at about half past nine with the bundle under his arm, and he had not been asked for anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked. Three people at the end of that trestle table had been watching him not be asked since about ten to nine.

At about half past ten a man who stands at the near end of that table on most days asked him a question out loud, in the ordinary voice, in front of about nineteen people. It was the first time in six weeks that anybody there had asked that man anything at all.

The boy of about nineteen counted what the man said and put it down and did not read it back, because a man had said a number yesterday and the boy had read that one back, and he was not going to read two of them in two mornings.

"Say the number again this morning where the clerk can hear it," the man said.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds looked at him for about as long as a figure takes to be said.

"No," he said.

He said it in the ordinary voice and he did not raise it and he did not put the bundle down, and about nine people heard the whole of it.

"You said it yesterday to a woman at a trough and about nineteen people heard that, and the clerk was standing nine feet away with her page out, and there is not one of us who can put your figure next to the figure on the second line of that book and say which of the two is a figure about a lot."

"I did not say it to a woman at a trough," he said. "She asked me a question about water and I gave her a number, and the number I gave her is a number about a man, and if you want to write it down you can write that down. If you want the number you can come and ask me for it and you can ask me in the morning and I will tell you whether I have got it this morning or not."

He went up the lane with the bundle under his arm and the hook in his hand, and nobody followed him, and three people standing at that table looked at the clerk, and the clerk had her page shut under her arm.

"You were asked for the number," somebody said.

"I was asked for it by a man who wanted to put it next to a figure, and that is a different question from being asked for it, and I have not worked out which one he meant, and I am not going to write it down until I know which one it was." She turned a page over and shut it again. "He did not refuse. A man who has said a thing and has not been asked a question has not refused anything and cannot be counted either way, and the record about the not asking says not asked."

---

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came the length of that trestle table at about eleven and put his right hand flat on the boards, and his left hand was in the cloth it has been in since the eleventh of the June. He stood there long enough that the clerk asked him whether he wanted the end of that table for anything in particular.

"I wanted to be the same end of the table as a man I have not been able to argue with in nine days," he said, "and that is not a thing you can enter, so I am going to stop talking about it."

At about half past eleven the man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in that ditch up to his thigh, and the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was not in it at all for the first time in about nine days. Two people at that table noticed the difference, and neither of them said what the difference was.

At about twelve the man who puts tables up asked the clerk of nineteen years, out loud and in front of about nine people, what the difference was between a figure a man says and a figure a man writes. She answered him in about four seconds.

"A figure a man writes is in the same place tomorrow," she said. "A figure a man says is in about nineteen heads and it is in them slightly differently every time and there is no way of getting two of them to agree and no way of knowing which of them is the one he said. That is the whole of it. I have been writing things down for five months and I have never once got a figure back off a person that matched what I wrote, and I have never once been told that I had got one wrong."

"Nobody has ever asked you whether you got it right," said the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing.

"Nobody has ever asked me whether I got it right," she agreed, "and I want that written down as a fact about this yard and not as a thing about me, and I am not going to write it down myself, so somebody had better do it or not do it."

Nobody wrote it down. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud that he was not going to do it because it was her sentence and she could put it on a page or not put it on a page, and three people at that table said that was correct.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down that bank and took up the same nine feet with his right hand in his coat. He had said nothing out loud for two days and about nine people at that table had looked at him to see whether he was going to, and he was not.

At about half past one the woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale came down that bank with the folded thing under her arm and went past the end of that table without stopping at it. Two people at the near end noticed, and one of them said out loud that she had not stopped there for two days. The man who puts tables up said he was not going to say whether that was about the book.

At about two the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up said one thing at the end of that trestle table with his hand flat on the boards. The boy of about nineteen counted what he said and got ninety-one and read it back to himself in a low voice, and one person heard the number and said it was higher than the figure he had got yesterday for a different sentence.

"**A figure a man says in a yard is a figure about a yard and a figure a man writes on a page is a figure about a page, and the two of them have been in this yard for six months side by side and about four of you have been treating them as one, and the reason you cannot get a bid run on the one is not that the bid is old. It is that the only figures anybody has for that lot came out of two mouths.**"

Nobody read that back from the top. Two people at the near end of that table said out loud that the reading of that lot stands exactly where it stood, and one of them said the other one had already said it, and the other one said it did not matter who said it first because nobody was going to write it down.

Then two people at opposite ends of that table said what the number had been yesterday, and they said different numbers, and neither of them had written it down, and both of them said they were certain. The man of about nineteen said he had counted the words in the sentence and not the number in it, and that the two of those are different jobs and he had only done the first one. He said he could have written the number down at about ten yesterday and that he had not thought of it, and that this morning he would not be able to get it back out of his own head. Three people at that table said that was the whole of what had just been proved, and one of them said it was proved in about nine seconds.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said the other thing. He said that a number four people remember differently is not a wrong number and is not a mistake anybody made, and that it is a number four people have, and that the only thing wrong with it is that there is one of it. Then he said that he had watched four other things in this yard go the same way since the sixth month and that none of the four had been anybody's fault either.

At about half past two a man came up that lane and went up it and did not stop. It was the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart, and he did not read anything on the boards or on the page or in the book. The near wheel on that cart was still dragging, and he got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and set it down and got back on the seat, and nobody said one word about it. One person said afterwards that a thing nobody says anything about is not a thing anybody has checked.

---

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open eighty days and was not run today, and that nobody proposed anything about closing it. The rival record has been in force a hundred and seventy-one days and was not read out, and no ninth reading was proposed. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and ten days past. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is forty days standing. The figure on the second line of that lot book is thirty-five days out of date, was not altered, and has nothing written beside it, and a clerk entered that a man said out loud there that a figure a man says and a figure a man writes are two different objects, and entered no figure. The word unchecked is in the margin of a clerk's page and is over nothing. The line under those twelve words is twenty-one days old. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid.

At about half past five the woman of fifty-eight came down that bank with two empty buckets, filled them at the trough, carried them up the two goes, and read the two lines in that lot book standing up on her way past without saying a word to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her. She did not stop at the end of that table on her way up this evening, and three people noticed that she did not stop, and none of them said what the not stopping was about.

Then the man of fifty-six read the four figures for the last time that day and got all four of them. The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up that bank wet to the chest at about half past four, stopped at the end of that table for about four seconds, and was not asked for anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The two lines in that book were on the face of a book in the open all day and are on it now, and the second of them is thirty-five days out of date. There is a figure in this district that a man gave out loud yesterday in about four seconds, and it is not on the page and not off the page, and it is not anywhere else at all except in about nineteen heads, and there is no instrument in this district that can go and get it.
