# Chapter 427: Two People Who Knew And Were Not Saying

Two people there had known since about eleven o'clock that something was going to be said out loud in it, and neither of them said it to anybody. The boy of about nineteen was standing there from about half past eight, did not know until about three, and was not told.

It was the twenty-fifth of the eleventh month and the sky was the colour of a wet road again. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

He said the four figures off those boards with his hand flat on them, and got all of them, and said the word with them, and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and thirty-five, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and fifty-one days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and sixty-five, and three hundred and twenty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and seventy-six days, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a figure about a sheet, and that there is no day-count for that sheet.

---

The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up had been at the east wall since about half past eight with his hand-cart, and the tent is still rolled on the back of it. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came to that end of the table at about eleven and put both forearms on the boards. They said about nine words to each other in about four seconds and then stood there for about nine minutes without saying anything else, and three people at the near end of that table watched two men not talk and none of them said what they thought they were doing.

"You have not said it," the mender said, at the end of it.

"I have not said it."

"You have been carrying it since the sixth of this month and this is the nineteenth day and you have said it about nine times in the last three weeks, every time to a different person, and every time it has been the same nine words."

"Nine words is a small thing to carry for nineteen days."

"Nine words in a yard is nineteen days of everybody there knowing that nine words exist, and half of them have worked out that the nine words are a name, and none of them can say so out loud because a name said out loud is a thing that is said twice, and the second time is the one that is written down." The mender took his forearms off the boards. "And I am not going to be the man who asks you for it, and you are not going to tell me, and we are going to stand at this table for the rest of the day and neither of us is going to do anything about it."

They both stayed at that table for the rest of the day. A clerk of nineteen years entered that two people spoke to each other at about eleven and that she was not told what about, and that the record about the not telling says not told, and that a person who has not been asked a question has not refused anything and cannot be counted either way.

At about half past eleven the man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in that ditch to his thigh and heard none of the nine words, and he said so afterwards to two people at the low wall, and both of them said they did not know whether that was true.

---

The boy of about nineteen came down that bank at about half past eight, which he has done on most days of this month, and he did his page at the east wall from about nine. He did not come to that table until about ten, and there he stood at the near end of it and read the twelve off the margin of the clerk's page going down them and got all twelve, and two people said the ninth one was still where his voice went.

"You are early," said the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up, without looking round. "You have been early every morning this week and you have never once come at eight before this month. What has changed."

"I have not got a morning routine and you know that I have not got a morning routine, because if I had one then it would be a thing about me and I would have to keep it, and I am not going to have a thing about me." He put his page down. "I came early because I did not want to be here for whatever it is."

"Then you have been told something," said the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing.

"No. Nobody has told me anything. Nobody has told me anything in this yard since the ninth of this month and I have asked about it four times since and every time somebody has said not asked, and I have written that down myself four times, and the record says not asked and it is not said by me and it is not said by anybody else." He picked his page up again. "I am not early because I know. I am early because the four of you have been saying for three days that something is going to be said. I would rather be standing in this yard when it happens than walking down the bank. I would rather be standing here not knowing what it is than be told, and about four of you have worked out by now that those are two different sentences and one of them is the one I mean."

At about twelve a man at the near end of that table said, out loud, that the boy of about nineteen had just told four people that he had a preference about being told things. The clerk of nineteen years said that a preference is not a column and that she was not going to write it anywhere. The boy said that was correct, and that he had said it out loud so that about nine people would know it had been said.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down that bank and stood nine feet off that table with his right hand in his coat. He had not been there at ten and two people had noticed. He said nothing out loud for about an hour, which is the fourth morning running and the fifth. A voice at the near end of that table said that a man who says nothing five mornings running has said the same thing five mornings running. Another voice said that a man who says nothing five mornings running has also not said five different things, and that those were two different sentences and one of them was the correct one.

At about half past one the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up that bank wet to the chest with the bundle under his arm and stopped at the end of that table for about four seconds. About nine people were within four feet of him and nobody said a word to him and he was not asked for anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked, and he was not offered an arm and he did not ask for one, and he went up the lane.

At about two the road keeper came up that lane and got the four figures off that wall as he went past, and stopped at the low wall, and looked down into that ditch for about two minutes, and said one word to nobody in particular and went on up the lane, and nobody asked him what the word was.

At about half past one the woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale came down that bank and went past that table without stopping at it, which is the fourth time in six days. A man said out loud that four was a figure and that five would be a habit, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud that the difference between four and five was a sentence and not a rule, and that about four of them at that table were keeping a count of a woman to see whether the count turned into something.

Nobody kept the count. The clerk of nineteen years said that she was not going to keep it either, and that a figure about how many times a person went past a table was a figure about a table and not about a person. The man of about thirty-one who digs loam said from the water that she was the first person there in two months to say that about anybody, and the clerk said she had not been asked to be the first person at anything.

At about quarter past two the boy of about nineteen asked the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing a question out loud and in front of about nine people, and the mender answered him in about four seconds.

"What happens to a figure that nobody can check."

"It stays a figure that nobody can check. There is no second place for it and there is no punishment for it and there is not a person you can go and ask, and if you put it on a page then in a month the page is a thing somebody can be shown, and if you do not put it on a page then it is a thing somebody can repeat." He did not look at the boy. "Your fortnight ends on the first of the month after this one. What are you going to do about that."

"I have not worked it out," the boy said, and three people at that table said that was the first time in about nine days that a person standing there had admitted in a plain sentence that they had not worked something out. The clerk of nineteen years entered that a boy of about nineteen said out loud in that yard that he had not worked out what he was going to do about a figure, and entered the question that was asked him and the answer that came back, and entered no figure.

At about half past two the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up said one thing out loud at the east wall, and the boy of about nineteen counted what he said and got fifty-six and read it back to himself, and two people heard the number.

"**If it is going to be said tomorrow then it will be said about eleven, because nothing there has ever been said after two and I have been putting that table up for six months and I have watched four things get said in this yard and every one of them was said in the morning.**"

Nobody said that he was right. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said that the time of day was the only part of the sentence he was going to argue with, and that he was not going to argue with it at all.

---

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open eighty-five days and was not run today, and that nothing was proposed about closing it. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and fifteen days past. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is forty-five days standing. The figure on the second line of that lot book is forty days out of date, was not altered, and has nothing written beside it. The word unchecked is in the margin of a clerk's page and is over nothing. The line under those twelve words is twenty-six days old. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid. Two people there knew a thing and neither of them said it, and a clerk entered that two people spoke to each other and entered not what about.

At about half past five the two empty buckets came down that bank and went back up them full, and the woman of fifty-eight read the two lines in that lot book standing up on her way past, said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her. Then the man of fifty-six read the four figures for the last time that day and got all four of them, and about nine people at that table noticed that both of the men who had known were still at that table when he did it.

The two of them stood at the ends of the same trestle table for the last hour of that light and neither of them said the nine words, and two people watched them not say it. The boy of about nineteen was in the same yard the whole time and did not know what the two of them were not saying, and the day was not over yet and would not be over for another hour.
