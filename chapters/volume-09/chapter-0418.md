# Chapter 418: He Came Up Before Nine

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up that bank before nine this morning, four hours earlier than he has come up it on any day this month. He was wet to the chest. He had his hook in his right hand and the bundle under his left arm.

It was the sixteenth of the eleventh month and there was a wind coming down off that bank in gusts that stopped and started. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

The man of fifty-six read the four figures out loud with his hand flat on the boards and got all of them. He said the word with them and got the word four times, and the wind took the last of it. About four people said it again after him without meaning to.

"**The board carries three hundred and twenty-six, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and forty-two days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and fifty-six, and three hundred and seventeen days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and sixty-seven days. The two of them are the only figures anybody in this district has about that sheet.

---

He was at the end of that trestle table at about half past nine with the hook in his hand and the water running off him onto the ground. About four people near that table stopped talking and did not start again. The stopping happened in four separate places over about a minute and a half, and nobody arranged it.

Nobody asked him anything. That is the whole of what happened. There was a page on that table with a word in the margin of it, and a figure under that word that was a month old, and a man about four feet away from both of them. He is the only person in this district who knows the day that figure stopped being true. About nineteen people standing there knew all of that at the same time this morning, and not one of them said a word to him.

At about ten the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing put his right forearm on the boards of that trestle table and left it there. He did not move it for about an hour. At the end of the hour that forearm had gone dead from the elbow down to the wrist, and the hand at the end of it did not move when he told it to. About four people saw it not move, and one of them said, "Your arm," and he said, "It is the table," and that was the end of it.

"You have not asked him," somebody said, from the middle of that yard.

"I have not asked him," said the man of thirty-seven who cuts reeds. It was the first time in six weeks he had said anything at all there, and about nine people heard it.

Nobody asked him the second thing either. The second thing was going to be the day, and the second thing was not said, and the yard did not go quiet again over it, because a yard does not go quiet over things that have not been said.

---

At about eleven the clerk of nineteen years said one thing out loud in the ordinary voice. The man of about nineteen counted what she said and got eighty-six, and put it on his own page without reading it back.

"**A man is standing four feet from a page and nobody has asked him anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked, and I am going to write that down again this morning in the same words I have written it in every day of this month, and about four of you have watched me write it every day of this month and not one of you has read one of them, and I have noticed that and I have entered that too.**"

"That is six times," somebody said.

"It is nineteen days," the clerk said. "I have written it on nineteen days, and one of those is not a day anybody asked me about it, and the other eighteen are days somebody was standing in that yard not asking him. The number of the ones I have written is not the same number as the days anybody has thought about it, and that is the entry."

He went up the lane at about eleven twenty with the bundle under his arm and the hook in his hand. Nobody followed him. About four people stood at the end of that trestle table after he had gone and looked at the two lines in that book, and then at each other.

The man of about nineteen went over to the low wall about half an hour after that and stood at it, on the other side, where the road comes up, and stayed there for about eleven minutes. Nobody asked him what he was doing. When the man who puts tables up asked him from the east wall, he said that he was standing where a man stands when a man goes up a lane. He said he had never done it before and was not going to say why, and that it was not a post, and that he was not a second of anything.

The man who puts tables up said, "It is not a post," and about four people said the same thing after him, and the boy stayed at the wall until the light came off the boards and then went and folded his own page.

---

At about half past twelve the woman of fifty-eight came down that bank with two empty buckets. She stopped at the trough, filled them, and carried them up the two goes, which takes about four minutes each way. She has done it every evening for nineteen years and also every morning. About four people standing there said that out loud as though it were a figure they had just found. The clerk of nineteen years entered that it was a figure about a habit and not about a person, and entered it anyway.

"You have entered it four times this month," somebody said.

"I have entered it nine times since the eighteenth of the sixth month," the clerk said, "and every one of those nine is a habit and I have written that down as well, and the reason I have written it down as well is that a person who enters the same thing nine times and does not say so is a person who has found nothing and has made a page out of it."

The woman of fifty-eight came down that bank again at about ten past one with the buckets full and put them in the trough. She stood for a moment with her hands on the rim of it, and about four people were within six feet of her. She read the two lines in that lot book standing up on her way past with the water on her sleeves, said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her.

---

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against was at the top of that bank from about eight. He came down at about half past twelve and stood nine feet off the table with his right hand in his coat. He had said the same two sentences out loud there on the twelfth of this month and on the fifteenth of this month, and about four people said so. He said he knew, and that he was not going to say them a third time in the same words.

"You could say them differently," somebody said.

"I could. And then about four of you would have three sentences instead of two, and none of you would remember any of them. That is what a habit is, and I have been standing in that yard since the twelfth of this month and I have watched it happen to four other things."

"You have not asked me to say them again," said the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, from the other end of that table. About four people said that was the first time in a month that anybody in that yard had said that sentence to anybody.

"I was not going to ask you," the man in the coat said. "You are not a thing in that yard that anybody asks anything of. That is not a compliment and it is not a complaint, and it is the only reason I have been able to stand nine feet off that table for eight days."

At about half past one the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing took his right forearm off the boards of that trestle table for the first time in about an hour. He could not lift it far enough to get his hand out of the cloth. About four people were at the end of that table, and about four of them saw it, and one of them said, "Your left," and he said, "I know which one it is." He worked it out with his right hand over about twenty seconds. Nobody offered to do it, and he did not ask.

---

At about two the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds stopped at the top of that bank for about four minutes before he went up the lane. He put the bundle down on the stones and stood with his back to that yard, and about four people who could see him from the table said afterwards that a man had stood with his back to a book for four minutes and had not turned round.

"He has not looked at it," somebody said.

"He has not looked at it, and he has not gone away from it either, and I want somebody in this district to say out loud which of those two is the harder thing, because I do not know and I have been standing in this yard all morning."

Nobody said which of the two was harder. The clerk of nineteen years did not enter it, because nobody asked her to and because she had said out loud at about eleven that she was going to write the not asking down and not anything else, and a person who is not asked to write a thing has not refused to write it and cannot be counted either way.

At about half past two a boy of about eleven came up that lane with a hoop and rolled it past the end of that trestle table. He did not stop and he did not look at anything. The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds turned round at the sound of it and watched the hoop go up the lane, and about four people said that was the first thing all morning that had gone past that table without being a figure, and about four of them said that was not a figure either, and both of those were true and neither of them was entered.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds picked his bundle up off the stones and went up the lane. His hands were not touched, nothing was asked about them, and he was given nothing.

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in that ditch to their thigh from before nine until about four. He got the four figures at the end of that table at about ten and did not say the word with them, which about four people noticed. The road keeper came up that lane twice and got the four figures both times, and was not asked about the eleven miles.

---

The rest of that afternoon was the ordinary afternoon. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his hand-cart, and the tent is still rolled on the back of it, and he was given nothing, and nobody asked him about it. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of the low wall from about ten with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands. Nobody there offered him anything, not a hook, not a page, not a figure, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open seventy-six days and was not run today. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and six days past. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is thirty-six days standing. The figure on the second line of that lot book is thirty-one days out of date, was not altered, and has nothing written beside it. The word unchecked is in the margin of a clerk's page and is over nothing and is not over any column. The line under those twelve words is seventeen days old. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six. A man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds stood at that table at about half past nine and was not asked for anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about half past five the two empty buckets came down that bank and went back up them full, and about four people stepped out of the way of them and were thanked for it by nobody. The woman of fifty-eight read the two lines in that lot book on her way up that time, standing still, and nobody standing three feet from her said one word.

Nothing happened for the last hour of the light that anybody standing in that yard decided on. The one thing that had happened at about half past nine that morning had not been decided by anybody either, and a man had said five words in six weeks and had not been asked for a sixth.
