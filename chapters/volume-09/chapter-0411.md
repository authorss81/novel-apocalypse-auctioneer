# Chapter 411: The Word Back On

The man of fifty-six said the word again on the ninth of this month after a morning without it, and the clerk of nineteen years wrote on her own page that a word a man has skipped once is a word a man can skip, and nobody at that wall said anything at all.

It was the ninth of the eleventh month, the light was flat, and there was no wind in the yard at all. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

He read the four figures out loud with his hand flat on the boards and got all of them, and said the word after each one, and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and nineteen, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and thirty-five days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and forty-nine, and three hundred and ten days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move today. A clerk of nineteen years entered that, and entered that the age of it as a figure about the figure is a hundred and sixty days, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a day-count for a sheet, and that there is no day-count for that sheet because there never was one.

---

He read the four again at about ten and got them. He read them at about eleven and got them. At about half past eleven he stopped for a while and rubbed the fingers of his right hand with the heel of his left, and about nine people watched him do that, and not one of them offered him anything.

"I could not hold the chalk yesterday," he said. "That is all it was. I have got no reason for it and I have not had one all morning."

"You have had a morning off a word," somebody said. "You have still got the four figures."

"I have got the four figures, and I am going to read them at four o'clock this afternoon, with the word or without it. I have not decided which, and I would rather nobody in that yard decided for me."

Then the clerk of nineteen years said one thing out loud in the ordinary voice, and the man of about nineteen counted what she said and got ninety-three and put it on his own page and did not read it back to himself.

"**I wrote the word unchecked twelve times on the first of this month before there was anybody in this yard, and it has been on that page every morning since, and if a man of fifty-six says it four times with a figure this morning and does not say it tomorrow then it is a habit, and a habit and a finding are two different things, and this one is a habit, and I am the one who put it there and I am the one who has to say that out loud.**"

Nobody read it back from the top. A thing read back by nobody binds nobody, and about four people said that out loud, and about four of them did not, and a woman who has said it out loud in this yard four times in a fortnight was the fourth to say it this morning.

The clerk of nineteen years put her own page down flat on the boards after that and did not pick it up again for about an hour. Three of them came and stood at it and read the twelve words in her hand going down the page. One of them said that he could not read the ninth one from where he was standing. The clerk said that was the ninth one's own fault and not his.

"A habit is a finding," somebody said at the near end of that trestle table.

"A habit is a finding about a person and not about the word, and you have just told me which of the two you would rather it was, and it is the one where you are not on your own."

"Then write it on the page," said the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, from the end of that table, with his left hand in its cloth, "and put your own name to it, and then in two months when it is still a habit you will have to come back and read your own name on it."

"I am not going to put my name to a word in a margin," the clerk said, "and it is not because I am frightened of it, and I want that said in the same breath, because about four of you have been putting those two things together since the first of this month and they are not the same thing. A name on a figure is a thing that cannot be taken off the figure by the person whose name it is, and a column with a name in it is a different instrument from a word in a margin, and I have been asked this month which of the two I would rather have been right about and I have not answered it and I am not going to answer it today."

Nobody asked her what that meant and two of them wanted to and did not, and the yard went back to what it was doing.

---

At about half past twelve the man of about nineteen came back from the trough with his own page in his hand and stood beside the man of fifty-six at that wall. Two people watched a boy of about nineteen stand at a wall beside a man of fifty-six, and neither of them said anything for about a minute.

"You have not read them out loud since this morning," the boy said.

"I have read them out loud five times since this morning."

"You have read them out loud five times since this morning and you have not said the word once since about ten."

The man of fifty-six put his palm flat on those boards and looked at it.

"I have not said it once since about ten," he said, "and I have got no idea why, and I am not going to stand at a wall with you and work it out. Go and do your page."

"You have got a figure on that page a stranger could check, and you have got eleven you could get four times this morning, and you have not said the word out loud once since ten. I am not saying it to be clever. I am saying it because you asked me a question on the seventh of this month and I have not been able to put it down anywhere, and it is sitting on my page under a line, and it is about your mouth and not about the figures."

The man of fifty-six did not answer that. The boy went and did his page. Two of the people who had heard all of it said afterwards that the boy had asked the man a question and had been told nothing, and the record about the not answering says not answered, and does not say refused.

At about one the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came the length of that trestle table and put his right hand flat on the boards and his left hand in its cloth where it has been since the eleventh of the June, and read the four figures off the wall over the mender's shoulder and got every one of them.

"I want to say the thing that has been going round since the eighth of this month, and then I am going to stop and go back to the fence. A word on a figure is a label. A figure is the last thing anybody in this district can be asked to carry a label on, and it is also the only thing a man can be asked to carry at all, because a figure is the only thing that can be set down and read again by somebody who was not there."

"That is two things said as one," somebody said.

"It is two things said as one on purpose. A label is the last thing a figure can carry, and a label is the only thing anybody can check. Those two are both true and they are about different things, and about four of you have been picking the one that suits you since the first of this month and calling it the whole of what I said."

At that somebody said that the label was the only checkable thing about the twelve figures, and the mender said yes, and then said that the label told you how the figure was got and not whether it was right, and that those were two different questions and a person standing at that table could ask one of them and not the other.

"A figure anybody can check is a figure somebody can be wrong about," the man who puts tables up said from the east wall, without coming over. "I have been putting that table up for anybody who will use it for six months and I have never once known what I was putting it up for, and I worked that out about four months ago and I have not told anybody, and the reason I have not told anybody is that the answer is a sentence and a sentence is not a table."

Nobody wrote the answer down, and two people said afterwards that they would have written it down if they had heard it, and he said he knew that, and that was the point of saying it to the few people still at the end of a long day.

Nobody wrote either of those down. The clerk of nineteen years had her own page under her arm and did not take it out.

---

At about two the man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up that bank with the water to his thigh. He read the four figures at the end of that table, got all of them, and said the word four times. That right arm of his did not go above the level of that shoulder at any point in the afternoon, and nobody said one word to him about it. The road keeper came up that lane at about three, got the four figures off that wall as he went past, and went on up without stopping. Nobody asked him about the eleven miles.

The line under the twelve words on that page was ten days old this afternoon. It is a figure off a list in a room four hundred yards up that bank, and no person in this district owns that list, and nobody has read it, and there is no way of checking it from this yard by any means available to anybody standing here.

"Ten days," somebody said at the near end of that trestle table.

"Ten days and a word on it," somebody else said, "and the word is the only reason you have looked at it at all. The word does not tell you whether it is thirty-one, or whether it is anything at all."

"I know that," the clerk said. "I wrote it because there was a line to fill, and I have said that out loud in this yard once and I am not going to say it a fifth time."

---

At about half past two a man came up that lane with a hand-cart, did not come into the yard, and stopped at the low wall. Everybody at that table looked up when he stopped. It was the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart, and he read nothing at all, not the boards, not the page, and not the two lines in the book.

The near wheel was dragging. He got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and set it down, and did it again about four seconds later, and got back on the seat.

"He has been at that wall nine times since the middle of the ninth month," somebody said, "and he has never once read anything on any of those nine days."

"He has never once read anything because nobody has ever asked him to read anything," said the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up, from the east wall. "Those are two different things and the rest of you have been counting him as a finding for six weeks."

Then he went down that lane and did not come up it again that day, and two of them said good evening as he went past the end of that table, and he said good evening back.

The man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down to that table at about four and stood nine feet off it, which is the distance he has stood at every day this month, with his right hand in his coat. He looked at the twelve words in the margin and at the one line under them that carried a figure and nothing behind it, and he said nothing out loud, and a voice at the near end of that trestle table said that a man standing nine feet off a page in the open is not a reading and is not a finding.

He came down that bank at about four with one thing in his head, which was the word. He was going to say it out loud with a figure beside it, the way the man of fifty-six said it. By the time he got to the end of that table he had understood that he was not going to say it. A man of fifty-six who has said it four times a morning since the first of this month does not need a man with a bad hand to come and do it also. He did not say that to anybody, and it is the reason he said nothing at all.

He had not asked for anything today and nothing was asked of him, and at about half past four he went back up the bank with his hand still in his coat.

Then the man of fifty-six read the four figures again at four o'clock and got all of them, and said the word after each one, and got the word four times. About nine people heard him and none of them said anything.

After that nothing happened there that anybody in it decided on. The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in that ditch until about half past four. He came up the bank wet to the chest and was not asked about anything. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his hand-cart. The tent is still rolled on the back of it, where it has been since the thirtieth of the June. He was given nothing.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open sixty-nine days and was not run today, and that nobody proposed anything about closing it. The first day of the eighth month is ninety-nine days past, and a ladder is spent as a ladder. The ninth of the nine printed nights is two hundred and two days back, and is open. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is twenty-nine days standing, which is a figure about a rule and not about how many days anybody kept it. The figure on the second line of that lot book is twenty-four days out of date, and was not altered, and has nothing written beside it. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid, and the count is still five. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six.

The two empty buckets came down that bank at about half past five and went back up them full. The woman of fifty-eight read the two lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody. Nobody there said one word to her.

The man of fifty-six was the last one at that wall. He put his palm flat on those boards for a while, the way he has for ten mornings. People said good evening to each other on their way out. The word in the margin of a clerk's page was still on it under a stone in the dark, and it will be there in the morning.
