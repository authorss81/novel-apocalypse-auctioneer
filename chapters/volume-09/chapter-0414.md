# Chapter 414: The Third Time He Wrote It

The man of about nineteen wrote a figure on his own page for the third time this month and got a third thing. He was still looking at the three of them side by side when three people at the near end of that trestle table came over to see what he was doing. He told them what he was doing before they asked him.

It was the twelfth of the eleventh month and the ground had gone soft at the south end of the yard in the night. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

The man of fifty-six read the four figures off that wall at about half past nine and got all of them and said the word with them and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and twenty-two, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and thirty-eight days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and fifty-two, and three hundred and thirteen days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and sixty-three days. A figure about a figure is not a figure about the sheet, and nobody has ever entered a day-count for that sheet.

---

The figure is a figure off a list in a room four hundred yards up that bank. It was entered on the thirtieth of the tenth month with the word unchecked beside it. This morning it was thirteen days old. No person in this district owns the list it came off, and nobody has read it.

"It is the same figure," the man of about nineteen said. "I have got it three times now and I know it is the same figure, and I could not tell you which of the three I copied and which of the three I got out of my own head, and that is a different thing from getting it wrong."

"You got it right three times," somebody said.

"I know that I got it right three times because three men told me I did, and I am the only one standing there who does not count as a person to be told. Nobody asked me to do that and nobody has ever asked me to do that. I want it written down that nobody has, and I have said it out loud this morning so that if somebody says later that I was asked, there are nineteen people who heard me say it was not asked."

The clerk of nineteen years entered that the boy of about nineteen said out loud in that yard that nobody had asked him to do a thing in it. She entered that the record about the not asking says not asked, and she entered nothing else about it. About four people said that was the correct entry, and about four of them did not say anything.

"You want a fifth column," somebody said.

"There are four columns of not-askings on that page, and a clerk has refused a fifth one out loud twice. I am not asking for one. If anybody standing in that yard writes my not asking on a page somewhere today they have taken the word not out of it, and I will know, and I do not know how I will know and I will know."

---

At about eleven the boy read the four figures off that wall in the ordinary way and got all of them without the word. Then he read them again with the word and got the word four times. About nine people said that he had done the same thing on the eighth of this month, and that he had said then he was not going to make a habit of it.

"I have not made a habit of it," he said. "I have done it twice. A habit is a thing you cannot stop doing, and I have stopped doing it every single time in between. If you want to call twice a habit then call it a habit, because you are the one who has to read it and not me."

Then he read the twelve in the margin of her page, going down them, in the ordinary voice, with the word at the end of each one. He got all twelve, and two people said that the ninth one was still where his voice went.

"You have got to the ninth for a fortnight," the man of fifty-six said. "You have never got to the tenth in daylight with the wind behind you, and I have never asked you to and I am not going to."

---

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam came up that bank at about half past ten with the water to his thigh. He got the four figures at the end of that table and went back down into the water. That right arm of his stayed below the level of that shoulder the whole of the day, and nobody said one word to him about it. The road keeper came up that lane at about eleven, got the four figures off that wall as he went by, and went on up without stopping. Nobody asked him about the eleven miles.

At the far end of the yard, which is the only part of it that stays dry, the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, and has said of himself that he is fifty-one, stood from about ten until about two with a flat book under his left arm. The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale stood a few feet off him with her arms folded. She did not speak to him and was not spoken to. Somebody said out loud at the near end of that table that his six households have not moved for two hundred and four days, that the figure is out of a ladder, and that nobody has asked him for it and nobody is going to.

He was not asked. The record says he was not asked, and the record is the only thing in this district that says so, and a clerk entered the figure and did not enter anything about the man.

Her four empty houses are four figures about four houses and are not a figure about her. They are not going to be offered again before the first day of the season after this one, on her own statement, which is a thing she said out loud once in that yard in the sixth month and has not been asked to say since that day.

---

A man came up that lane at about half past twelve and stopped at the end of that trestle table. Two people saw him do it. He stood there for about a minute and looked at the two lines in that book, and then at the page under the same stone.

Nobody there had ever seen him before and nobody went over to him. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud, in a low voice, that nobody was going to go over to him. A man is a stranger until somebody standing there decides he is not, and that decision is not the stranger's to make and is not ours to make quietly either.

"Will you read it to me," the man said, from the end of that table, and he said it the way people ask for a thing they are sorry to have to ask for. "The two lines. I have got a sheet of paper at home and I cannot get anybody to write on it."

The man of about nineteen looked up off his own page. He did not say no. Nobody had asked him to, and he was not going to be asked. A thing nobody has been asked to do is not a thing a person has refused, and he was going to say that out loud because he wanted it said and not because it was a rule.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said, "Then I will read them to him myself, and nobody will say I was appointed, because I am not going to be anything, and half of you have just looked at the end of this table to see who would do it, and I would like the ones that did it to notice that they did it."

He read the two lines standing up, in the ordinary voice, from the top, without touching the book. About four people said afterwards that two lines taken in about four seconds and said out loud by a man who was not asked is a reading, and about four other people said it was not one, and the clerk of nineteen years counted what she had said on the way in and got two figures out of it and would not put either of them down. Nobody read the two lines back from the top afterwards. The man from the lane said thank you, and said that he had two children and that neither of them could read, and went down the lane.

The clerk of nineteen years entered that a man who was not asked to read two lines out loud to a stranger said out loud that nobody had asked him. She entered that the record about the not asking says not asked. She entered that the two lines were read out loud by a person who is not a job, and entered nothing else about it. She did not enter whether the man from the lane was satisfied.

---

At about one the clerk of nineteen years said one thing out loud at that table. The boy counted what she said and got a hundred and twenty, and put it down, and three people heard the count come out of his mouth.

"**That page in my coat and that page under that stone are two different instruments and neither of them is a second of anything, and the one in my coat is a thing a person can fold and put in a pocket, and the one under that stone is a thing a person who has never met me can walk up to and read, and I have been asked which of the two I would rather have been right about and I am not going to answer that, because I would rather both, and I cannot have both, and the whole of what I know about how that page got the word on it is that I wrote the word.**"

"You know how it got the word on it," somebody said. "You do not know whether the figure under it is right."

"No," the clerk said. "I do not. And the rest of you have known that since the seventh of this month and one of you said it out loud on that day and got no answer, and I am not going to pretend this morning was an answer."

---

At about three the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came back from the fence and stood at the end of that trestle table and did not say anything about the man from the lane, and three people at that table did not ask him, and the clerk of nineteen years entered that he was not asked and the record about the not asking says not asked.

"You read them to him," somebody said at last.

"I read them to him because I was standing there and he asked, and I am not a post," the mender said. "That is the whole of it, and it is not going to be the second time. If it is the second time then I am the man who reads things to people, and half of you will have said so by the end of the month, and then a man with a cloth on his hand is an instrument this district built. It is not. I did not build it and I did not want it. I read two lines in a yard in the middle of the afternoon and I am not going to do it again this month."

The road keeper came up that lane at about four and got the four figures off that wall and was not asked about the eleven miles.

---

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in that ditch until about half past four. He came up the bank wet to the chest and was not asked about anything. The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his hand-cart. The tent is still rolled on the back of it, and he was given nothing, and nobody asked him about it.

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open seventy-two days and was not run today. The ninth of the nine printed nights is two hundred and five days back, and is open, and was not closed in anything today. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and two days past. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is thirty-two days standing. The figure on the second line of that lot book is twenty-seven days out of date and was not altered. The word unchecked is on twelve figures in the margin of a clerk's page, and it is over nothing, and it is not over any of the nine columns on it. The line under those twelve words is thirteen days old and cannot be checked by anybody. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid.

The two empty buckets came down that bank at about half past five and went back up them full. The woman of fifty-eight read the two lines in that lot book on her way up, said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her.

Then the man of about nineteen folded his own page once and put it inside his coat. People said good evening to each other on their way out. The three figures he had written were the same figure, and there is no way anybody standing there at half past five could have told which of the three he copied.
