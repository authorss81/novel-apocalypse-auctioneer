# Chapter 421: A Figure In A Ditch

The woman of fifty-eight stopped at the trough with her buckets half full and asked the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds what the water had been that morning. Nine people were within four feet of her. Two of them said afterwards that nobody standing there had asked that man a single thing since the middle of the tenth month, and that the woman of fifty-eight was the one who had broken it. Neither of them was asked where that had come from.

It was the nineteenth of the eleventh month and the wind had come back round to the north-west in the night and was coming down that bank in gusts that stopped and started. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

The man of fifty-six got the four figures off that wall with his palm flat down on the boards and got all of them, and said the word with them, and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and twenty-nine, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and forty-five days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and fifty-nine, and three hundred and twenty days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and seventy days, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a figure about a sheet and that there is no day-count for the sheet.

---

He came up that bank at about ten with his hook in his right hand and the bundle under his left arm, and he came back down it inside a minute. The woman of fifty-eight was at the trough, with the trough between the two of them.

"You have got water in that," she said.

"I have," he said.

"How much."

He looked at her for about four seconds. Nobody there had asked him anything in six weeks. She had asked him about a bucket about once a month for nineteen years and had never once asked him anything else, and both of those were known at the end of that trestle table.

Then he said it in the ordinary voice, in about four seconds, and about nineteen people heard all of it. The man of about nineteen counted what he said and got seventy-five and read it back to himself in a low voice, and two people at the near end of that table heard the number come out of his mouth.

"**I have carried four thousand bucketfuls out of that ditch since the first of this month, and I have got the count in my head, and I went in up to my waist at the low end to fill them because there is no rope left on that post, and it is a figure about a man and not about a ditch, and you may have it and you may not pay me for it.**"

Nobody read that back from the top. A man who has said a thing out loud in a yard and has not had it read back from the top has not refused anything and cannot be counted either way, and the record about the not reading says not read back.

The clerk of nineteen years took her pencil out and put it back in her coat without writing anything, and two people saw her do it.

"I would like to write that down," she said.

"Then write it down," he said.

"I cannot. Not that. It is a figure about a man and I do not put those on pages, and that is not a rule of this district, that is what I am. I have never put one on and I am not going to start this month." She looked at the boards for a moment. "I have entered that a man was asked a question at the trough and answered it, and that nobody read it back from the top, and that is the whole of what I have entered and I would like somebody to notice that it is a third of it."

"Then it is a third of it," said the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, from the far end of that table, with his left hand in its cloth, "and half of you have already decided the other two thirds, and I would like to hear one of you say out loud which two thirds you decided on."

Nobody said. The wind came over the top of that bank and took the next thing anybody said, and about nine people at that table looked at the book under the stone instead of at each other.

"It is a figure about a ditch being carried out of a ditch," somebody said at last, from the middle of the yard, "and there is no rope on that post."

"It is a figure about a man carrying buckets out of a ditch," said the man who puts tables up, from the east wall, without turning round, "and the two of those are not the same figure, and he said which one it was before anybody asked him, and half of you have already started adding his count to the six and a half thousand bucketfuls on the first line of that book. If you add them you will have a figure about two months of work and it will be on nobody's page and I do not know what it will be good for."

Nobody added them. Two people at that table looked at each other over the top of the stone on that book. One of them said that the man who puts tables up was the only person there who had noticed it in about nine days, and he said he had noticed it at about the third day and had said nothing for six of them.

---

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in that ditch to his thigh at the time and he stayed in it. He said one thing from the water and it was the only time anybody standing there heard his voice raised above the ordinary that morning.

"He answered a question about water with a number about buckets," he said. "That is a worse answer than the one I gave you in October and I gave you that one standing in the same place. I said a number and a man wrote it down. He said a number and there is nowhere to write it down. I want somebody in this yard to tell me which of the two of us is worse off, and I have been in this ditch for two months and I have stopped being able to see the answer from down here."

Nobody told him. The clerk of nineteen years entered that a man asked a question there and was not answered it, and entered that the record about the not answering says not answered, and does not say refused.

At about half past ten the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing at the end of that trestle table. The man of about nineteen counted what he said and got eighty-six and put it down on his own page under a line and read it back to himself.

"**The fifth of the five things this district does not have is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and it is not paid this morning, and I have watched it not get paid four times in six months, and a man has just said a figure out loud and told you that you may not pay him for it, and that is the first time anybody in this district has said the price in the same breath as the thing.**"

Two people at that table said out loud that a price said in a yard more than once is a thing a few people know and not a price. He said that was correct, and that he was not the man who was going to find out what it took to make one of those into a price, and went back to the fence without saying it again.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds stood at the end of that table for about as long as the man of fifty-six takes to read four figures, and then went back down the bank into the water. Nobody offered him a hand and he did not ask for one, and nobody asked him to pay for the buckets and nobody was asked to pay for them.

---

At about half past eleven the man of about thirty-four who digs loam came up the bank with the water to his thigh and got the four figures at the end of that table and said the word with them four times. That right arm of his did not go above the level of that shoulder at any point in the day, and two people noticed it and neither of them said a word.

The road keeper came up that lane at about eleven and got the four figures off that wall as he went past, and stopped at the low wall and looked down into that ditch for about a minute. Nobody asked him what he was looking at and nobody asked him about the eleven miles.

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down that bank and stopped nine feet off that table with his right hand in his coat. He had heard the whole of it and he had said nothing, and about nine people had looked at him to see whether he was going to, and one of them said out loud that he was not going to because that is what he does.

At about half past one the man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up asked the clerk of nineteen years a question and she answered it in one breath. He asked her whether she had put the number in. She said no. He said he had not either, and one said that two men refusing to write a number down within a minute of each other was the most this yard had managed all month, and nobody argued with him about it.

At about two the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing put his right forearm on the boards of that trestle table and left it there. At the end of the hour it had gone dead from the elbow down to the wrist, and the hand at the end of it did not move when he told it to. Two people saw it not move and one of them said, "Your arm," and he said, "It is the table," and that was the end of it.

---

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open seventy-nine days and was not run today, and that nobody there proposed anything about closing it in a mouth or in a page. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and nine days past, and a ladder is spent as a ladder. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is thirty-nine days standing, and a man said the price of it out loud at about half past ten. A clerk entered that he said it and did not enter the price, because she was not asked to, and because a price said out loud in a yard is not a figure on a page. The figure on the second line of that lot book is thirty-four days out of date, was not altered, and has nothing written beside it. The line under those twelve words in a clerk's margin is twenty days old and cannot be checked by anybody in this district, and the word unchecked is over it and is over nothing. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid.

At about half past five the two empty buckets came down that bank and went back up them full. The woman of fifty-eight read the two lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her. Two people said good evening to her on the way up and she said good evening back, and two people said afterwards that it was the first time in about nine days that anybody at that table had said good evening to her.

Then the man of fifty-six read the four figures for the last time that day and got all four of them, and the boy of about nineteen read the twelve at the same moment at the end of that table. One said it was a coincidence and another said it was not, and nobody had a way of settling it, and the clerk wrote neither down.

At half past five the ditch at the low end had the same water in it that it had at ten in the morning. The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in it until about half past four and got out on the same step he has been getting out on for two months. Nobody standing there could have told the difference between the water at the low end and the water he was standing in, and the man who knows the answer to that is a man of about thirty-seven, and he is somewhere up that bank with a bundle under his arm and nobody has followed him.
