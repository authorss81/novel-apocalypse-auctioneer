# Chapter 426: She Has Said She Is Not Going To

A man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up began at about eleven to work out, in front of about nine people, that the woman of fifty-eight was the only person there who had ever read a thing back from the top. He got about nine words into it before somebody stopped him, and the reason he gave for stopping was that she had said out loud on the eleventh of the tenth month that she was not going to read a thing back again, and nobody had asked her to.

It was the twenty-fourth of the eleventh month and about nineteen people were in the yard of Lot Seventeen by ten.

The four figures came off that wall in the ordinary voice with the man's palm flat on the boards, and he got every one of them, and he said the word with them, and he got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and thirty-four, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and fifty days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and sixty-four, and three hundred and twenty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move this morning, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that it did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and seventy-five days, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a figure about a sheet and that the sheet has no day-count of its own.

---

He got nine words into it, and the ninth word was a name for her. No such name is on a page in this district and no one at that table uses it. Three people heard all nine words, and a man at the far end of that table put his hand flat on the boards and left it there.

"Stop there."

"I have not said anything yet," said the man who puts tables up.

"You have said enough for me to know what the nine words were going to be, and I am going to say the rest of it out loud so that about nine of you cannot pretend later that you did not know."

He said it to the table and not to the yard, and he said it standing up.

"The woman of fifty-eight read a line back on the eleventh of the tenth month. She has not been asked to do a thing since and she has said out loud that she is not going to read a thing back again. She has not said that to any one of you. She said it once, in the open, to nobody, and about four of you have been waiting nineteen days for somebody to ask her so that you would not have been the one who asked."

Nobody at that table said one word. The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in that ditch and did not come up, and the clerk of nineteen years had her page shut under her arm, and about nine people looked at the ground between the trestle table and the low wall.

"You are not a post," the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said from the far end of the same table, with his left hand in its cloth. "You are not a reader and you are not a second of anything and nobody is going to put a word over you on a page. I have watched about four of you go looking for an instrument in this yard since the first of this month and this is the first time one of you has been within about nine words of finding one, and I want the whole of this yard to notice what just happened, which is that the thing that stopped it was a man saying no, and not the woman."

"Who was it you were going to give it to," said the man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up.

"Nobody. That is why I stopped you."

"You were going to give it to her."

"I was going to say nine words," the mender said. "What you do with nine words afterwards is your business and it is not my business, and I am not going to be the man who finishes a sentence that starts a job."

The man who puts tables up stood at that table for about as long as it takes the man of fifty-six to read four figures and did not say the other words, and then went back to the east wall with his hand-cart. The tent is still rolled on the back of it, and he was given nothing, and nobody asked him about it.

---

The boy of about nineteen was at that table for the whole of it and said nothing. At about half past eleven he took his own page out of his coat and put it down on the boards and turned it over, and put his hand flat on the back of it, and then turned it back again.

Somebody said he had put it face down, and he said he had not.

"You put it face down."

"I turned it over and I turned it back again and it has got the writing on it now, and the other three times I did it in this district I left it that way for about an hour, and this is not one of those and I am not going to be counted as one of those by anybody."

The clerk of nineteen years said that she agreed with him and that she was not going to enter it either way. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said that a boy of about nineteen who knows the difference between turning a page over and putting it face down is going to be asked about that difference before the end of the week by somebody who wants an instrument. The boy said he knew, and had thought about it, and had a fortnight.

At about twelve the clerk of nineteen years said one thing out loud at the near end of that trestle table and the boy of about nineteen counted what she said and got sixty-eight and read it back to himself in a low voice, and three people heard the number.

"**Nineteen years she has asked about a bucket and nothing else, and half of you have decided in the last four days that she is an instrument this district has built, and she is not one, and a person who is thanked for a thing stops being an argument and becomes a thing the man said, and I have watched four of you do that to a book.**"

Nobody read that back from the top. The man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up said, from the east wall, "That is about me and not about the book," and the clerk said yes, and that she had said it about four of them and had not named which four, and that naming them would have been the same operation as naming her.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down that bank and took up the nine feet off that table with his right hand in his coat. A voice at the near end of that table said that a man who had not said one word about any of it for six days was standing nine feet off a table where four people had nearly made a woman into a job.

"You have been standing there since half past twelve," he said. "You came down at half past twelve and I have watched you since and you have not looked at that book once."

"No."

"It is under a stone and it is thirty-nine days out of date."

"Yes."

"Then somebody here has been holding a conversation about a woman for about an hour with a book on the end of it that neither of them has looked at."

That was the end of it. About nine people at that table said afterwards that it was the first exchange between two men here in about nine days in which one of them had not been asked a question. The clerk of nineteen years entered that two people said things at each other and entered no figure, and one of them said that was the correct entry.

At about half past one the man of about thirty-four who digs loam came up out of that bank with the water to his thigh, got the four figures at the end of that table, and said the word with them four times. That right arm of his did not go above the level of that shoulder at any point in the day and nobody said one word to him about it.

At about two the road keeper came up that lane, got the four figures off that wall as he went past, and was not asked about the eleven miles. At about half past two the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up that bank wet to the chest with the bundle under his arm and stopped at that end of the table for about four seconds. About nine people were within four feet of him and nobody said a word to him, and he went up the lane, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about a quarter past two a man came up that lane with a hand-cart and did not come into the yard and stopped at the low wall. Everybody at that table looked up. It was the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart, and he read nothing at all, not the boards and not the page and not the two lines in the book. The near wheel on that cart was still dragging, and he got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and set it down, and about nine people watched a man lift a wheel with one arm and not one of them said a word about it. He was there about a minute and a half and then he went on up the lane. The man who puts tables up said that a dragging wheel was the only figure on that lane that was the same every morning, that it was a figure about a cart and not about a road, and that he had been watching it for about eleven visits and had never once written it down.

At about three a man of about sixty-four got up off the stones at the foot of that low wall and stood for about four seconds with nothing in his hands, and then sat down on the stones again about two feet along. Nobody offered him a hand, nobody said a word about it, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given.

---

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open eighty-four days and was not run today, and that nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and fourteen days past. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is forty-four days standing. The figure on the second line of that lot book is thirty-nine days out of date, was not altered, and has nothing written beside it. A clerk entered that a woman said out loud there that a person thanked for a thing stops being an argument, and entered no name, and entered that a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud that he was not going to finish a sentence that starts a job. The word unchecked is in the margin of a clerk's page and is over nothing and is not over any of the nine columns. The line under those twelve words is twenty-five days old. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid.

At about half past five the two empty buckets came down that bank and went back up them full, and the woman of fifty-eight read the two lines in that lot book standing up on her way past, said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her. Two people said good evening to her and she said good evening back, and three people at that table looked at the man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up when she did it. He said out loud that he was not going to say what he thought, and they said that was the third time this week and that they were not going to keep looking.

Then the man of fifty-six read the four figures for the last time that day and got all four of them. The nine words at the east wall at about eleven this morning were nine words, and there are eight of them left unsaid. A woman who has carried water to eleven houses for nineteen years is not a post and is not a reader and is not a second of anything, and nobody there asked her anything today, and on the nineteenth of this month she asked a man a question and not one man asked her one, and that is the way it has been for nineteen years.
