# Chapter 425: The Reason Nothing Has Moved

A clerk of nineteen years said out loud there at about eleven that a person who has not been asked has not refused and cannot be counted either way. Three people who were standing at that table worked out, while she was saying it, that it was the reason nothing had moved in nine days, and not one of them said that out loud while she was still there.

It was the twenty-third of the eleventh month and the ground had begun to firm up after four days of it. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

The man of fifty-six said the four figures out loud with his palm flat on the boards and got all of them, and said the word with them, and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and thirty-three, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and forty-nine days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and sixty-three, and three hundred and twenty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and seventy-four days, and entered that there is no day-count for a sheet anywhere in this district and that there never has been one.

---

She said it standing at the near end of that trestle table with her page under her arm and she said it to about nine people and not to the yard, and about nine of the nineteen heard all of it and the rest were too far off. The man of about nineteen counted what she said and got eighty-six and read it back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have written that a man has not been asked for something on twenty-six days now, and I am going to write it again this morning in the same words, and I have only just worked out why nothing in this yard has moved in nine days. It has not moved because he has not been asked, and it has not moved because I have written down that he has not been asked, and I have done both of those on twenty-six days out of twenty-six.**"

Nobody read that back from the top. The clerk of nineteen years put her own page down flat on the boards of that table and did not pick it up for about an hour.

"It is both," said the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, from the other end of the same table, with his left hand in its cloth, "and you have known that since the ninth of this month, and you have said a thing four times in this yard that you worked out in four seconds this morning, and I want you to hear what I have just said and I am not going to say it again."

"I have heard it," she said. "I want to say one more thing and then I am going to stop talking until half past four."

"You said a thing out loud in this yard on the ninth of this month and said that a habit and a finding are two different things, and half of you have been repeating that sentence at me for a fortnight. It is a habit. I have said it four times and I am saying it now for the fourth time and it is worth nothing at all, and I am a clerk and I am not going to say a fifth thing in a yard for a month. That is the whole of what I have come down here for."

She went back to the far end of that table and three people followed her with their eyes. One of them said afterwards that a clerk of nineteen years had just told nine people in a yard that the only thing she had to say this morning was a thing she had already said three times, and that nobody had argued with her.

At about half past eleven the boy of about nineteen asked her a question out loud, and he did not count what she said. Three people at that table noticed that he had not counted it, and one of them asked him why. He said that on the seventeenth of this month he had said out loud there that he would not be checked on anything for a fortnight, and that he had not worked out until this morning whether counting a woman's sentence in front of nine people was being checked or was doing it, and that he was not going to work it out standing up.

"You said nothing has moved in nine days. What is the nine days."

"The fifteenth of this month to this morning, counting the fifteenth, is nine days," she said. "That is when the figure on the second line of that book became a month old and a man said out loud there that he had put it there. I have not moved anything since. Not a figure, not a column, not a page, not a chair, and not a sentence, and every one of those is a thing I could have done on any of the nine days without asking anybody."

"You could have corrected it," said the man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up, from the east wall.

"I could have written a second figure beside a first figure, which this district has a rule about, and I did not, and the reason I did not is about forty feet from me and down in a ditch and it is not on a page." She did not turn round. "I have not entered the reason and I am not going to. There is a person about forty feet from me who has not been asked for it and has not been asked for it on any of the twenty-six days I have been writing it down, and if I put a reason on a page then in a month there is a page with a reason on it and a man in this place who is a figure on it without anybody having asked him, and about four of you would call that progress."

Nobody at that table said the name of the man she meant, and the record about the not saying says not said. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud that the reason she had just given for not writing a figure down was a second thing the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds could not have refused even if he had wanted to, and three people said that was the first time anybody had said that out loud there.

---

At about twelve the man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in that ditch to his thigh and said one thing up the bank without stopping what he was doing.

"Nine days," he said. "I have been in this ditch for every one of the nine and nobody has come to the end of it and asked me a single question, and I have worked out in that time that not being asked is not a favour done to me. It is a thing that has not happened yet. About four of you are still waiting for me to be angry about it and I have decided not to be, and the clerk has just told you why, and I am going to be angry about it in about a month if I am still here, and I do not know what I will be angry about by then."

The clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a figure about days out loud in that yard, and entered that she did not enter what he was going to be angry about in a month, and that a man who has said a thing out loud has not refused anything and cannot be counted either way.

At about half past twelve the woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale came down that bank and went past that table without stopping at it, which is three times now in four days. One person said out loud that two is a figure and that three is a habit, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said out loud that the difference between a figure and a habit was a sentence and not a rule. Nobody argued with him.

---

At about one the man the figure of twenty-one years is against came down that bank and stood nine feet off that table with his right hand in his coat and said nothing out loud. Two people at that table looked at each other, and one of them said that he had now said nothing in that yard for four days running, and the other one said it was five. Then one of them said that it was a market of one thing in a room with no roof, and that every person in it had said so at some point since the first of this month. The man the figure of twenty-one years is against said, "That is the first use of that word I have heard in six months and it was used correctly."

At about half past one the man of about thirty-seven who puts tables up asked the man of about nineteen a question out loud, and did not get an answer straight away, and the boy counted nothing.

"You have written that not-asked down twenty-six times since the twenty-ninth of the last month. Why have you not written down what it was you were not asking him for?"

The boy did not answer for about as long as the man of fifty-six takes to read four figures. Then he said that the clerk's page had four columns of not-askings and five headed columns, and that the columns were empty, and that an empty column is a question that has been asked and not answered. If he wrote down what he was not asking for then the column would have a thing in it, and the thing would be a figure, and it would look like a refusal, and a person who has not been asked has not refused.

Nobody read that back from the top. The clerk of nineteen years said out loud, in front of about nine people, that a column of a thing with a word over it is not a column of people. She said she had heard a boy of about nineteen give the reason for that better than she could give it, and that she was not going to put his reason on a page because he had not asked her to, and that the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

At about half past two the road keeper came up that lane, got the four figures off that wall as he went past, and stopped at the low wall and looked down into that ditch for about two minutes. Nobody asked him what he was looking at, and nobody asked him about the eleven miles. At about three the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up that lane and went up it and did not stop, and the near wheel on that cart was still dragging. He got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and set it down, and about nine people watched him and not one of them said a word about it.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was in that ditch until about half past four. He came up the bank wet to the chest with the bundle under his arm and stopped at the end of that trestle table for about four seconds, and about nine people were within four feet of him, and nobody said a word to him, and he went up the lane. The record about the not asking says not asked. He was not offered an arm and he did not ask for one.

The man who puts tables up for anybody who will use them was against the east wall with his hand-cart, and the tent is still rolled on the back of it, and he was given nothing, and nobody asked him about it. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall from about ten with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody there offered him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given.

---

A clerk of nineteen years entered that the bid has stood open eighty-three days and was not run today, and that nobody proposed anything about closing it. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and thirteen days past. The rule said out loud in that yard on the tenth of the tenth month is forty-three days standing. The figure on the second line of that lot book is thirty-eight days out of date, was not altered, and has nothing written beside it. A clerk entered that a clerk of nineteen years said out loud in that yard that a person who has not been asked has not refused and cannot be counted either way, and entered the reason she gave out loud for not writing a figure beside the first one, and entered no figure. The word unchecked is in the margin of a clerk's page and is over nothing. The line under those twelve words is twenty-four days old. Nothing went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and it was empty at about six. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid.

At about half past five the woman of fifty-eight went down that bank with two empty buckets and came back up it with them full, and read the two lines in that lot book standing up on her way past. She said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her. Then the man of fifty-six read the four figures for the last time that day and got all four of them, and the boy of about nineteen read the twelve off the margin of her page going down them and got all twelve. Three people at that table said the two of them had not done that at the same moment since the eighteenth of this month, and the clerk wrote down neither the coincidence nor the not-coincidence.

Nine days, the clerk named there at about eleven this morning, and not ten, and she counted the fifteenth as the first of them. The figure on the second line of that book is on the face of a book in the open and is thirty-eight days out of date, and nine days after the day it became a month old, and nothing about that has changed.
