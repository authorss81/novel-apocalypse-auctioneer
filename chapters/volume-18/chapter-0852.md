# Chapter 852: The One Hundred And Thirty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Page Came Down That Lane Yesterday And Was Still In A Woman's Hand At The Foot Of That Bank And Was Read Out Loud At The Near End Of The Second Table At About Eleven This Morning In About Nineteen People's Hearing And A Clerk Entered Nothing And Gave No Reason And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and ninety-one days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and fifty. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and sixty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and eighty days. Seven hundred and forty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and fifty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and sixty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and thirty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and thirty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and sixty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and ninety-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a page came down that lane yesterday and is in a woman's hand at the foot of that bank this morning, and it was read out loud at the near end of the second table at about eleven in about nineteen people's hearing and nothing was entered, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and sixty-six mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and sixty-five. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and sixteen mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and thirty-six, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and seventy-seventh of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a woman read the three lines on that page out loud at the near end of the second table at about eleven this morning to about nineteen people, and a clerk entered nothing and gave no reason.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-first night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-first afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the second time in nine years, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood fifty-one days, and a page came down that lane yesterday and it is in a woman's hand at the foot of that bank, and it was read out loud at those boards this morning and it is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said that it did not move this morning because nobody came down that lane with a page in their own hand, and he said no figure at all after that.

---

The page was still in Sabra Holt's hand at the foot of that bank at about a quarter to eight, and the young man was sitting on the flat stone about nine feet off the near end of the low wall with his coat buttoned to the throat, and nobody had gone down the eleven feet to look at it in about nineteen hours.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman who has carried a page for a night and a morning has it in her hand the way a person holds a thing they have not decided to put down or pick up, and that about four of them looked at her hand from the top of the bank and then looked away, and that none of them has been able to say afterwards what she was supposed to do with it.

---

At about ten to eight Orren Pike put his sheet flat on the boards and said the one thing he had come to say.

"The lane count is twenty-eight and it did not move," he said, "and it did not move because nobody came down that lane this morning with a page in their own hand, and it moved yesterday at about a quarter to ten because a young man came down it with one and Sabra Holt read it at the foot of that bank. That is the whole of the rule and I have had it for four years and it is not mine and it does not go up."

---

Nobody said anything at the boards.

---

Mavis Dorr had both hands flat on the boards of the second table at about half past ten and about nine people at the top of eleven feet were standing about four feet off her and about four of them had come up out of the yard before she got there.

"I want to say the thing that has been sitting in me since yesterday morning," she said, "and I am going to say it and then I am going to stand here while about nine people say it back at me in whatever way they want to. That thing is that a yard has been worse at not reading a thing than it is at reading a thing."

---

"Yesterday a page came down that lane with three questions on it and about nine people heard all three of them read out at the foot of that bank, and I have been thinking about it since about ten to five yesterday afternoon, and what I have been thinking about it is that the three lines were about nine feet from a man's head and the man was not asked, and that is not the part that is going to come back on us. The part that is going to come back on us is that a piece of paper is sitting eleven feet below this yard in a woman's hand and about four people in this yard have read over it and about nine have not, and that on the day it goes back up that hill it is going up either because somebody in this yard knows what is on it or because nobody in this yard bothered to find out, and I know which one of those two I would rather be able to answer for."

---

Sabra Holt came up about four feet of that bank at about a quarter to eleven and did not go to the boards.

"I will read it," she said. "I read it yesterday and I will read it again this morning, and I am telling you now, at about a quarter to eleven, at about four feet off those boards, that I am not going to read it because if I read it then a page a room sent down nine miles of hill has been read out in a yard by a woman of fifty-two with nineteen years and the third of the nine oldest keys, and at the top of that hill there is a room, and what that room has at about nine this morning is not three questions and one page. It is a woman who read them."

---

"Then a clerk reads it," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"A clerk reads it," said Sabra Holt, "and I would like about nine people at the top of eleven feet to notice that that is better and not better, and that I am not going to say why."

---

Mara Quill read it out loud at about eleven.

She stood at the near end of the second table with the lot book shut in front of her and the page in her own hand, and she read the three lines in the flat voice of a woman who has read a list out in front of people for fifteen years, and about nineteen people in that yard heard all three of them.

"That district is to state the name of the person who asked. That district is to state the morning on which he asked. That district is to state the answer he was given."

---

Nobody said anything at about four feet off the boards.

---

And then about four people at the top of eleven feet said nothing out loud but about four of them had all looked at the same thing at the same moment, which was the column in the lot book with the heading over it, because the heading says *whoever* and what it says it is for is whoever read a thing out loud, and a woman had just read a thing out loud at those boards in about nineteen people's hearing.

---

"I am not going to write my name in there," said Mara Quill.

She said it before anybody said anything about it, and she said it in the same flat voice, and she did not open the book.

"The heading says whoever and the one name in that column does not satisfy the rule and the second space on that form is still empty, and if I put my own name in there this morning then the rule has been satisfied by a woman who read a piece of paper out loud, and about four people at the top of eleven feet would have to spend the rest of the week working out what that means, and it does not mean what it looks like it means, and I am not going to be the first name in there for something that is not that."

---

"Nobody in this yard is going to be the first name in there for anything," said Mavis Dorr, "and I would like about nine people at the top of eleven feet to hear me say that as a plain fact and not as a complaint, because that is where we are, and it is where we have been since the one hundred and twenty-first morning, and a piece of paper came down that lane yesterday and it has made the same place look bigger."

---

Adrian Vale was at about four feet off the near end of the first table and he had not come to the second table this morning.

He had been about nine feet from the boards when the reading started, and he turned his back on it before the first line was finished and stood facing the first table with his hands behind his back, and about four people at the top of eleven feet watched a man of thirty-eight turn his back on a room's question being read out in a yard, and about four of them have said since that he did it without being asked by anybody and that he did not look at anybody while he did it.

---

"I have been in that room," he said, at about ten past eleven, to about four people and not to the boards, when it was over. "I went up that lane with a question in my hand when I was younger than I am now and I brought down a question in my hand and it came back as something else, and I said out loud on the one hundred and thirty-third morning that I am not going to be the second man in this basin to carry a thing out of a yard and have it come back as something else. I am not standing at four feet off those boards while a page comes out of a room and goes into a yard, because that is a man in a yard with a room's question in his mouth, and I will not be that again."

---

The turner said one thing about the hill and said it before anybody asked him anything.

"I said yesterday at the foot of that bank that I am not going to be the hand that carries it up," he said, "and nobody asked me and nobody has asked me this morning either, and I would like about nine people at the top of eleven feet to notice that the second time was easier than the first time because the first time I had to say it out loud and the second time I did not."

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-first night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-first afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said out loud that a thing can be read out loud in a yard without becoming a thing anybody in the yard has to answer, and that the reading is not the taking, and that he has watched people read things out loud for seventeen years and the taking was never the reading, and a man of about sixty-four said good afternoon and nothing else.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the twenty-fifth time running, and about four of the nineteen had been standing at about four feet off the near end of the second table at about eleven this morning while three lines were read out loud.

---

At about ten to five the light went off the boards and about nineteen people were within about four hundred yards of them.

The trestle was still standing at the near end of the second table and it had been standing two mornings now, and the man of about thirty-two had not come up the bank for it. The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book. The column carries one name and the heading over it still says whoever and the one name does not satisfy the rule and the second space on that form is still empty. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid. Four hundred and eleven is on a sheet at a gatepost and nobody in this basin has computed by how much it is out. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move, because nobody came down that lane this morning with a page in their own hand, and the toll is ninety-eight and it is spent, and the ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five, and a man of thirty-eight is standing at about four feet off the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back and has not been told he is a good man and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that three lines were read out loud at those boards at about eleven on the one hundred and thirty-sixth morning and that about four of them have been saying all afternoon that it made it worse, and that they could not tell anybody what worse meant, and that the reading has put the three lines into about nineteen people's heads where yesterday they were in about nine people's, and that nobody in that yard has said out loud that this is why a page should not have been read.
