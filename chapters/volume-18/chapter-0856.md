# Chapter 856: The One Hundred And Fortieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Young Man Who Came Down That Lane With A Page In His Own Hand Sat At The Foot Of That Bank This Afternoon And Asked Out Loud In The Ordinary Voice How Long This District Has Got And Nobody In That Yard Gave Him A Figure And The Man Who Keeps A Tally Said Out Loud That He Could Have Given Him One And Was Not Going To And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and ninety-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and fifty-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and seventy days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and eighty-four days. Seven hundred and forty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and fifty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and sixty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and thirty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and thirty-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and seventy-three days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and thirteen marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and ninety-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a young man at the foot of that bank asked out loud this afternoon how long this district has got, and nobody in that yard gave him a figure, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and seventy mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and sixty-nine. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and twenty mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and forty, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and eighty-first of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a young man at the foot of that bank asked out loud at about one this afternoon how long this district has got and nobody in that yard gave him a figure, and the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said out loud that he could have given him one and was not going to.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-fifth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety-four of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-fifth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the second time in nine years, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood fifty-five days, and a page came down that lane and a young man asked at the foot of that bank this afternoon how long this district has got and nobody in that yard gave him a figure, and a page with no figure on it is not a refusal, and the page is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and then said a figure, and he said the figure before the clerk's hand had moved.

---

"One hundred and sixty-one," he said, at the near end of the first table.

About four people said it after him at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet who were standing about four feet off him did not say it after him this morning, and about four of them have said since that they did not say it because they were listening to somebody at the bottom of that bank and could not hear the boy and would not ask him to say it again.

---

The trestle was standing about four feet along from the near end of the second table at about ten, where the man of about thirty-two puts it, and he was in the yard at about ten and he put it there and did not say one word about the four mornings it had been standing at the near end.

About four people at the top of eleven feet looked at it in its own place and about nine of them did not say one word about it, and about four of them have said since that a yard that could not put a name on a piece of paper on the one hundred and thirty-eighth morning had put a trestle back in its own place in about half a second on the one hundred and thirty-ninth, and that about four of them could not say what the difference was and have not gone looking.

---

The turner came up the eleven feet at about ten past nine and went back down at about twenty past ten and stood at the foot of that bank for the rest of the afternoon, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who said on the one hundred and thirty-fifth morning that he would not be the hand that carries it up had been standing eleven feet below that page every afternoon since, and that about four of them have said that they do not know what a man does when he stands near a thing he has refused to move.

---

At about one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said the number into the middle of that yard and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down, and the young man was about nine feet off the near end of the low wall with the page in his own hand across his knees, and he heard it.

About four people at the top of eleven feet were at the boards and about four of them looked down the eleven feet at the same moment and about four of them have said since that the whole of the one hundred and fortieth afternoon turned on the fact that a man says a figure out loud in a yard every afternoon and a young man heard it.

---

"How long has this district got," said the young man.

He said it at about one in the ordinary voice, to the man of about forty-eight, and he did not raise it, and he did not put it to anybody else.

---

The man of about forty-eight looked at him and about four people at the foot of that bank did not make a sound.

"I could give you that," he said. "I want you to hear me say that I could give you that, because it has been in this yard every afternoon since before the middle of the winter and I have never once been asked for it by anybody and I never thought anybody was going to be."

---

"I am not going to give it to you," he said, "and I am not going to tell you that it is not mine to give, because I do not know that. What I am going to tell you is that the figure I said at about one this afternoon is a figure of work, and that a figure of days is a different thing, and that I have not got one, and that nobody in this yard has got one, and that if one of us had one then a room at the top of nine miles of hill would have it too and I would have heard about that before I heard about a page."

---

"Nobody has got one," said Mavis Dorr, from about nine feet further along the wall, and she said it out loud and not quietly, and about four people at the foot of that bank heard her say a thing they had all been thinking about for about two days.

---

And then the young man said the other thing, and he said it to Sabra Holt and not to the yard.

"The third line is the only one of the three I am sure about," he said, "and I have read it coming down that lane and I have read it about nine times since and it says the same thing both times, and I cannot tell you which of two things it wants, and I have been not able to tell you since about half past nine this morning and I have not said anything about it because I did not think it was mine to say anything about."

---

"Which two things," said Sabra Holt.

---

"Whether it wants the words a man says," said the young man, "or whether it wants to know that a man says them."

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a young man read one line of a page about nine times on the one hundred and fortieth morning and could not tell which of two questions it was, and that about nine people in that yard have read the same three lines since the one hundred and thirty-sixth morning, and that about four of them have not been sure of that line either and have not said so, and that nobody at the top of nine miles of hill will ever be able to tell which question a yard meant, because a question on a piece of paper has to be read out loud by somebody and the somebody is different every time.

---

Sabra Holt held the page out and did not open it.

"You have just said the thing about that page that I have been not saying for about two days," she said, "and I would like about nine people at the top of eleven feet to notice that a young man who has been in this yard for about four days has said it before a woman of fifty-two with nineteen years, and that I am not going to be bitter about it, and that I am not going to thank him for it either."

---

Mavis Dorr said the thing about the refusal at about half past two and she said it out loud at the foot of that bank with about four people standing there.

"Listen to what we are about to do," she said. "We are going to hand that young man a page with nothing on it, and he is going to take it up nine miles of hill, and a room is going to open a piece of paper and find nothing written on it, and nobody in that room will be able to say that this district said no, because we have not said no. We have not said no because saying no would want a line on it, and a line wants a name on it, and a name in this yard is the yard, and the yard is about nineteen people, and about four of them have said that out loud this week at about four feet off those boards."

---

"So there is going to be nothing on it," she said, "and I would like about four people at the foot of this bank to hear me say that I know that, and that I am not asking anybody to stop it, and that I am not going to be the one to stop it, because stopping it would be the first line."

---

Nobody at the foot of that bank said anything.

---

"I will take it up tomorrow," said the turner, at about half past two, to about four people and not to the yard, "if the yard wants a page with nothing on it in a room by morning, and I want it noticed that I said that before anybody asked me."

"You are not taking it up," said Sabra Holt. "He is to take it up. You said on the one hundred and thirty-fifth morning that you are not going to be the hand that carries it up that hill and you are not going to change your mind at about half past two on the one hundred and fortieth because the yard is out of ideas."

---

"I am not changing my mind," said the turner. "I am offering. There is a difference and about four people at the top of eleven feet will not be able to tell what it is and I am not going to explain it."

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-fifth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety-four of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-fifth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the twenty-ninth time running, and about four feet away from him on a flat stone a young man was sitting with a piece of paper across his knees and heard all four figures and the sentence and did not write any of it down.

---

At about ten to five the light went off the boards and the young man stood up off the flat stone with the page and said that he would be there in the morning and that he was not going to be there for ever, and that there was other work on that hill and he was not the only one who carried it.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a page with nothing on it is not a refusal, and that about four of them got to the same place on their own on the one hundred and fortieth afternoon, for the first time, on the back of somebody in that yard saying out loud that there was going to be nothing on it, and that about four of them have been going on with their work since and have not said one word about it to anybody.

The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book. One name is in that column and whoever is still the heading over it, and it does not satisfy the rule, and the second space on that form has been empty all morning. Six months is the clerk's count this morning and there is no seventh of it anywhere. Fourteen is what this district has made and there is nothing else on that list. Five is what this district does not have and not one of them has been paid for. Four hundred and eleven is on a sheet at a gatepost and by how much it is out nobody in this basin has worked out. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move, because this morning nobody came down that lane with a page in their own hand, and the toll is ninety-eight and it is spent, and the ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five, and a man of thirty-eight is standing at about four feet off the near end of the first table and has not been told he is a good man and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three.
