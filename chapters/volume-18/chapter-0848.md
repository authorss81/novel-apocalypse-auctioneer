# Chapter 848: The One Hundred And Thirty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of About Forty-Eight Asked The Clerk A Question Out Loud At The Near End Of The Second Table At About Ten To Eight Before He Had Said His Number And She Answered It And Said Out Loud That She Is Not Going To Be Asked To Keep A Thing That Has No Column And Then He Said The Number At About One And It Was The Other Way Round From Every Morning Of This Run Of Mornings

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and eighty-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and forty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and sixty-two days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and seventy-six days. Seven hundred and thirty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and fifty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and fifty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and twenty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and twenty-nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and sixty-five days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and five marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and ninety-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man of about forty-eight asked the clerk a question out loud at the near end of the second table at about ten to eight, before he had said his number, which is a thing he has not done, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and sixty-two mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and sixty-one. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and twelve mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and thirty-two, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-seven and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and seventy-third of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and the clerk answered it out loud and said that she is not going to be asked to keep a thing that has no column, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard both halves of it and about four of them have said since that the second half was the answer and not the first.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and eighty-seventh night of that run, having slept on four hundred and eighty-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the seventy-seventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a woman who keeps a scale came up that bank and said out loud that there is no hour of the asking that anybody in that yard agreed on, and a man of about sixty-four said good afternoon and nothing else, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood forty-seven days, and a man of about forty-eight said the number into the middle of that yard at about one after he had asked a question, which is the other way round from every morning of this run of mornings, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

Orren Pike said no figure out loud at all on the one hundred and thirty-second morning and a man of about forty-eight asked the clerk a question at the near end of the second table before he said his number.

---

The yard did what the yard does. Orren Pike was at the near end of the first table at about ten to eight with his sheet against his chest and he did not say a figure, and about four people at the top of eleven feet noticed, and about four of them have said since that the boy has said a figure out loud at the same hour every morning for four years and that on the one hundred and thirty-second morning he said nothing and that nobody asked him why, and that about four of them wanted to and did not.

---

At about ten to eight a man of about forty-eight asked the clerk a question.

He had not said his number. He had his hands behind his back and he came the length of the yard and stopped at the near end of the second table, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet turned round, and Mara Quill had the lot book open and her hand on the corner of the page at the back of it.

"Are you going to be asked to keep it," he said.

---

About nine people at the top of eleven feet did not make a sound.

---

"Keep what," said the clerk.

---

"The asking," said the man of about forty-eight. "I have not got a word for it and I am not going to invent one at ten to eight. I mean the thing that has happened at these boards on about four mornings this week, and I mean the four questions, and I mean the woman with the beam, and I mean the nine people standing at your table yesterday at about half past one with nothing to do. I am asking you, out loud, at these boards, at ten to eight in the morning, before I have said my number, whether anybody in this district is going to ask you to keep it."

---

The clerk took her hand off the page at the back of that book and put it flat on the boards, which she has done twice in about five mornings, and about four people at the top of eleven feet watched her do it.

"No," she said. "And I am not going to be asked to keep a thing that has no column."

---

"Will you say the rest of it," said Mavis Dorr, "because that is the half that matters."

---

"There is no rest of it," said Mara Quill. "That is the whole sentence and I have said it in the order it has to be said in. I am not going to be asked, and I could not keep it if I were, and the second of those two is the one I have been clerk of this book for fifteen years for."

---

"Then what happens to it," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"Nothing," said the clerk. "It goes out of that book and about four hundred yards of air and it does not come back, and in about four years there is a page in this basin or there is not, and I am not going to be the one who makes it, and I have said that out loud at about ten to eight in the morning in front of about nine people because a man asked me a question in front of about nine people and that is the only reason a person gets to hear a thing like that said in a yard."

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the clerk said *nothing* the way a person says a word they have put down in front of themselves and looked at, and that the man who drives the cart said afterwards that he had not heard a woman in that yard say *nothing* like that in his life, and that about four other people at the top of eleven feet said the same thing in four different words before the light changed on her coat.

---

"He asked you before he said his number," said Mavis Dorr, at about twenty past ten, to about nine people at the top of eleven feet. "I want it noticed. That man has said that number at about one every morning of this run of mornings and about four of us are standing here waiting for it, and this morning he came and asked a question at about ten to eight and he has still not said it, and I do not think he is going to say it at about one today, and I want about four people to have noticed that before it happens."

---

He said it at about one.

He said it into the middle of that yard in the ordinary voice and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard it and about four of them have said since that they had been waiting for about three hours to find out whether a man who had asked something could still say a number in the same voice, and that he could, and that about four of them found that harder to sit through than the asking.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came up that bank at about ten to two with the beam in one hand and about nine people at the top of eleven feet looked at her, and she did not put the beam down on the boards this time. She stood about four feet off the near end of that table and she held it.

"There is no hour of the asking that anybody in that yard agreed on," she said. "A boy of seventeen has said that two o'clock is when the boards are free and a man who drives a cart has said that two o'clock is when the boards are free and a man who digs loam asked a question at about half past ten on the one hundred and twenty-ninth morning and was answered, and a man of about forty-eight asked a question at about ten to eight this morning, and none of those four hours was fixed by anybody before the hour arrived, and none of those four people had ever asked each other."

She turned the beam a quarter of the way in her hands.

"I asked my question at ten to two on the one hundred and twenty-seventh morning because that is when I had finished trying to have a different one, and I would have asked it at about two the morning before if I had thought of it, and I would have asked it at about ten to eight this morning if I had thought of it then, and I did not, and I am not going to be told by anybody in that yard what hour it was supposed to be."

---

"That is the second time you have come up that bank and said the same thing," said the turner, from about nine feet off.

"I know it is," said the woman who keeps a scale. "I came up on the one hundred and twenty-seventh and asked a question and went down. I have come up on the one hundred and thirty-first and the one hundred and thirty-second and said two other things and gone down both times, and the three of us in that bank are the only three people in this yard who have come up it in about nine days, and I have not worked out what the three of us are doing and I have stopped trying this morning."

---

Sabra Holt had been in that yard since about ten and had said nothing until about half past two.

"I have an office," she said. "I have had it for nineteen years and it is the third of the nine oldest keys and nobody in this district has used it this week, and I have been in this yard for five mornings and not one person in it has asked me one question, and I am not going to stand here and be praised for that and I am not going to be sorry about it either. I would like about four people at the top of eleven feet to notice that there are nineteen years and a key in that sentence and that I said them out loud at about half past two today, and that I have not said them out loud in about four years, and that the reason I have said them today is that a man of about forty-eight asked a clerk a question at ten to eight and she said no before he had finished, and that I have never in nineteen years seen anybody in this district do that before this morning, and that I have not decided yet whether it was because of him or in spite of him."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nine people heard it for the twenty-first time running, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the one name in that column does not satisfy the rule, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid.
