# Chapter 872: The One Hundred And Fifty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month At About Ten The Turner Put His Thumb On The Chalk Along The Edge Of That Second Table And Found Out That A Clerk Rubs It Out At The End Of A Month And Nobody Asked Him Why He Had Done That And The Lane Count Is Twenty-Eight And Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and eleven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and seventy. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and eighty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred days. Seven hundred and sixty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and seventy-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and eighty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and fifty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and fifty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and eighty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and twenty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and fifteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and the turner put his thumb on the chalk along the edge of that second table at about ten and found out that a clerk rubs it out at the end of a month, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and eighty-six mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and eighty-five. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and thirty-six mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and fifty-six, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and ninety-seventh of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and the turner put his thumb on the chalk along the edge of that second table and found out what a clerk does with it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and eleventh night of that run, having slept on five hundred and ten of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and first afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the twelfth morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood seventy-one days, and a man put his thumb on chalk that gets rubbed out at the end of a month, and the page is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and then said a figure, and he said the figure before the clerk's hand had moved.

---

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is twenty-eight and did not move this morning, and then he said a figure, and about four people said it after him at the same moment and out of step with each other.

"One hundred and seventy-seven," he said, at the near end of the first table.

---

About nine people at the top of eleven feet said it after him too, and the clerk did not look up from the boards, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that this is the fourth morning running he has said a figure and that on the two mornings between them he said nothing at all, and that about four of them have stopped asking him why.

At about ten the turner put his thumb on the chalk along the edge of that second table, right down at the level of the wood, and dragged it about nine inches along the edge, and about four people at the top of eleven feet watched a man's hand do that and did not know what he was doing.

---

"Who does that," he said.

---

The clerk came about four feet along the second table and looked at the smudge he had made and then looked at the marks beyond it and then at him.

"I do," she said. "At the end of a month I put my sleeve flat on that edge and I go along it and it comes off in a grey roll and the wood is bare under it and then somebody puts the tables back and there is nothing there to see. It has been going on for longer than I have been at that book and it will be going on after I have not."

---

"You count the month," said the turner.

---

And about nine people at the top of eleven feet stopped what they were doing, because that was the first time in this block anybody had put the two of them in the same mouth.

"I do not count a month," said the clerk. "I rub a thing out because it is on the wood and the wood is used. Counting a month is a thing a person does when the month comes round and wants to know how many have been. I have told about nine people at the top of eleven feet that I was not going to do that and I am not doing it this morning, and if you want to know what is on the wood under my sleeve at the end of a month, that is a different question and you can ask it and I will answer it, and it is not the question you just asked me."

---

Nobody asked it.

Then a woman of about fifty with a barrow came along the rail and said, to about nine people at the top of eleven feet and not to anybody in particular, that a thing on that edge would last until the end of a month, and that is longer than nothing, and that about four of the five things this district does not have have not lasted as long as that.

---

"Chalk," said the man of about thirty-two, who puts the tables up, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has just seen.

And then four of the five places named yesterday at about ten in about nineteen people's hearing were still refused and a sixth one had come up out of the wood in about the time it takes the light to come along the second table and reach the far end of it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood quite still.

---

The clerk sat down on the near end of the first table, which she had not done in about nine years of mornings, and looked at the smudge the turner had made with his thumb.

"You want to know whether the chalk is a place," she said, "and I am going to tell you what chalk in this yard is, and then you can decide. It is the only part of that table anybody has ever written on. There are four hundred and fifteen marks on that edge this morning and about four people at the top of eleven feet counted them yesterday while the light was low and not one of them said what they had got and the marks are still there. And every single one of them goes off at the end of a month in a sleeve."

---

She put her thumb on the smudge herself and looked at it.

"So that is what a place in chalk in this yard is," she said. "It is a place where a thing gets put down and then gets taken off by a person who is not asked, and about four people at the top of eleven feet will come down in the morning and look at the bare wood and not one of them will be able to tell whether anything was ever written there or whether they are imagining it. You have not found a place for this. You have found the opposite of a place. A thing in chalk in this yard is a thing that gets entered and then gets un-entered by whoever comes back with a sleeve."

---

The turner looked at the smudge he had made for a while.

"That is the first thing anybody in this yard has said to me this block that I did not have an answer ready for," he said, "and I put my thumb there myself, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who fitted a rail this morning put his hand on four hundred and fifteen marks that a clerk is going to rub out at the end of a month and neither of them has said a word to the other about it."

---

At about eleven the man at the end of the second table said one thing out loud, without getting up and without moving his forearms off the boards, and about nine people heard it and not one of them answered him.

"Yesterday this yard refused five places," he said. "This morning it has found a sixth and refused that too, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a yard which has found six places in two mornings is not looking for a place. It is looking for an excuse to keep looking. I have been at this table fifteen years and I am not going to say one word about which of the two of those is true, and I am not going to say a word about myself either, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet know that by now and about four of them found it out on the one hundred and fifty-second morning."

---

Nobody answered him.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet have said since that he said the second half of that on purpose and out loud, in about nine people's hearing, and that about four of them have said that a man of about thirty-seven who has been at that table for fifteen years has now twice put a rule on his own mouth in front of witnesses, and that about four of them have said they would rather he had not.

---

Adrian Vale was at about four feet off the near end of the first table all morning and did not go down the eleven feet once, and at about half past twelve he picked up the stub of chalk that the turner had left on the boards and turned it over in his fingers and put it back down where he had found it.

"I have not written anything with that in nine years," he said, to about four people, "and I am not going to start this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have noticed that the reason I gave is that I have not used it and not that I have not got anything to write."

---

At about one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into the middle of that yard and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that he has been saying that number into about four hundred yards of air for longer than any of them have been standing at the foot of that bank, and that nobody has ever come down that lane for it, and that he has never once asked for it to be written down.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and eleventh night of that run, having slept on five hundred and ten of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and first afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet who were watching that wall could see that nothing was being said down there.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the forty-fifth time running.

Nobody asked him what any of them is for, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the turner had a hand in his pocket all through the second half of this morning and that about four of them noticed and about four of them did not ask him, and that about four of them have said that a man who has just been told by a clerk what his own thumb did ought to be the first person in this yard to ask that man of fifty-six something, and that he did not, and that about four of them found that harder than the chalk.

---

It went off the boards at about half past four. About nineteen people were at the foot of that bank and about four of them said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in this yard, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said nothing at all, and that was not the same as not being there.

The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines, and not one of the thirty-three is in the lot book and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and that count is not going to be lower. The heading over the one name in that column is whoever, that name does not satisfy the rule, and the space under it on that form has got nothing in it at all. Six months is what a clerk counted this morning and a clerk rubbed the chalk out at the end of a month this morning and the two of those are not the same thing and she has said so out loud. Fourteen is what this district has made and it did not move. Five is what this district does not have and the fifth of the five has not been paid for and is not going to be paid for by anybody rubbing anything. A sheet at that gatepost carries four hundred and eleven, and by how much it is out is not a thing anybody in this basin has done. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move, because that lane has not had a page come down it in anybody's own hand since the one hundred and thirty-fifth morning. The toll is ninety-eight and it is spent. The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five. The bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot. A man of thirty-eight picked up a stub of chalk this morning and put it back where he found it and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three and has not been told he is a good man.
