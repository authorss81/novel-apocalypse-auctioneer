# Chapter 851: The One Hundred And Thirty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Young Man Came Down That Lane At About A Quarter To Ten With A Page Folded Once In His Own Hand And A Woman With Fifty-Two Years And The Third Of The Nine Oldest Keys Read It Standing Up At The Foot Of That Bank And Did Not Carry It Up The Hill And Mavis Dorr Said Out Loud In About Nine People's Hearing That It Is Not The Room's Question And The Lane Count Moved To Twenty-Eight At The Boards Before The Clerk's Hand Had Moved And Nothing Was Entered Anywhere

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and ninety days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and forty-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and sixty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and seventy-nine days. Seven hundred and forty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and fifty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and fifty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and twenty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and thirty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and sixty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and ninety-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a young man came down that lane at about a quarter to ten this morning with a page folded once in his own hand, and the lane count moved from twenty-seven to twenty-eight, and it moved because somebody came down that lane with a page in their own hand, and it did not move for anybody going up that hill, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and sixty-five mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and sixty-four. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and fifteen mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and thirty-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and moved this morning from twenty-seven. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and seventy-sixth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a young man came down that lane with a page in his own hand and Sabra Holt read it at the foot of that bank and did not carry it up the hill, and about nine people heard what she read and nobody answered any of it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninetieth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and eighty-nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eightieth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the second time in nine years, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood fifty days, and a page came down that lane in a young man's own hand and it was read at the foot of that bank and it was not entered in the lot book and it was not entered on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about half past ten and said it before the clerk's hand had moved and before he said any figure at all.

---

At about a quarter to ten a young man came down that lane.

He came down it on foot, the way the lane is walked, and he had a page folded once in his own hand and he held it against his coat with his thumb, and he stopped about nine feet off at the foot of that bank and did not come any nearer to anybody in that yard.

Nobody had seen him on that lane. About nine people at the top of eleven feet had been looking at the boards and the lot book and the loam for the best part of an hour, and the lane comes down behind the second table and behind the bank, and a person coming down it is not a person you see until they are at the bottom.

---

The man of fifty-six was on his stone with his hands loose at his sides. Sabra Holt was about nine feet off the near end of the low wall with her hands behind her back. Mavis Dorr was about four feet off that, and the turner was about nine feet off Mavis Dorr with a hand out flat at nothing in particular, and the man who digs loam had his spade down at about four feet further on, and about four others were standing about four feet apart along the foot of that wall the way they stand when there is nothing to do at that hour.

---

"It came down with the early thing," said the young man. "It is for this district and it is not mine. I have to take it back up and I am not to leave it anywhere else in the yard."

---

Sabra Holt did not go to him for it.

She stood where she was and put her hands out in front of her and waited there, and about four people at the foot of that bank have said since that a woman with nineteen years behind her and the third of the nine oldest keys did not walk nine feet towards a piece of paper in front of about nine people, and that she waited for the young man to bring it to her, and that he did, and that neither of the two of them said one word about the nine feet.

---

He put it into her hand.

The page was folded once and there was a heading across the top of it in the shape a room nine miles up that hill puts on the top of a thing, and under the heading were three lines and the writing was not a clerk's hand, and about four people at the foot of that bank could see that there were three lines and could not read them from where they were standing and did not try.

---

Sabra Holt read it out loud, standing up, in the ordinary voice, at about a quarter to ten at the foot of that bank.

"That district is to state," she said, "the name of the person who asked."

---

Nobody said anything at the foot of that bank.

---

"That district is to state," she said, "the morning on which he asked."

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet had come down about four feet of the bank by then and were standing on the slope with their hands at their sides looking down at the top of a woman's head, and the man of fifty-six did not turn round and was not asked anything, and it was about nine feet from where Sabra Holt was standing.

---

"That district is to state," she said, "the answer he was given."

---

She folded the page back the way it had been folded and put it down at her side in her own hand.

About nine people at the foot of that bank had heard all three of those lines and not one of them had answered any of them, and about four of them have said since that they heard three sentences about nine feet from a man's head and understood about two of them, and that the one they understood best was the third one, and that they could not have said afterwards why.

---

"That is not the room's question."

Mavis Dorr said it from about four feet off the near end of the low wall, and she said it at the volume she says a thing at when she wants the foot of a bank and not the top of it, and about four hundred yards of air in that yard carried it as far as the slope and no further.

"It came down that lane in the room's hand," she said, "and it is the yard's now, and I am going to say that once this morning and about nine people at the foot of that bank have heard it, and I am not going to be asked whether I have a right to say it, and if anybody asks me that I am going to stand here and say it again."

---

"Go on, then," said the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, from about nine feet further along the wall, "and tell me what the yard does with it."

---

"I am not going to tell you what the yard does with it," said Mavis Dorr, "because I do not know, and if I did know then I would have to say it out loud at those boards, and there is nowhere to put a thing said out loud at those boards, and I have spent about nine mornings at about four feet off those boards finding that out."

---

The turner, who had come up the eleven feet at about nine and gone back down at about ten past nine and had been standing at the foot of that bank since, said the one thing he had come down for.

"I am not going to carry it up," he said. "I said as sent at those boards on the one hundred and twenty-fourth morning for the first time in nine years and nobody turned round, and I have been up nine miles of hill twice this week refusing to say a thing, and I am not going to be the hand that carries a room's question up that hill and brings a room's answer down it. That is the whole of what I came down for and I would like about nine people at the foot of this bank to notice that nobody asked me."

---

"Nobody asked you," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"I am not going to be asked," said the turner, "and that is not a complaint, and I would like it written down that I said so, and I know there is nowhere to write it, and I am not going to ask for it."

---

Then Sabra Holt said the thing about the hill, and she said it to the young man and not to the yard, and the young man stood about nine feet off and took it.

"I am not going to carry this up that hill," she said. "There is a lane and a page and a hand, and if I put this page in my hand and walk up nine miles of hill with it, then at the top of that hill there is a woman of fifty-two who carried an answer up, and nobody in this yard decided that anybody should carry anything up, and I am not going to have it be me by carrying something."

"You could hand it to the young man," said the turner, "and he is going up it anyway."

"He is to take it back up," said Sabra Holt, "and he is to take back up whatever is on it when he goes, and I am not going to decide what is on it, and I am not going to decide what is not on it either."

---

At about half past ten Orren Pike put his sheet flat on the boards of the second table and said three things, and Mara Quill had her hand on the corner of the page at the back of that book and it did not move while he said any of them.

"Nobody has come down that lane this morning with a page," he said, "and I know it before anybody at these boards tells me because I have watched that lane for four years and I did not see anybody on it, and the lane count is twenty-eight, and it moved this morning because a page comes down that lane in a person's own hand and one came down at about a quarter to ten in a young man's own hand and Sabra Holt read it at the foot of that bank."

---

"It did not move for the turner," he said. "He went down the eleven feet at about ten past nine and he is not carrying a page and he did not have one, and the count moves for a page coming down and not for a person going up, and nobody in this yard has to be told that twice because I have had it for four years and it is not mine."

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet said *moved* after him, out of step with each other, and the figure is a count of pages and not a figure of money and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard the difference and none of them said one word about it.

---

"One hundred and fifty-six," said the boy, at the near end of the first table, at about ten past eleven, and he said it before Mara Quill's hand had moved.

He said it in the ordinary voice and about four people said it after him at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about four of them have said since that they do not know why a morning of a man of thirty-eight is said in a yard the way a figure is said, and that nobody has worked out what to do about that and nobody is going to.

---

Mara Quill took her hand off the corner of the page at the back of that book and put it flat on the boards on either side of the lot book, and she said one sentence, and she gave no reason.

"There is nothing on that page that I can enter," she said, "and I am not going to enter any of it, and I am a clerk and not a person who finds out, and I have said that out loud in this yard before."

---

"Would there be," said Mavis Dorr, "if it had a column."

"There is a column," said Mara Quill, "and the heading over it says whoever, and there is one name in it and the one name does not satisfy the rule and the second space on that form is empty and it is going to be empty at about half past four this afternoon, and if I put a name in that column this morning then the name in it would be the yard's, and I am not going to write the yard down."

---

The young man was still at the foot of that bank at about one.

He had not gone up. He sat down on the flat stone about nine feet off the near end of the low wall with the page in his hand across his knees, and he said that he was not to leave it anywhere else in the yard and that he was going to be there when the light went off the boards, and that he was not in a hurry about anything else.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet had been standing at those boards since about half past one and about four of them had come down the bank at some point in the afternoon and gone back up again, and nobody had asked the young man one question about the three lines, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a young man sat eleven feet below a yard for half a day with a piece of paper on his knees and not one person in that yard walked down and read it over his shoulder.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the twenty-fourth time running, and about four of the nineteen had spent the morning about nine feet from the back of a man's head listening to three sentences about a name and a morning and an answer.

Nobody asked him anything that afternoon. The last person in that yard to ask him a question had been a woman with a beam at about ten to two on the one hundred and twenty-seventh morning.

---

At about ten to five the light went off the boards and about nine people were within about four hundred yards of them and about nine of them had come early and about four of them put their hands in their pockets one at a time and said they had to go.

Sabra Holt was at about four feet off the near end of the second table with the page in her own hand and she did not put it in the lot book and she did not put it on the page at the back of that book and she did not read it out loud again. The trestle was still standing at the near end of that table and the man of about thirty-two had not come up the bank for it. The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book and not one of the thirty-three is about a page that came down a lane. A clerk has counted six months this morning and that is the whole of it. This district has made fourteen things and that is the whole of that too. The count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid. Four hundred and eleven is on a sheet at a gatepost and nobody in this basin has computed by how much it is out. The lane count is twenty-eight and it moved this morning, and the only reason it moved is that somebody came down that hill with a piece of paper in their own hand, and a piece of paper is not a person and a lane does not know the difference, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a yard that has been asking out loud for about nine days had a count move on it this morning for the first time since anybody could remember, and that what moved it was not the asking.

The young man stood up off the flat stone at about ten to five with the page in his hand and started up the lane, and nobody in that yard said anything to him about the three lines, and about four feet of stone and about eleven feet of bank and about four hundred yards of air carried him up out of that yard and carried nothing else with him.
