# Chapter 843: The One Hundred And Twenty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Who Has Been Weighing Things At The Foot Of That Bank For Nine Years Came Up About Four Feet Of That Bank And Asked The Man At The Foot Of That Low Wall What The Four Figures On That Wall Are For And He Gave Her The Answer He Gives At About Four Every Morning And A Boy Of Seventeen Said A Morning Out Loud At Those Boards Before The Clerk's Hand Had Moved And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and eighty-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and forty-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and fifty-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and seventy-one days. Seven hundred and thirty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and forty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and fifty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and twenty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and twenty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and sixty days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and eighty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a person who has been weighing things at the foot of that bank for nine years came up about four feet of that bank and asked a question at the near end of that second table at about two in the afternoon, and it was the same question, and the clerk wrote nothing, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and fifty-seven mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and fifty-six. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and seven mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and twenty-seven, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-seven and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and sixty-eighth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a question was asked at those boards at about two in the afternoon in about nineteen people's hearing and it was the same question, and the answer was the answer he gives at about four, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not make a sound.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and eighty-second night of that run, having slept on four hundred and eighty-one of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the seventy-second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the answer came up off that wall about four hundred yards and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it at the boards, and a man of sixty-one heard it from underneath that wall about four feet off the man who said it and said good afternoon and nothing else, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood forty-two days, and a question was asked at those boards this afternoon and it was not the asking of a man of thirty-eight, and the column has one name in it and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

Orren Pike put his sheet flat on the boards of the second table at about ten to eight and said three things in a row, and the clerk's hand was on the corner of the page at the back of that book and it did not move while he said any of them.

"Nobody has come down that lane this morning," he said. "The lane count is twenty-seven and it did not move, and it did not move because a page comes down that lane in a person's own hand and the last time anybody came down that lane with a page in their own hand was the one hundred and sixteenth morning, and that is the rule and I have had it for four years and it is not mine."

"The one hundred and twenty-seventh morning," he said.

He said it in the ordinary voice and about four people at the top of eleven feet said *morning* after him, out of step with each other, and the figure is a morning and not a figure of money and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard the difference and none of them said one word about it.

---

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said the number into the middle of that yard at about one and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down, and the clerk wrote nothing at all this morning before that, which is the ordinary way.

Sabra Holt was in that yard from about ten and stood about four feet in from the boards and did not go to them. The turner came up the eleven feet at about nine and had not said *as sent*.

---

At about ten to two the woman who keeps a scale came up about four feet of the bank.

She carried the beam up in one hand, which she has not done since the morning she carried it up in both hands, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a woman with a beam come up a bank at ten to two in the afternoon.

She got to the near end of the second table and she put the beam down across the boards with both hands flat on it, one on each side of where the lot book was, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stopped what they were doing.

Mara Quill took her hand off the page at the back of that book and put it in her lap.

---

"I am going to say why first," said the woman who keeps a scale, "and then I am going to ask it, and the order is that way round on purpose, and I have never done it in that order in my life and I have thought about it since about eleven."

She did not look at anybody while she said it. She looked at the boards.

"I have watched that figure every morning since the seventy-sixth. I have watched it from about forty feet off and I have never been wrong about it and I have never once been right about it either, because I do not know what it is for, and I have said in this yard that I do not know, and I have been weighing things at the foot of that bank for nine years and that is the longest thing I have ever watched."

She put one finger on the boards.

"And I have watched this yard ask one question for thirteen mornings. One. A man of thirty-eight asked it on the one hundred and twelfth morning and nobody has asked it since, and I have gone at that every morning waiting to see a second one come out of somebody, because a yard with one question in it is a yard with one man in it, and I have watched a yard with one man in it for nine years from about forty feet off and I know what it looks like and I do not want to look at it again."

She took her finger off the boards.

"And I have not got a second question of my own. I have had about four days of trying and I have got nothing, and this morning at about eleven I stopped trying, and what I have stopped trying for is the only question anybody in this district has asked out loud in thirteen mornings, and it is mine this afternoon because there is no other one. I would like about nine people at the top of eleven feet to hear me say that it is not a good question and that I am asking it anyway and that I have said out loud that I have not got a better one."

---

Nobody at the top of eleven feet said anything.

---

She turned round and looked down about eleven feet.

"What are the four figures on that wall for," said the woman who keeps a scale.

She said it in the ordinary voice, at the near end of the second table, and about nineteen people heard it, and she did not soften it and she did not put anything in front of it, and about four feet of stone and about eleven feet of bank and about four hundred yards of air carried it down to where a man was sitting with his hands loose at his sides on a flat stone at the foot of a low wall.

---

The man of fifty-six did not get up and did not come up the bank.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they were waiting for him to get up, and that he has not come up that bank in about fourteen mornings and that the whole of this yard had been built on the understanding that he would not.

He put one hand flat on the stone.

"I do not know what any of them is for," he said.

He said it in the ordinary voice, and he said it at the speed he says it at about four, and he said it flat, and then he took his hand off the stone and he did not say one word more, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have listened to that sentence at about four every morning for fourteen mornings and that this afternoon it came up the bank at ten to two instead, and that they did not get a second thing out of it and were not going to.

---

Nobody at the top of eleven feet made a sound.

---

Mavis Dorr had both hands flat on the boards of the first table and about nine people at the top of eleven feet saw her get them there, and about four of them have said since that she was going to say something and that the clerk's hand came off the page at the back of that book and went into Mara Quill's lap about as long afterwards, and that the two of them were about four feet apart and neither of them said anything.

---

"That is not fair," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"It is that exactly," said the woman who keeps a scale, without turning round, and she did not raise her voice, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard her say the second half of that sentence at the same volume as the first half, and about four of them have said since that it was the first time in about nine years they had heard a person in that yard refuse something and be right about it in the same breath.

---

Adrian Vale was at about four feet off the near end of the first table and he watched a woman he had watched from about forty feet off for about nine years put a beam down on the boards of the second table and ask a question he had asked on the one hundred and twelfth morning, and the question had gone out of the yard on the one hundred and twelfth morning with his name nowhere on it, and it had come back on the one hundred and twenty-seventh morning with a beam laid across the boards and a woman saying out loud that she had no better one, and the thing he had carried up a hill in his mouth and had not been allowed to say at those boards was nine words long and it was not about the wall, and he understood for the first time in about nineteen mornings that he had never once wanted the wall, and that he had been standing in that yard for a hundred and forty-eight mornings as a man who was going to be asked about a wall, and that this afternoon the wall had been taken off him by a person with a beam who had not looked at him once.

He did not say one word. He had a shape in that yard at about half past two on the one hundred and twenty-second morning and he did not use it.

---

The woman who keeps a scale picked the beam up off the boards and stood with it against her hip and about nine people at the top of eleven feet waited for her to say something else, and she did not, and about four of them have said since that they had braced for a second thing and that there was not going to be one and that a person who has been watching a yard for nine years knows by about half past two whether a yard has anything else in it.

---

"Thank you," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"Do not thank me at those boards," said the woman who keeps a scale. "I have been at the foot of that bank for nine years and I have not been thanked at those boards once in nine years and I am not going to start, and besides I have not done anything. A woman with a beam asked a man a question that he answers every morning at about four. That is the whole of what happened at about ten to two this afternoon."

---

The man at the end of the second table had not moved for the whole of it and had his forearms on the boards about four feet off the lot book, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked at him when the answer came up the bank and about four of them looked at him when the woman picked the beam up, and about nine of them did not ask him anything at all, and he made no note of it on the side of his own hand, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who has made a note on the side of his own hand on two mornings of the last three made no note of this one, and that none of them has worked out what that means and that none of them is going to ask.

---

"Nobody is going to ask him a second one," said the turner, out loud, from about nine feet off the near end of that second table, to about nine people at the top of eleven feet, and about four of them looked at him and about four of them did not. "I want to be able to say in about four years that there was one question in that yard on the one hundred and twenty-seventh morning and that nobody put a second one to him, and I am saying it now, at about half past two, in front of about nine people, because a room nine miles up that hill has been asking this district a question every morning for about fourteen mornings and if in about four years somebody in that room asks whether this district asks things twice then the answer is going to be whatever the nine people at the top of eleven feet say it is between now and then."

Nobody answered him.

"That is not a rule," said the turner. "I have been in this yard nine years and I know what a rule sounds like. If anybody in that yard wants to ask him something at about ten past two tomorrow then the whole of what I have just said is worth about as much as the trestle standing at the end of that table."

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and eighty-second night of that run, having slept on four hundred and eighty-one of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the seventy-second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said nothing else, and the man of about sixty-four had been asked one question in that yard in his life, on the one hundred and twentieth morning, and he was not asked one this afternoon, and about four people at the top of eleven feet who were watching that wall at about half past three could see that nothing was being said down there, and about four of them have said since that the whole of the one hundred and twenty-seventh afternoon came up eleven feet and the man at the bottom of it was the only person in that yard it did not reach.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the sixteenth time running, and about four of them heard the sentence twice in one day for the first time since the afternoon of the one hundred and twelfth morning, and about four of them have said since that the two times were the same sentence at the same speed and that they have not been able to get anything out of the difference and are not going to try.

Mara Quill had the page at the back of that lot book open on her knees and she did not write on it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked at it and about nine of them did not ask her about it, and it carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book, and the column carries one name and the heading over it still says whoever and the second space on that form is still empty, and the turner went back down the eleven feet at about half past four without saying one word about what he had said at about half past two, and the woman who keeps a scale went down the four feet she had come up with the beam in one hand, and nobody in that yard has decided anything this afternoon except a man of about thirty-two, who took a trestle down at about ten to two, which is the hour he has taken it down at for nine years.
