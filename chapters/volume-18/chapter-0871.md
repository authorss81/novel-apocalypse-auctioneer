# Chapter 871: The One Hundred And Fifty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month At About Ten About Nine People At The Top Of Eleven Feet Named Five Places To Put The Asking In And Four Of The Five Were Refused By Something Standing In Front Of Them And The Lane Count Is Twenty-Eight And Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and ten days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and sixty-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and eighty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and ninety-nine days. Seven hundred and sixty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and seventy-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and seventy-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and forty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and fifty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and eighty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and twenty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and fourteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet named five places this morning to put the asking in and four of the five were refused by something standing in front of them, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and eighty-five mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and eighty-four. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and thirty-five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and fifty-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and ninety-sixth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet named five places this morning and four of the five were refused.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and tenth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundredth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the eleventh morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood seventy days, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet named five places this morning and four of the five were refused by something standing in front of them, and the page is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said no figure this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet noticed that before the light had finished coming across the boards.

---

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about four people said it too from further along. At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is twenty-eight and did not move this morning, and then he stopped, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked at the boards instead of at him.

---

At about ten Mavis Dorr put both hands flat on the first table and left them there.

"I have been standing at about four feet off the near end of this table for five mornings," she said, "and every one of those five mornings somebody in that yard has said a thing out loud that could not be written down, and not one of us has asked the question that is sitting in front of us, which is where does it go. Not what it means. Where it goes."

"You are asking for a box," said the man who digs loam.

---

"I am asking for a place," said Mavis Dorr. "A box is a thing you make. A place is a thing that is there. About nine people at the top of eleven feet have been putting this yard's asking on a rail and on a wall and in the air for five mornings, and about four of them have found out by now that a place has to have been a place before anything is put on it."

Then she said them out loud, the five of them, one at a time, and she said where she had got them from, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood and listened to all five.

"The boards," she said. "The rail. The low wall at the foot of this bank. The lane. And the back of the lot book."

---

"That is five," said Sabra Holt.

"That is five, and I have put them in the open at about ten this morning on purpose, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have noticed that I am doing it on purpose, and the reason is that if a yard finds a place quietly then in about four days one person finds it quietly and it looks like an idea."

---

The clerk said the first one, standing up, with the lot book open at a page with nothing ruled on it.

"The boards are mine and the book on them is a Council's," she said. "I have said that here before and I am not going to say it twice, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard me say it the first time. You cannot put a yard on a board that belongs to a book. There is a form in that book. It has five lines and one of the five has got a name in it and the heading over that space says whoever. I am not going to put about nineteen people into a form with a space on it for one person. That is the whole of what I am going to say about the boards this morning."

---

The turner took it next, standing with his back to the rail.

"I fitted that rail," he said, "and nobody in this yard is to thank me for it and I am not going to be thanked for it this morning. What a rail is for is leaning on. A man leans on it at about half past four and then he goes up the eleven feet and goes home. If you put a thing on that rail, then in the morning there is a thing on it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet will come down and look at it, and not one of them will know whether it is the same thing as yesterday's or a different thing that looks the same. A rail is a good place to be tired and it is not a place to be sure."

---

Sabra Holt took the low wall, and she took it in the ordinary voice, and about nine people heard her.

"The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning," she said. "Sixteen people gave five and three gave six and the sum came to ninety-eight and that toll is spent, and it may not be raised and it may not be paid twice, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet know all of that. And there is no saying in this district that points at a question. I have been the third of the nine oldest keys for nineteen years and the only thing that key has ever been able to do is stop things going up that hill, and I stopped four, and I am not going to stand here this morning and find a fifth."

---

Adrian Vale took the lane, standing still at about four feet off the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back.

"I went up that lane with nothing in my hand on the one hundred and fifty-fourth afternoon and I said so out loud before I went, so that nobody in that room on the top of that hill could be told I came up it anyway," he said. "That lane does not hold anything. It carries things in one direction and only in one direction and only in the hand of the person who is carrying it. Put a thing on it and you have not found a place for it. You have found a courier, and you have not asked anybody whether they would carry it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have got to the same place on their own, which is that a courier is a person who can say no."

---

He stopped there, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard the whole of it, and about four of them noticed that he had put a reason on the fourth refusal when nobody had asked him for one.

---

Then the fifth one was left, and it sat in the middle of that yard for about as long as the light takes to come off the boards and go along them and find the far side of the second table. The clerk did not want to take it and she took it.

---

"That page at the back of that book has been carrying thirty-three lines for longer than I have been a clerk in this district," she said, "and three of the thirty-three are lines nobody in this district has ever heard read, and that count is not going to be lower, and not one of the thirty-three is in the lot book and not one of the thirty-three is about a question. You want me to write a thirty-fourth on it. I am not going to. And I am not going to tell you why, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard me say on the one hundred and fifty-first morning that I had already said why once."

Then the man of about thirty-two, who puts the tables up, said the thing that made the rest of the morning go the way it went.

---

"That is five," he said. "She named five and the yard refused five, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have noticed that the fifth one was refused by the woman who offered it. So that is not a yard that has run out of places. That is a yard that has run out of reasons to think a place is what is wanted."

---

Nobody answered him, and the woman who keeps a scale weighed a bar of iron at the foot of that bank through the whole of it and said nothing at all to anybody.

Adrian Vale said one more thing at about eleven, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard it and about nine people heard it from further along.

---

"I have been carrying nine words since the one hundred and second morning," he said, "and I am not going to say them, and I am not going to put them on any of the five places she named, and I would like it noticed that I have said that out loud at about eleven this morning before anybody asked me anything about it. About four people at the top of eleven feet have been waiting five mornings for somebody to make that offer and about four of them did not think it was going to come out of a man of thirty-eight."

---

At about one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into the middle of that yard and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and tenth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundredth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet who were watching that wall could see that nothing was being said down there.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the forty-fourth time running.

---

Nobody asked him what any of them is for, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that this is the first morning in this block that a question was put into the open at about ten and got five refusals, and that nobody carried any of it down to that wall at about four to see whether the wall would take it, and that about four of them found the hardest part of five refusals to be that there is then nothing left to do at about four.

---

The light went off those boards at about half past four, and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in this yard went out of about four mouths at the top of eleven feet and about nine people were still standing at the foot of that bank when it had gone.

The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book. Whoever is the heading over the one name in that column, that name does not satisfy the rule, and the second space on that form is empty and has been empty since the one hundred and thirty-fifth morning. Six months is what a clerk counted this morning and she did not count a seventh and is not going to. Fourteen is what this district has made and the fourteen did not move this morning. Five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is as unpaid as the other four. A sheet at a gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and by how much it is out is not a thing anybody in this basin has done. The lane count is twenty-eight and it did not move, because nobody came down that lane this morning with a page in their own hand. The toll is ninety-eight and it is spent. The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five. The bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot. A man of thirty-eight has said out loud that he is not putting nine words on any of five places and has not been told he is a good man and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three.
