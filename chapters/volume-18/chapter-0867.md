# Chapter 867: The One Hundred And Fifty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Clerk Read A Room's Page Out Loud At Those Boards At About A Quarter To Ten And Then Did Not Read The Rest Of It And Entered Nothing And Said Nothing About Noticing And Nothing Was Entered And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and six days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and sixty-five. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and eighty-one days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and ninety-five days. Seven hundred and fifty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and seventy days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and seventy-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and forty-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and forty-eight days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and eighty-four days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and twenty-four marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and ten marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a clerk read a room's page out loud at those boards this morning and then did not read the rest of it, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and eighty-one mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and eighty. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and thirty-one mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and fifty-one, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and ninety-second of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a clerk read a room's page out loud at those boards this morning and did not read the rest of it and said nothing about that.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and five of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the ninety-sixth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the seventh morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood sixty-six days, and a page went back up that lane with nothing written on it, and a clerk stopped reading halfway through a page this morning and entered nothing, and the page is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

---

The clerk is thirty-four and has been a clerk for fifteen years and she reads a room's page out loud at those boards on a morning the way she has done about four hundred times, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood about four feet off her this morning to hear it.

---

"The name of the person who asked is the man of thirty-eight," she said, in the ordinary voice. "The morning on which he asked it is the one hundred and twelfth. The answer he was given was that nobody in this yard knows."

---

She stopped, with her finger on the line.

---

The boards were the boards and about nine people at the top of eleven feet were waiting, and about four of them have said since that a woman who has read about four hundred pages out loud at those boards did not finish this one, and that about four of them have said that what stopped her was not the line and that anybody who has been in this yard for four days could see that.

"Does that go in," said the man who drives the cart.

---

"It does not go in," said the clerk. "It has not gone in since the one hundred and thirty-sixth morning and it is not going to go in this morning either, and there is nothing I have to say about that, because I said all of it at those boards on the one hundred and thirty-sixth morning and about nine people heard me say it."

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet said, out of step with each other, "You did."

---

"I did," said the clerk, "and I have said it twice and I am not going to say it a third time, and about four people at the top of eleven feet are going to go on asking me about it every morning until one of us gets tired and it will not be me."

Nobody asked her again at about ten.

---

About nine people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they noticed something and that they could not say what they had noticed, and that about four of them have said the noticing and not the thing, and that about four of them have said that a clerk who enters nothing is the easiest person in this district to stand next to and that on the one hundred and fifty-first morning she was not.

---

Sabra Holt read the rest of it herself at about half past ten, out loud, in the ordinary voice, standing at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and about nine people heard her and she entered nothing.

"That is a room's page," she said, "and a clerk has read a clerk's copy of it and stopped, and I have read the whole of it, and I am not going to say one word about the stopping, and about four people at the top of eleven feet are going to want me to and I would like them to notice that I am not going to."

---

Then nothing happened for about three hours and everybody in that yard found something to do.

---

The man of about thirty-two put his trestle about four feet along from the near end of the second table and it went there and stayed. The man who made the stone worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table and about nine people at the top of eleven feet walked about four feet to look at what he was doing and then walked back. The man of about twenty-nine who drives the cart stood his cart on public lot and about four people watched him not get asked about it.

---

The clerk turned the lot book over about half past twelve and looked at the page at the back of it for a while with her thumb holding her place in the lot book itself, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet could see her doing it and about four of them have said since that thirty-three lines is what has been on that page since before she was a clerk here, and that three of them are lines nobody in this district heard read, and that about four of them have said that a clerk who is the only person in this yard who is paid to keep this book looked at a page in it about half past twelve on the one hundred and fifty-first morning and then shut it.

She shut it.

---

"Not this morning," she said, to about nine people, and about four of them said why not, and she said, "Because there is nothing on it and if I start at the top of it somebody in this yard will stand at that bank and watch me get to the bottom of it and I am not doing a thing like that in front of about nine people at about half past twelve on a morning when I have already stopped reading half of one page out loud."

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the thirty-third line on that page is a line about a door, and that a clerk shut the book rather than let anybody see her get to it, and that about four of them have said that neither of those things is going to be on any page in this district.

---

At about one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said the number into the middle of that yard and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down and about four people said it after him out of step with each other.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and five of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the ninety-sixth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet were watching that wall at about half past three and could see that nothing was being said down there.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the fortieth time running.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the clerk was at those boards when he said it and that she did not look up, and that about four of them have said that a woman who has read about four hundred pages out loud and entered three lines has spent the morning not looking up, and that about four of them have said they do not know what that is either and have not said what it is.

The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book. Whoever is the heading over the one name in that column, and that name does not satisfy the rule, and the second space on that form is empty and has been empty since the one hundred and thirty-fifth morning. Six months is what a clerk counted this morning and it is the whole of her count. Fourteen is what this district has made. Five is what this district does not have and not one of the five has been paid for. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost and by how much it is out nobody in this yard has ever worked out. The lane count is twenty-eight and it did not move, because nobody came down that lane this morning carrying a page of their own. The toll is ninety-eight and it is spent. The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five. The bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot. A man of thirty-eight is standing at the near end of the first table and has not been told he is a good man and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three.
