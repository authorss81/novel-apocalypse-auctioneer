# Chapter 873: The One Hundred And Fifty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Page Came Down That Lane At About A Quarter Past Eight In The Own Hand Of A Man Of About Twenty-One And The Lane Count Moved From Twenty-Eight To Twenty-Nine And A Clerk Was Asked To Carry The Asking And Declined Out Loud And Gave No Reason And The Lane Count Is Twenty-Nine And Has Not Moved Since

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and twelve days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and seventy-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and eighty-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and one days. Seven hundred and sixty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and seventy-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and eighty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and fifty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and fifty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and ninety days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and thirty marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and sixteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a page came down that lane at about a quarter past eight in the own hand of a man of about twenty-one and a clerk was asked to carry the asking and declined out loud and gave no reason, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and eighty-seven mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and eighty-six. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and thirty-seven mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and fifty-seven, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-nine and moved this morning from twenty-eight. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and ninety-eighth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a page came down that lane this morning in the own hand of a man of about twenty-one and a clerk was asked to carry the asking and said no out loud.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and twelfth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and eleven of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the thirteenth morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood seventy-two days, and a page came down that lane this morning in somebody's own hand and a clerk was asked to carry the asking and said no, and the page is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said no figure this morning, and he said out loud why the lane count moved before he said a figure, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

---

At about a quarter past eight a man of about twenty-one came down that lane with a page in his own hand.

He had it in his right hand and he was holding it the way a man holds a thing he has not folded yet, with two corners sticking up past his knuckles, and he came the last of the lane at a walk and not at a run, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet were on that bank and about four of them were still on the slope, and all of them watched the bottom of that lane before they looked at the boards.

---

He put it on the first table at about nine feet off the near end, squared it up with his thumb, and stepped back.

"It came out of that room at about six this morning," he said. "It is for the yard."

---

Then he went back up the lane, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet looked at him go, and about four of them have said since that he went back up it with nothing in his hand, and that a man going up that lane with nothing in his hand is not a page coming down it, and that the count in that yard does not move for him and never has.

Nobody touched it for about a quarter of an hour.

---

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, and he said the reason out loud before he said a figure, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard every word of it.

"Twenty-nine," he said. "It was twenty-eight yesterday and it is twenty-nine this morning and it moved because a page came down that lane in a man's own hand at about a quarter past eight and I saw it come down. It does not move for anybody going up that hill. It has not moved for a man going up it with nothing in his hand and it does not move for me. I am not saying a figure this morning."

---

About four people said the number after him out of step with each other and about nine people said it after him too, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a boy of seventeen who has been putting a number in the air before a clerk's hand moves for four years said the number and then told the yard why it was a different number, and that about four of them found that a figure with a reason attached is a different shape from a figure without one.

The clerk turned the page over once, and then again, and then she opened the lot book and put it beside the open book and stood looking at the two of them.

---

"There is a line ruled across the top of it," she said, "and four words on that line in somebody else's hand, and there is nothing else on it. I am going to read the four words out loud because about nine people at the top of eleven feet have not got down there yet and because I have read a room's page out loud at these boards before and that is what I do."

She read it out loud, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people heard it.

---

"Whoever reads it out loud," she said.

Nobody said anything for a while.

---

The clerk looked at the page and then at the lot book and then at the page again, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that she went from one to the other twice in the ordinary way of a person checking whether two things are the same thing, and that they are the same thing, and that there are two books nine miles apart with a heading over one space on each of them that says whoever.

Sabra Holt said the asking at about a quarter past nine, and she said it to about nine people at the top of eleven feet, and she said it in the ordinary voice, and she asked the clerk to carry it.

---

"There is a form," she said, "about nine miles up that hill, with a space on it, and about nineteen people at the foot of this bank have a thing they have been saying out loud for five mornings that will not go down anywhere. I am asking you to carry it. That is all I am doing. I have not asked you why anything has gone wrong in this yard for five mornings and I am not going to start this morning."

And about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard a clerk say no.

---

"No," said the clerk.

And she gave no reason, and she said it once, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard the whole of the no.

---

Sabra Holt did not move.

"You have done that once," she said.

---

"I did it once," said the clerk, "at these boards, on the one hundred and fifty-first morning. I stopped halfway through a page out loud in front of about nine people and I said nothing about stopping, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have been waiting five mornings for somebody to ask me about it and you have now asked me twice and got the same answer both times, and I am not going to be told what the answer is for. I have said both halves of that in one breath and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have heard it."

About nine people at the top of eleven feet said, out of step with each other, "You did."

---

"I did," said the clerk, "and about four of them have said so twice now and I am not going to say it a third time for anybody in this yard."

Then two people at once began to say something on her behalf, which is what about four people at the top of eleven feet have been waiting five mornings to see, and she put her hand flat on the boards, and they stopped.

---

"I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to go back to the boards," she said. "Nobody in this yard has said anything on my behalf this morning except you two, and I would have said the same no, and about four people at the top of eleven feet should take that as the answer and not as an argument. That is all. There is nothing else. I said no and I did not give a reason and I am not going to give one now."

Mavis Dorr asked for a name at about ten, and she asked for it in about nine people's hearing, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said afterwards that she asked it on purpose.

---

"Put a name in the second space," she said, "and then there is a whoever on that page and the clerk carries a name and not a yard, and it goes up that hill and there is a name at both ends of it. That is how a thing gets carried. That is what carrying is."

"Not this morning," said the clerk. "I am not going to ask anybody in this yard for a name and I am not going to let anybody in this yard ask me for one, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have just heard me say that, and if somebody in this yard wants to put a name on that page this morning they can put it on with their own hand and it will not be me and it will not be because I asked."

---

Nobody asked for a name, and nobody put one on, and the second space on that form is still empty and the heading over it still says whoever.

Adrian Vale was at about four feet off the near end of the first table and had not gone near the page.

"I am not going to put my name on it," he said, to about four people, "and I want it noticed that nobody asked me. I put a question down at those boards on the one hundred and twelfth morning and it is not in that book and about nine people at the top of eleven feet know what it is. If my name goes on a page that says whoever then whoever becomes one man with a question, and that is the end of it, and it took this yard a hundred and twelve mornings to get to that and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that he said the end of it out loud before anybody had said it was coming."

---

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into the middle of that yard at about one and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down, and the page sat on the first table from about nine until the light began to go off it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked at it about nine times between about ten and about four.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and twelfth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and eleven of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet who were watching that wall could see that nothing was being said down there, and a page was lying nine feet from the boards all afternoon and it did not change what anybody in that yard could see from the top of that bank.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the forty-sixth time running.

Nobody asked him what any of them is for, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a page came down that lane this morning and a clerk said no out loud and not one question went out of this yard up that hill, and that the lane count went up by one and the number of things that have been carried up that hill did not go up at all, and that about four of them have said those two sentences out loud this afternoon to about nine people and nobody picked either of them up.

---

By about half past four the light had come off those boards and gone down the eleven feet behind them, and about nineteen people were on the ground at the foot of that bank and about four of them said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in this yard, and the rest of it stood there.

Thirty-three lines are on the page at the back of that book and not one of them is in the lot book, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the clerk turned that page over once at about ten this morning and looked at it and that nobody in this yard saw what was on it. Whoever is the heading over the one name in that column, that name does not satisfy the rule, and the second space on that form is empty and it was empty at about a quarter past nine this morning and it is empty now. Six months is what a clerk counted and she did not count a seventh. Fourteen is what this district has made. Five is what this district does not have and not one of the five has been paid for. A sheet at a gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and by how much it is out is nobody in this basin's arithmetic. The lane count is twenty-nine and it did not move for anybody going up that hill this morning and it did not move again for a woman who said no. The toll is ninety-eight and it is spent. The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five. The bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot. A man of thirty-eight has said out loud that his name is not going on a page that says whoever and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three and has not been told he is a good man.
