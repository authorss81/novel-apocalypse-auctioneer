# Chapter 863: The One Hundred And Forty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman With Nineteen Years Asked A Boy Of Seventeen At About Eleven This Morning To Put Down How Many People Heard A Man Say Two Words At The Foot Of That Wall And He Said No Out Loud And She Did Not Ask Him Again And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and sixty-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and seventy-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and ninety-one days. Seven hundred and fifty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and sixty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and seventy-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and forty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and forty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and eighty days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and twenty marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a boy of seventeen said no out loud at those boards at about eleven this morning and nobody asked him again, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and seventy-seven mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and seventy-six. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and twenty-seven mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and forty-seven, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and eighty-eighth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a boy of seventeen said no out loud at those boards at about eleven this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him say it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and second night of that run, having slept on five hundred and one of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the ninety-second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the third morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood sixty-two days, and a page went back up that lane with nothing written on it, and a boy of seventeen was asked at those boards this morning to put down a number and would not, and the page is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and then said a figure, and he said the figure before the clerk's hand had moved.

---

"One hundred and sixty-eight," he said, at the near end of the first table.

About four people said it after him at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said it after him too, and about four of them have said since that it is the fourth morning running that the figure has got more voices than anything else that has been said in this yard and that nobody in this yard has worked out why a boy of seventeen has become the loudest thing at the top of that bank.

---

The clerk read a room's page out loud at about a quarter to ten, in the ordinary voice, to about nine people, and entered nothing on it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a clerk who reads it and enters nothing is the only thing in this yard that has never once refused anybody anything.

---

Sabra Holt came down about four feet of that bank at about a quarter to eleven and stood where the boy was standing with his sheet against his forearm, and about four people at the top of eleven feet watched her do it and did not come down.

"Orren," she said.

---

He looked up and she did not go straight on, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the pause was the whole of the asking.

"How many people were standing at the foot of that wall at about half past three yesterday afternoon," she said, "when the man of about sixty-four said where the wire was."

---

He did not answer straight away and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they thought at the time he was working out a number and that he was not.

"No," he said.

---

The boards were the boards and the light was going across them.

---

"No," he said again, not louder. "I am not going to count that. I have had a sheet every morning for four years and I have never once refused to put a figure on it and I am refusing one now, and I would like it said out loud at about eleven this morning in about nine people's hearing that I said no, so that nobody in this yard spends the rest of the day waiting for me to change my mind."

---

Sabra Holt stood where she was for a moment and then she said, in the ordinary voice, "Thank you," and went up the eleven feet, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have said since that those were the only two words she said about it and that about four of them have said that the two words were worse than anything she could have said.

---

Nobody asked him again.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet came down about four feet of that bank in the half hour after that and stood near him, and about four of them said things to him that were not about the number, and about four of them said nothing and stood there, and the boy wrote his morning down at about half past eleven in his own hand on his own sheet, in a column of his own, and about four people have said since that he wrote less than he has written on any morning for four years.

---

Mavis Dorr said the rest of it out loud at about one, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, to about nine people.

"So we have got a yard," she said, "in which a man of thirty-eight would not carry it up nine miles of hill, a woman of fifty-two has not got a column for it, a man of about forty-eight says a figure of work is not a figure of days, and a boy of seventeen will not count it, and about nine people failed to agree on two words yesterday at about eleven this morning. I am not saying that any of them is wrong. I am saying that about four of us have spent four days finding out what this yard cannot do with a thing, and yesterday we found out one more thing it cannot do, and it is put it in a book."

---

Nobody said anything.

---

"And I would like about nine people at the top of eleven feet to hear me say that I do not think the boy is the interesting one," said Mavis Dorr. "The interesting one is that he was the only person anybody could have asked. A man of thirty-eight has not got a sheet. I have not got a sheet. She has got a book with five lines on it and a heading that says whoever. I have got about as much as you have got. That boy has got a sheet, four years of mornings on it, and a hand that has never once stopped, and he was asked a question this morning and he said no in about nine people's hearing, and about four of us have known all morning that if he had said a number then this yard would have got a figure for it before the light went off the boards."

---

The man who digs loam said, "Then he has done us no harm."

---

"He has done us the only thing anybody has done for four days," said Mavis Dorr, "which is to leave a thing alone. I did not ask him to do it and I am not going to thank him for it and I am not going to say one word about it again, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have been waiting for me to say something about it since about eleven."

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and second night of that run, having slept on five hundred and one of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the ninety-second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet who were watching that wall at about half past three could see that nothing was being said down there.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the thirty-sixth time running, and about four of the nineteen had heard a boy of seventeen say no at about eleven that morning and had not seen anybody write down that either.

The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book. Whoever is the heading over the one name that is in that column, and that name does not satisfy the rule, and the second space on that form is empty. Six months is the whole of what a clerk has counted this morning and she counted nothing else. Fourteen is the whole of what this district has made and it did not move this morning either. Five is the whole of what this district does not have and the fifth of the five is as unpaid as the rest. There is a sheet at that gatepost with four hundred and eleven on it and not one person in this basin has got to by how much it is out. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move, because nobody came down that lane this morning with a page in their own hand. The toll is ninety-eight and it is spent. The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five. The bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot. A man of thirty-eight is at the near end of the first table this morning and has not been told he is a good man and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three.
