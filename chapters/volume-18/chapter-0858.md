# Chapter 858: The One Hundred And Forty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month At About Eleven This Morning The Man Who Digs Loam Asked A Man Of Fifty-Six Out Loud What The Four Figures On That Wall Are For And He Was Given The Answer He Gives At About Four Every Morning And About Nine People At The Top Of Eleven Feet Heard It And About Four Of Them Carried It Down In Their Heads To Look At A Piece Of Paper And It Does Not Fit And Nothing Was Entered And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and ninety-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and fifty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and seventy-two days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and eighty-six days. Seven hundred and forty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and sixty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and sixty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and thirty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and thirty-nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and seventy-five days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and fifteen marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and the man who digs loam asked a question out loud at the foot of that low wall at about eleven this morning and was given the same answer he gives at about four every morning, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and seventy-two mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and seventy-one. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and twenty-two mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and forty-two, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and eighty-third of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and the man who digs loam asked him out loud at about eleven what the four figures on that wall are for and he was given the answer he gives at about four every morning, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it and about four of them carried it down eleven feet in their heads to look at a piece of paper.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-seventh night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-seventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the second time in nine years, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood fifty-seven days, and the only answer this yard has was said out loud at the foot of that low wall this morning and it does not fit any of the three lines on that page, and the page is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said that it did not move, and he said no figure at all after that.

---

At about eleven the man who digs loam came down about four feet of that bank and went the rest of the way.

He had his spade over his shoulder and he put it down against the low wall about four feet from the man of fifty-six, and about four people at the foot of that bank looked at him do it and about nine people at the top of eleven feet looked down the eleven feet and could not hear anything at all.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the man who digs loam said four words at the boards on the one hundred and forty-first morning and stopped in the middle of a sentence, and that on the one hundred and forty-second morning he went down a bank to ask a question he had heard other people ask, and that about four of them have not worked out which of those two things was braver and have stopped trying.

---

"What are the four figures on that wall for," said the man who digs loam.

He said it in the ordinary voice, standing up, about four feet off the low wall, and about four feet of stone carried it and about eleven feet of bank carried it the rest of the way, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it.

---

The man of fifty-six did not get up.

He put one hand flat on the stone and he said, in the ordinary voice, and at the speed he says it at about four, that he does not know what any of them is for.

Then he took his hand off the stone and he did not say one word more, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have listened to that sentence at about four every morning for about nine days and that this morning it came up the bank at about eleven instead, and that it was the same sentence at the same speed and that they did not get a second thing out of it and were not going to.

---

"Thank you," said the man who digs loam, and picked his spade up off the wall and went back up about four feet of that bank.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet came down the eleven feet in the next quarter of an hour, which about nine of them had not done on any morning this week, and they came down to look at a piece of paper that a woman of fifty-two was holding in her own hand at the foot of that bank.

Mavis Dorr got there first and read the third line out loud over a woman's shoulder, which is the only time in about nine mornings that anybody in that yard has read anything out of that page at the foot of the wall.

"The answer he was given," she said. "That is us. That is the third line. We can fill that one."

---

About four people at the foot of that bank said nothing and about four of them had already got to the same thing.

"That is the answer he was given at about eleven this morning," said Mavis Dorr, "and it is the answer he was given at about four this morning, and it is the answer he was given to a woman with a beam on the one hundred and twenty-seventh morning, and the page wants one of them."

---

"How are we going to know which one," said the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally.

"You are not," said Mavis Dorr. "That is the whole of it. He says the same sentence at about four every afternoon and he said the same sentence at about eleven this morning and he will say the same sentence again this afternoon, and about four of us have just heard two of them inside one morning, and they are the same sentence, and there is no way in this basin of telling the one that page wants from the ones it does not."

---

"But we could put it on," said Mavis Dorr, and stopped, and about four people at the foot of that bank looked at her.

"I have been standing here going to say something and going to stop," she said. "I have said it and I am not going back on it." "We could put it on. That is the thing about it. It is true. Whatever we picked it would be a sentence that man has said in this yard more times than I have been at these boards this week, and it would be true, and the page would be answered, and the room at the top of nine miles would have the third of its three lines."

---

"And about nine people at the top of eleven feet would be able to say that we picked it," said Sabra Holt.

---

"Yes," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"Then we do not pick it," said Sabra Holt, "and I am not going to say that is cowardice, and I am not going to say that it is careful, and I am going to say what it is: picking one of them is naming. It is not naming a person. It is naming an afternoon. And a room that gets one afternoon out of this yard has been told by this yard which afternoon it is, and about nineteen people live in this yard, and about nine of them have asked that question out loud and about four of them have said out loud this week that they did not decide to, and the afternoons we would be choosing between are the afternoons they did it on."

---

"That is all right," said Mavis Dorr, at about half past twelve, at the foot of that bank. "Then say the other half of it and do not be kind to me. Is there an afternoon we are allowed to pick."

---

"There is," said Sabra Holt. "The one on the one hundred and twelfth morning, because that is the only one that happened to one person and about nine people at the top of eleven feet can say so out loud."

---

"And that is the one that makes this yard one man," said Mavis Dorr, "and we said that out loud at about half past one on the one hundred and thirty-seventh morning and we were right then and we are right now, and I would like about four people at the top of eleven feet to notice that the yard has been right about this twice and has got nothing to show for it either time."

---

Nobody at the foot of that bank said anything.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-seventh night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-seventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the thirty-first time running.

About four of the nineteen had heard the same sentence at about eleven that morning and about four of them have said since that a man who has said the identical sentence at about eleven and at about four on one morning has been asked it twice in a day for the second time since the one hundred and twenty-seventh morning, and that about four of them cannot get anything out of the two occasions and are not going to, and that about four of them have not been able to stop noticing that the two were the same words at the same speed.

---

At about ten to five the light went off the boards and about nine people were within about four hundred yards of them.

The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book. Whoever is the heading over that column and there is one name under it, and that name does not satisfy the rule, and the second space on that form has been empty all morning. Six months is what the clerk counted this morning and she did not count a seventh. Fourteen is what this district has made and there is no fifteenth of it. Five is what this district does not have and none of the five has been paid. The figure on that gatepost's sheet is four hundred and eleven and by how much it is out nobody in this basin has worked out. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move, because no one came down that lane this morning with a page in their own hand, and the toll is ninety-eight and it is spent, and the ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five, and a man of thirty-eight is standing at about four feet off the near end of the first table and has not been told he is a good man and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the one hundred and forty-second morning was the morning this yard had its own answer said out loud in front of it and looked at the page and found that the answer was no use, and that about four of them have said that the worst of it is that it was true, and that about four of them have said that they would rather it had been wrong.
