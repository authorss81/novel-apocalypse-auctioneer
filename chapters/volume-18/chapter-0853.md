# Chapter 853: The One Hundred And Thirty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month About Nine People At The Top Of Eleven Feet Named The Three Lines On That Page Out Loud At Those Boards One At A Time At About One In The Afternoon And Every One Of Them Was Refused By The Person It Would Have Used And Nothing Was Entered And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and ninety-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and fifty-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and sixty-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and eighty-one days. Seven hundred and forty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and fifty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and sixty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and thirty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and thirty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and seventy days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and ten marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and ninety-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and about nine people at those boards named the three lines on that page one at a time this morning and every one of them was refused by the person it would have used, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and sixty-seven mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and sixty-six. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and seventeen mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and thirty-seven, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-three lines on it and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and seventy-eighth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and the three lines on that page were named out loud at those boards one at a time at about one this afternoon and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard each one be refused.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-second night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety-one of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the second time in nine years, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood fifty-two days, and a page came down that lane and the three lines on it were named one at a time at those boards this afternoon and not one of the three was filled, and it is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and not one of the thirty-three is about a question, and the second space on that form is still empty, and the page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said that it did not move, and he said no figure at all after that.

---

The trestle was still standing at the near end of the second table at about ten and it had been standing three mornings.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet looked at it at about ten past ten and about nine of them did not say one word about it, and about four of them have said since that a man who has put a trestle up at about ten every morning for about nine years has now left one standing for three mornings and has still not come up the bank for it, and that the not-coming is the part that has started to be a thing in that yard.

---

At about half past twelve Mavis Dorr put the three lines out on the boards of the second table in words rather than on anything.

She had no paper and she did not want the page, and she said so before anybody could ask her for it, because the page was in Sabra Holt's hand eleven feet below and was going to stay there.

"I am going to say the three lines one at a time," she said, "and I am going to say out loud the name of the person in this yard who could put something in each one, and I am not going to ask anybody to do anything, and I am not going to ask anybody to say yes or no, and if you have something to say about the one that is your line then say it, and if you have not got something then do not."

---

"Line one," she said. "The name of the person who asked. That is yours, Adrian."

---

Adrian Vale was at about four feet off the near end of the first table and he had not come to the second table this morning and he had not come to it yesterday either.

He came about nine feet to the near end of the second table at about ten past one and stood there and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not make a sound.

"I want to say out loud, at these boards, with about nine people at the top of eleven feet hearing it," he said, "that if a name goes on that page it will be mine, and that it will be mine by accident, and that it would be right by accident, and that neither of those is the same as being true."

---

He put one hand flat on the boards of the second table, which he had not done at that table on any morning anybody in that yard could remember.

"I asked one question at the foot of that low wall on the one hundred and twelfth morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it and a clerk wrote down that the asking happened, and then about four days ago I said out loud at these boards that I am not going to be the second man in this basin to carry a thing out of a yard and have it come back as something else. I have been standing at about four feet off this table for about nine mornings being the man who started it, and I have watched about nineteen people do a thing I did on my own, and I am not going to have a piece of paper come down nine miles of hill and go back up it with my name on it, because the day that happens this yard is one man again and it will be one man on paper and it will be about nineteen people on the ground, and the paper is what a room nine miles up that hill can see."

---

He took his hand off the boards.

"That is not about being careful," he said. "That is about there being about nineteen of us and one space."

---

"Line two," said Mavis Dorr. "The morning on which he asked. That one is yours, Orren."

---

The boy had his sheet flat on the boards and he did not look up at her when she said it.

"I have got it," he said. "It is in my sheet in my own hand and it is on the page I keep for myself and it has been in my sheet for about nine days and I have not said it out loud and I am not going to say it out loud this morning either."

---

"I am not going to say no to you," he said, "because you did not ask me a question. I am going to say that it is not going on that page, and that is different."

---

"About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a boy of seventeen said two sentences at those boards and that the second one was the first one he had ever put in front of the asking instead of beside it."

---

"Line three," said Mavis Dorr. "The answer he was given. That one is not anybody's and I want about nine people at the top of eleven feet to notice that it is not anybody's."

---

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said it before anybody else did.

"Mine is not it," he said. "I say a number into about four hundred yards of air every morning at about one and about nine people hear it and nobody writes it down, and I said out loud on the one hundred and thirtieth morning that I do not know what I am keeping it for, and I am saying it again this morning in front of about nine people: I do not know what I am keeping it for, and that has not changed, and a room nine miles up that hill can have my number if it wants my number but it is not the answer to anything."

---

At about twenty past one a man of sixty-one came up about four feet of that bank and said one sentence at the near end of the second table and went back down it.

"That answer belongs to a man who gives it every morning at about four whether anybody asks him or not," he said, "and he has never asked anybody to put it anywhere, and I have been at the foot of that bank for seventeen years and I have never once put down what a man said to me, and I am not going to start this afternoon because a piece of paper came down a lane."

---

Sabra Holt said the whole of the rest of it at about half past one, standing at about four feet off the near end of the second table with the page folded once in her own hand behind her back.

"There is nobody in this yard who can fill that page and I am going to say it as a plain fact and not as a thing anybody should be proud of this morning," she said. "That page has got one name on it and one morning on it and one answer on it, and about nineteen people live in this yard, and there is no way of filling three lines on a piece of paper with about nineteen people that does not make one of them the yard."

---

"And I want about nine people at the top of eleven feet to hear me say what follows, because it is the part that matters and it is not about me." She turned round and looked about nine feet at the boards. "If one of us fills that page then that one of us is the yard, and about four people at the top of eleven feet would know exactly which one, and the room at the top of nine miles would get one person out of this district instead of a district, and that one person would be a woman with a beam who went back down a bank, or a boy of seventeen who says a figure before my hand moves, or a man of thirty-eight who has a question he is not allowed to say at these boards. And whichever one it was, the other eighteen of us would have handed it to her at about half past one in the middle of an afternoon without deciding to."

---

Nobody at the top of eleven feet said anything.

---

"So no," said Sabra Holt, "and I am not going to be comforted about it, and I have said that I am not going to be comfortable in this yard five or six times now and this is the first time I have said it about a piece of paper, and I would like about four people at the top of eleven feet to notice the difference and not to ask me about it."

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and ninety-second night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ninety-one of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighty-second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was not asked one question this morning.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank because it was public lot and about four of them said it too, and then the man of fifty-six put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the twenty-sixth time running.

---

Nobody asked him anything that afternoon and nobody has asked him anything since the woman with the beam asked him at about ten to two on the one hundred and twenty-seventh morning.

The light went off the boards at about ten to five and about nine people were within about four hundred yards of them. The page at the back of that book carries thirty-three lines and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book. The column carries one name and the heading over it still says whoever and the one name does not satisfy the rule and the second space on that form is still empty. A clerk has counted six months this morning and that is the whole of it. This district has made fourteen things and that is the whole of that too. The count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid. Four hundred and eleven is on a sheet at a gatepost and nobody in this basin has computed by how much it is out. The lane count is twenty-eight and did not move, because nobody came down that lane this morning with a page in their own hand, and the toll is ninety-eight and it is spent, and the ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a yard said out loud at about half past one on the one hundred and thirty-seventh morning that it cannot fill three lines on a piece of paper, and that it is the first thing anybody in that yard has said out loud that it cannot do, and that about four of them have been asking each other all afternoon what the yard is going to do instead, and that none of them has got an answer, and that the piece of paper is still eleven feet below them in a woman's hand with three blank lines on it.
