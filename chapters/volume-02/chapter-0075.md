# Chapter 75: Nine Feet and Not Fourteen

At about four in the afternoon on the eighteenth of this month a man of about sixty-eight came up out of a wet chamber at the Harrow end of the Needle with water to his chest and a galvanised bucket in one hand, and he put the bucket on the boards of the engine house, and he said, to nobody in particular, that it was a quarter past four and that there were two more of them before dark.

The engine is not a locomotive and it is not a bus and it has never moved. It is a stationary oil engine on a brick plinth inside a brick house about eleven feet above the flood, with a belt going down through a hole in the floor to a pump, and the pump draws the Harrow interchange, and it has drawn it every day since the flood year.

He is called Gilman Trew and nobody had asked him what his job was.

---

The party had been on the viaduct for five hours and it was four of the Road House men, Adrian Vale, a lamp-man, and the foreman, and the foreman's name is Ivor Kell and he has been a foreman for twenty-two years and he is not a good man at a fight and he is the best man with a shovel in this district.

**The finding on the eighteenth, entered by Nessa Pell from a sheet and not at a rail, is that four things had gone wrong at the Harrow end in one afternoon and that three of them had gone wrong in the week before, and that not one of them had been reported by anything, because there is nothing at the Harrow end of this railway that reports.**

First: the ballast had gone about thirty yards west of the engine house. The cess had become a channel and there was a foot of brown water in it and the drain under the four-foot had stopped. Second: the sump had taken gravel off the shoulder above it and the pump was throwing water over the lip of the brick house instead of down the outfall, and the outfall was the thing the water had to be able to reach, and the outfall was eleven feet above a nine-foot flood. Third: the engine had been running at a fault since the second of April and had been getting hotter, and the only man who knew it was hot was the man who put his hand on the pipe every evening at six.

And the fourth thing is the one the whole afternoon turns on, and nobody found it, because it is not a fault: **Gilman Trew has been keeping that engine for nine years and he has been paid for it out of the float of a firm that gave the lease up on the thirtieth of last month.**

There is a fifth thing, and it is the reason there was no first, second or third in any week before this one, and it is the oldest thing at the Harrow end and it is a hole in the ground.

**The ground signal at the Harrow end went out of the same frame in the fourth year, when the Tongue was taken out of it, and it has never been put back.** There is a plinth on the embankment thirty yards east of the engine house with the bolt-holes in it and a cap of lead over them and no iron in it. A signal is how a railway tells somebody what it is doing. A ground signal is how the far end of a line tells the near end that the far end has stopped being a place a train can be.

So: the water went up on the west side and nothing said so, twice, and the gravel went into the sump and nothing said so, and the engine has been running hot for a fortnight and nothing said so, **and the reason nothing said so is that there is nothing at the Harrow end of this line that has anything to say.** It is not that the report was lost. It is that there is nobody at the other end of it, and a report to nobody is not a failed report. It is nothing at all.

That is the third fault of Selik Marne's bridge administration, and the administration has not been asked about it, and nobody in it knows, and it is in the book with a date on it, and the reason it is in a book in Alder Reach and not in his own is that he did not know.

Kell got the brushwood out of the cess himself and stood in a foot of water passing bundles up to two men on the shoulder, and he did it for about two hours without once raising his voice, and then he came out and said the thing that had been sitting in him since the ninth.

"You have all been up on that bank for a month," he said. "**I want the room to hear one sentence from a man who has been up here nine times in nine years and has never been asked anything. I am not angry about the lease. I am angry that I did not know until the ninth of April that I had been carrying a road house's oil up a bank for nine years on a float, and that when I did know, the first thing I did was stop, and I stopped, and the stopping took nine days to fix because nobody in this city knew what the oil was for.**"

---

**And then the man who keeps the engine was asked what his job was, and it took the lamp-man four minutes to do it in the right order, because the tenth hand was not there and the lamp-man was, and Oswin Tace asked it in a brick house in the rain and got an answer nobody expected.**

The lamp-man is forty-one and has asked twice that it be said, on two separate days, in two yards, that he counts lamps and not signals, and he asked it a third time on the sixteenth of this month in front of a drover from the uplands, and he said it here before he asked anything else, at the door of the engine house, to about eleven people.

"I am a lamp-man," he said. "**I do the lamps. There are nine of them between the second place and this one and I do them twice a week and I have done them since I was twenty-two, and I am not a signal hand and I am not a keeper and I am not anybody's man.**"

And then he asked the man in the bucket.

"What is the job," he said. "**In the ordinary way. I have been a lamp-man for nineteen years and I have never once been asked what a job is and I have decided to ask everybody, so: what is yours.**"

Gilman Trew wiped his face with the back of his wrist and looked at the bucket.

"**Keep her running,**" he said.

"That is not a job," said the lamp-man. "**That is a thing a machine does. Say the part with you in it.**"

"**Six in the morning and six at night,**" said Gilman Trew. "**Down into that chamber to clear the grating because it silts in a fortnight and it silts faster now, and up, and the oil, and the belt, and the outfall, and the fire at half past five in the winter, and the sheet. And if she stops at midnight the water comes up on the ninth place's bank inside a month, and I have seen it come up once, and I was forty-one, and I am not going to see it twice.**"

Nobody said anything for a little while, and the water came over the lip of the outfall and went down the face of the brick house in a sheet, and the lamp-man wrote it down in a pocket book with a pencil stub, in about nine words, and read the nine words back to him before he put the book away.

> **THE JOB: KEEP THE PUMPING ENGINE AT THE HARROW END RUNNING. SIX IN THE MORNING AND SIX AT NIGHT. DOWN INTO THE CHAMBER TO CLEAR THE GRATING. OIL, BELT, OUTFALL, FIRE, SHEET. SIXTY-EIGHT YEARS OLD. PAID OUT OF A FLOAT. NOT A RATE. NOT A NAME.**

And the finding printed underneath it, in the same hand and not by the lamp-man, is that **this is the fourth time in eleven weeks that a person in this district has described a job that has been happening for years, and that every one of the four was found by somebody who was doing a different job, and that not one of the four was found by anybody with a sheet.**

---

And then the party stopped shoring for an hour, because Kell sent two of his men down to the ninth place for dry boards and dry socks and because a man who has been in water since two in the afternoon has to be given an hour, and in that hour the engine house filled with about eleven people and the two subjects that had been in every yard for six weeks came into a brick box on a viaduct and turned out to be nothing like they had sounded.

Ivor Kell had found out about the lease on the ninth of April. He had come to the Road House yard at six in the morning on the tenth with a question that was not about the lease, and Tarin Kest had answered it in about four sentences, and Kell had then said the sentence that a foreman says when he has understood something and does not like it.

"**Twenty-two years I have kept that man's stock out of the weather,**" he said. "**And there is nowhere in this city to keep it. Eleven vehicles a week, four spaces, and I have been putting the overflow on a bank nine miles west on a lease you gave away, and on the thirtieth of last month the bank stopped being ours and the vehicles kept coming, and there were seven a week with nowhere to stand for nineteen days, and I want you to know that I counted every one and that I have a figure and that the figure is not a complaint, it is a method.**"

"You have a method," said Tarin.

"**I have a yard full of vehicles standing in the rain against a wall and I have priced the standing salvage in two figures and both figures are mine, and one of them is four hundred and ten coppers and the other one is nothing,**" said Kell. "**That is the whole of what I have come to say and I said it on the tenth and I am saying it again on a viaduct because a man who says a thing once in an office and then finds out the office was right about the rest of it has to say it somewhere with weather in it.**"

Nobody argued with him, and his figure went into the book with his name on it against it, which is the first time a foreman's arithmetic has been entered in the public lot book, and the entry says that he asked for it to be entered and that he asked for it to be entered **because a method that is not written down is a man being angry, and a method that is written down is a rate that has not been argued yet.**

And then he asked the question that had brought him up the bank, and he asked it to Adrian and not to Tarin, and the reason he gave was that Tarin had already answered a version of it in an office and a foreman wants the other half.

"Who pays for the oil."

The reason the district was up there at all, and it is worth putting down before the answer, is that a man in a good coat said a true sentence in a yard on the fourteenth and nobody in Alder Reach was able to argue with it, and the sentence was that a certificate is not a safety instrument, and a man with eleven coppers a quarter in his hand agreed with it and sent a form up the bank to find out what else was there. There was not much else. The engine house is a brick box about nine feet by eleven with a stove in the corner of it, a bench, a shelf of tools, and a stovepipe that goes out through the roof and comes back in under the eaves. The engine is a horizontal single-cylinder about the length of a cart with a flywheel at the end of it the size of a cartwheel. It is nine years old, it was new in the flood year, and it cost a number that is in a book in Alder Reach and not anywhere else.

Adrian Vale stood in that house for about two hours with a pencil and did not take a single figure, and that is the finding, and it is in the book, and it is the first time in seven weeks that the man who prices things has stood in a room and priced nothing, and the reason is not modesty.

**He tried. That is entered. He asked Kell what a bundle of brushwood costs and Kell said he did not know and did not care, and he asked the lamp-man what a week of oil costs and the lamp-man said he had never been asked that either, and he asked Gilman Trew what he is paid and Trew said he is not paid, he is given what is left in a tin, and Adrian wrote that down and then struck it out and then wrote it again, because a man who is given what is left in a tin is not a man on a rate and is not a man on a wage and is a man in a position, and this district has met that before and did not know what to call it.**

"**There is no column for this,**" he said, out loud, to a brick wall.

"And there is no column for the ballast either," said Kell. "**You have come up here to find out who a road is owed to and there is nothing here to be owed to anybody. There is an engine in a box and a bucket and a man of sixty-eight, and the man of sixty-eight is the only asset and you cannot post him.**"

**He did get one figure out of the room and it is in the book, and it took two evenings and a fair hand, and it is the only figure anybody has, and it came off a shelf behind the tools where the man keeps the sheet: nine years of Road House oil receipts in a tradesman's own hand, folded in a bundle with a bit of tarpaulin over it, because the man put it there against the day the man above him stopped bringing it.**

The receipts are for oil and for lamp oil and for about nine pints of machine oil a month, bought on a standing arrangement with a chandler on the lower terrace and drawn against the float. They are in an employer's name and not in his, and the employer is a firm, and the firm gave up a lease on the thirtieth of last month.

"So the engine cost nine years of a Road House float and a man of sixty-eight's hands," Adrian said, "**and the sheet on that shelf is the only document in this district that says one single thing about any of it, and it says it in a tradesman's hand on the back of a bill, and nobody has ever asked the man for it, because nobody has ever asked the man anything.**"

"**Then get the figure out of it,**" said Kell.

"**It is out of it. Fourteen thousand seven hundred and forty-two coppers, and it is not a price, and I want that entered, because it is the second cost this district has ever costed and the first one is on a kitchen wall in a hand big enough to read from a doorway, and that one had no figure at all and this one has a figure and neither of them is a price, and the reason is the same in both, and the reason is that a price is a number a person can hand over and this district does not contain the person the number is for.**"

---

**And the oil is the whole of the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is in the book, and it took the tenth hand's page and two evenings and it is nine cans in a fortnight at seven coppers a can.**

It is called a lot of figures because it is the only arithmetic anybody in this district can do about the Harrow end, and here it is entire.

> **NINE CANS IN A FORTNIGHT. SEVEN COPPERS A CAN. SIXTY-THREE COPPERS A FORTNIGHT. TWENTY-SIX FORTNIGHTS A YEAR. ONE THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-EIGHT COPPERS A YEAR.**
>
> **NINE YEARS. FOURTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY-TWO COPPERS, SPENT OUT OF A FLOAT, ON A ROAD AND AN ENGINE AND A SUM OF WATER, WITH NO DOCUMENT IN THE WARD MARKET DISTRICT OR THE WATER OFFICE THAT HAS THAT FIGURE IN IT OR ANY PART OF IT.**

That is the sum. It is in the book on the twentieth of this month, in a fair hand, and the sentence under it is the tenth hand's and it is four words long: **NOBODY OWED HIM. HE OWED OIL.**

And then, on the twenty-second, a lease clerk in a room in the ward market district read out four thousand leases in twenty-two years and found that the answer to the question had been in a book at the end of her own table the whole time, and the reason it took until the twenty-second to find out is that on the twentieth nobody in Alder Reach had thought to go and look, and that is in the book as the fifth thing anybody in this district did not think to do in a month.

---

**And two things were asked of Adrian Vale in that brick house and both of them were asked by people who were not asking for anything, and the first was whether he was going to read the engine, and the second was not asked at all and turned out to matter more.**

The lamp-man asked the first, at about half past five, in the ordinary voice, because he had asked everybody everything all day.

"**You read things,**" he said. "**Are you going to read her.**"

And the answer, in the book, with the hour on it, was no, and the reason is not a rule and not a limit and not a discipline and the book says so in those words.

"**I have had an hour available to me every day since the fifth of this month,**" Adrian said. "**And I am standing in a brick box on a viaduct with a pump in it and a man in a bucket, and I have been in this district for seven weeks, and I know exactly what a man does with an hour he has been keeping for nineteen days. He spends it here, on a machine, in the rain, on a thing that is not a lot, and he comes home able to say that he read a pump, and that is a sentence he will be saying in about four years when somebody asks him what he did in the flood year.**"

"That is a good reason," said the lamp-man.

"**It is the worst possible place to decide in and that is all it is,**" said Adrian. "**Put it down as that. Do not put it down as me being right.**"

---

**And the second thing was not asked, and it is the last hour of the light on the eighteenth of April, and it is the whole of what the day was for.**

The party came down off the viaduct at about ten past six because the light went at half past five in that week and there are four changeovers between the fourth place and the ninth and this was the far end of the world. Kell went first with a lamp. The lamp-man went second and did the ninth lamp on the way, because that is what he was there for, and it took him four minutes, and he said so.

And at the top of the bank, at the changeover with the two feet six inches of step down into about a foot of standing water, Adrian Vale stopped and wrote in a pocket book, in the ordinary way, eleven lines, and then read them to Tarin Kest, who had walked up from the ninth place to meet them because he was going to be there anyway.

The eleven lines are in the book in full and they are the only eleven lines in it that are not a figure, and the finding under them is that **a man who has spent seven weeks putting numbers on things wrote eleven lines with no number in them and read them out loud to another man in the rain, and neither of them pretended there was a price on the page.**

> **THE ENGINE AT THE HARROW END HAS NOBODY'S MONEY ON IT AND IT HAS HAD FOR NINE YEARS.**
>
> **IT IS THE ONLY THING ON THIS LINE THAT HAS KEPT A FLOOD FROM GETTING WORSE, AND THE ONLY PERSON WHO KNOWS THAT IS THE MAN WHO PUTS HIS HAND ON THE PIPE AT SIX EVERY EVENING, AND HE IS SIXTY-EIGHT, AND HE IS NOT PAID, HE IS GIVEN WHAT IS LEFT IN A TIN.**
>
> **IF SHE STOPS THE WATER COMES UP ON A BANK FOUR MILES AWAY WHERE THERE ARE THIRTY-ONE PEOPLE AND FOUR HOUSES AND NO PLATFORM, AND THE WAY IT COMES UP IS NOT A FLOOD COMING DOWN. IT IS A BANK GOING OUT FROM UNDER A THING THAT WAS STEADY, AND THE MAN OF SEVENTY WHO LOOKS AT THAT WALL EVERY EVENING HAS SEEN ONE GO AND HE HAS TOLD US SO AND WE DID NOT WRITE IT DOWN AT THE TIME.**
>
> **SO THE FIRST THING IS: THE PUMP IS NOT GOING TO STOP. I CANNOT MAKE THAT TRUE BY SAYING IT AND NEITHER CAN ANYBODY IN THIS YARD, AND THE ONLY THING THAT MAKES IT TRUE IS OIL, AND OIL IS SEVEN COPPERS A CAN, AND NINE CANS IN A FORTNIGHT IS SIXTY-THREE COPPERS, AND NINE YEARS OF THAT IS FOURTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY-TWO COPPERS, AND WE DO NOT KNOW WHO OWED IT AND THE ANSWER IS IN A ROOM FOUR STREETS FROM HERE.**
>
> **AND THE SECOND THING IS: I CANNOT READ HER, AND I WANT THAT WRITTEN DOWN IN THE ORDER I SAID IT IN. I COULD. THERE IS AN HOUR AND I HAVE HAD IT FOR NINETEEN DAYS. I AM NOT GOING TO, BECAUSE I AM IN THE WRONG ROOM, AND IF I READ A PUMP IN A BOX ON A VIADUCT I WILL HAVE SPENT AN HOUR AND I WILL NOT HAVE BOUGHT ANYTHING, AND THE REASON I HAVE BEEN REFUSING TO READ FOR FIVE WEEKS IS THAT I KEEP LOOKING FOR A PLACE WHERE A READING IS WORTH WHAT IT COSTS, AND THERE IS NOT ONE, AND THAT IS NOT THE SAME AS NOT LOOKING.**

Tarin Kest stood in the rain at the top of that changeover for about eleven seconds after, and then said one sentence, and it is in the book, and it is the shortest thing the Road House has said all month.

"**Then somebody has to go and look in the room,**" he said.

And Adrian said, "**Yes.**"

And Tarin said, "**I will go with you. I signed the paper in that room. I am not going to send anybody else to read it and I am not going to go by myself.**"

---

**And the last thing on the eighteenth of April is a lamp-man's pocket book, and it is in the sleeve at 411 with everything else, and the tenth hand entered it as a document, and the entry under it says the reason and the reason is that it is the first piece of paper in nine years that came out of a job rather than out of a building, and the man who wrote it does not read figures and has never been asked to.**

There is one line in it that is not the job.

> **THE MAN OF THIRTY-NINE FROM THE WATER OFFICE READ MY FIVE QUESTIONS OUT OF HIS OWN BOOK ON THE SEVENTH OF THIS MONTH AND HE READ THEM IN THE ROAD. HE ASKED ME WHY I KNEW THE HAT AND I TOLD HIM AND HE WROTE IT DOWN. HE HAS NOT BEEN WEST OF THE SECOND PLACE SINCE THE FLOOD YEAR AND HE CAME FOUR HOURS UP A BANK TO LOOK AT A HUT WITH A BOY IN IT.**

And under that, in the same hand, and it is the last line in the pocket book, and Oswin Tace has said that he wrote it on the top of the viaduct on the eighteenth of this month and that he did not know then that anybody was going to read it and that he would have written it anyway.

> **THERE IS AN EMPTY ROOM OVER THE COUNTING OFFICE ON THE SECOND FLOOR AND IT HAS HAD A BED IN IT FOR TWO YEARS AND NOBODY IN IT, AND HE HAS THE KEYS, AND HE WROTE IT DOWN BECAUSE A BOY SLEPT IN THE RAIN, AND THE SECOND COPY OF THAT BOOK GOES TO A MARKET IN ABOUT THREE WEEKS.**
