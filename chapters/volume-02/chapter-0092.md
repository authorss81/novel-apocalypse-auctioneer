# Chapter 92: Nine Vehicles

At about ten in the morning on the seventh of this month a man with a lamp walked the length of nine vehicles standing on a siding four and a half hours up a bank, counting buffers, and the count came to nine, and it took him about forty minutes, and nobody had asked him to do it.

His name is Gilman Trew and he is sixty-eight and he keeps an oil engine in a brick house about eleven feet above the flood at the Harrow end, and his job is an engine, and he is not a signal hand and has never been one, and the reason he counted the buffers is that a man who has been keeping a pump for nine years knows that nine vehicles standing in one place for a hundred and eighteen days is nine vehicles leaning.

He did not say that they were leaning. He said the count, and then he said that the fifth vehicle is about half an inch nearer the column than it was in the fourth year, and that a rail on a siding settles, and that **it is not a fault and it is not a stall and it is not a mystery, and he would like whoever is writing the book to write down that a man of sixty-eight said it was not a mystery before anybody asked him whether it was.**

Nobody asked him whether it was. He was on that siding for about an hour and a half because he had gone up the bank to look at the ninth-place board, which he cannot read, standing four feet off it, and he had gone up to look at it because a man he knows had told him there was a new line painted on the top of it.

It has been raining since about four for three days. The shoulder at the ninth place is nine feet above a drop of nine feet, the whole of it is bad ground, and about forty people are on it who are not in a household and are not on a roll and got in because somebody held a door.

---

**And on the eighth of this month four people went and looked at the three reasons a train on that siding cannot go east, and the three reasons are not one reason, and the district has spent nine weeks treating them as one.**

The first is the loop. The siding runs off the line through a set of points and round the water column and back onto the bank, and a train that comes in over the points and goes round the loop comes out facing the way it went in, which is the other way, and a train that has to travel east has to come out of that loop facing east.

The second is the frame. **The points on that loop are worked from a lever frame in the hut, and the ninth place is the only frame on the whole line with a bracket cut in it that the iron fits, and the bracket is empty, and it has been empty since the sixth of April.**

The third is the toll. Putting the iron in the bracket and throwing the points and running nine vehicles round that loop is a reversal, and the use of the iron is to send one vehicle back along the section it came from, one movement, one way, and the use is not given back, **and the toll on that iron is the origin, and the origin is a place, and a place is charged, and this line has a page in a public book that says exactly how many times.**

Nobody had put the three reasons in one place before the eighth of this month, and the person who did it was a woman of about fifty-one who keeps a book of departures and who had been asked for the number by somebody she had not seen before, and who gave it in about four seconds.

"One hundred and forty-one," she said. "**Since the flood year. Out of nine hundred and six movements in the book. And I can tell you what a hundred and forty-one is and I cannot tell you what one of them is, because I have been in that hut eleven years and I have never once been able to say when the last thing left here.**"

---

**And the page that carries the number is in the public lot book in Alder Reach and has carried it since the eighth of May, and it is a page in a book and not a board, and it is four lines long and the fourth is the whole of what this volume is about.**

> **THE TOLL ON THIS LINE IS THE ORIGIN. THE ORIGIN IS A PLACE AND NOT A PERSON. THE LINE'S OWN BOOK HAS NINE HUNDRED AND SIX MOVEMENTS IN IT SINCE THE FLOOD YEAR. A HUNDRED AND FORTY-ONE OF THEM NAME THE NINTH PLACE AS THE ORIGIN HELD.**
>
> **NOBODY IN THIS BASIN CAN FIND THAT OFFICE IN ANY REGISTER THIS BASIN CAN REACH. THE NAME IS SPELLED OUT IN FOUR ENTRIES IN A COLUMN OF SIXTY-ONE THAT THE LINE BOOK HAS NOT GOT, AND IN FOUR ENTRIES IN A KEEPER'S COLUMN HEADED ORIGIN HELD, AND THE FOUR IN ONE BOOK ARE NOT THE FOUR IN THE OTHER, SO THERE IS A FIFTH SOMEWHERE.**

The fourth line of that entry is under the count and it is not a finding and it is not a sentence anybody said, and it is in the tenth hand's hand, and it is this: **every one of the hundred and forty-one was done by somebody in this district, on a railway, without asking a place.**

---

The boy of nineteen is at the fourth place. He has not been anybody's keeper since the fourth of June and has said so four times in three rooms, and his conditions have no end on them and are not a term, and the iron is on a bench about four feet from the end of it, under a coat, with a book on top of the book.

It is about the length of a forearm and it weighs four pounds and one ounce, and the wedge end is cut to a wedge and worn to a shine about two inches along the top, and there is a hole at the other end with a loop of red signal wire through it that was cut loose in the fourth year and has not been used as a loop since, and the bed of the hole is bright where nine years of cast iron has been polished by one wire in one direction.

It is the only thing in this district that throws those points, and the ninth place is the only frame on the line with a bracket it fits, and **the two of those facts are four hours apart on a bank, and they have been four hours apart since the sixth of April, and about eleven people have said so in public in nine weeks and nobody has done anything about it, because doing something about it means carrying a piece of cast iron four hours up a bank and the person who carried it the first time was nineteen and thought it was a bar.**

"I will not carry it again," said the boy. "I said so in a yard and I am saying it on a bench. **I will hold it while somebody else ties a rope on it and I will not be the one who goes in the hut, and if anybody tries to make me be the one who goes in the hut they are going to have to do it in front of a keeper and a board.**"

The keeper of that hut is fifty-one and has held the only pair on that line for eleven years and she said the thing about the bracket before anybody asked her, in nine words, standing in her own doorway with her arms folded.

"The bracket was cut in the ninth year," she said, "**and I have had it empty for five years, and I have never once asked anybody to fill it, and I want that written down before anybody decides I have been sitting on it.**"

---

And then on the ninth of this month a woman of about thirty-eight with a hook and a sacking sheet did a thing nobody had thought of doing. **She went up the bank on the fifteenth of a month for her own trade and came back with a question that four people in Alder Reach had not been able to answer in nine weeks.**

She buys old paper off four houses once a month. She goes up on a day she chooses, because she goes to four houses and she knows which day each of them has paper on. She is not going up there for a railway and she is not going to be at a board at a time she chose for a house.

On the ninth of this month she was in the second house on that bank and she asked the woman of the column, who is the water, a question about a piece of paper, which is her whole trade, and the question was: **has anybody ever put a platform at this place.**

She came down the bank on the tenth with the answer in about nine words and the answer was no, and the rest of the day was spent on what a no means.

"It is not a station," said the keeper of the hut, when it was read out. "It has never been a station. It is a siding and a column and a hut and four houses and thirty-one people and a man of thirty-nine who is not paid, and a woman of about fifty who is the water, and a line of chalk sixty yards west of my door that stops about nine people a year. **A platform is a thing you get off a train at.**"

"You have never got off a train here," said the paperer.

"Nobody has," said the keeper. "**And that is the whole of the answer and it took nine years to find out and it took a woman with a hook to ask it, and I have been asked four times in eleven weeks whether I would hold something and nobody has ever once asked me what this place is for.**"

---

And the whole of what a train needs to stop at a place that has never had one was worked out on the tenth of this month in a counting room in Alder Reach in about an hour, and it is a list of four things, and three of them cost money and the fourth one costs a question.

A platform. Nine feet of face, thirty feet long, out of old sleepers and boards, with a kerb.

A lamp.

A board, so that a person standing on a platform in the dark can find out what the place is.

And the fourth thing is a place in a line book that says a train stops here, and the line book is kept by hand at the east end, and the person who keeps it is a clerk of the ward market who is not the market and who has said in three conditions and a disclaimer that she will do it in the hours the counter is shut and not in the hours it is open.

The fourth thing is the only one of the four that cannot be bought with salvage, **and nobody in that room said so for about nine minutes, and the reason nobody said so is that the fourth thing is a question, and a market has never bought a question in its life.**

The money came out of the standing salvage, and the standing salvage is a security, and the finding of the tenth of this month is that **the fourth security posted in May has turned out to be the money, and that a security which is spent on the thing it secures is not a security any more, and that nobody in that room had thought of that when four people posted four things on the seventeenth of May and said out loud that a security is a thing somebody else can go and look at.**

Tarin Kest said the sentence about it in about eleven words and it is in the minute in his own words and it is the second time in a month he has been the man who says the true thing about his own security.

"It is salvage," he said. "**Nine years of it, a hundred and forty sleepers and about four hundred and ten coppers' worth of boards, and I posted it in May so that somebody could walk up that bank and look at it, and now you want to build a platform out of it, and a man who walks up that bank in August is going to find a platform and not a security, and the finding is that the only thing I ever had to stand behind was a pile of timber and I have just spent it on a place to stand on.**"

The registrar entered that finding, and entered the delay for it, and entered that **a security is a thing somebody else can go and look at and that a spent security is a thing somebody else has looked at, and that the district has one fewer security than it had in May and one more platform than it had in May, and that the trade was struck at the right price by a man who did not want the trade.**

---

**And the thing that made the whole of it possible was found on the tenth of this month at about half past ten in the morning, in a public book, by a woman of about thirty-one who was not looking for it, and it is the entry of the tenth of May and it is four lines long.**

In a room above a chandler's shop, on the tenth of last month, five people who were not there to listen to Adrian Vale struck two of the four places out of his own March draft, invented an order of weeks, and invented a fourth bearer whose only standing is the ending of the use.

And they named the origin. They named the ninth place as the origin of the toll, **and the naming was a decision and not a reading, and the room said so in its own words on the face of the sheet, and the object was not asked and did not agree.**

The two women it was done to were both in the room and were both asked before it was decided. The keeper of the hut gave one sentence in the order she meant it. The woman of the column gave two, and they are in the book in that order: **I do not like it, and I say yes.**

"A decision and a reading are two different things," said the clerk of the ward market, reading it out at the counter at about eleven, "and only one of them is on that sheet, **and what it means is that for the first time in nine years the toll of a movement on this line can be charged to a place that was asked, and not one of the hundred and forty-one was.**"

Nobody in the counting room said anything for about four seconds.

"Then the iron can go back in the bracket," said the tenth hand.

"**The iron could always go back in the bracket,**" said the registrar. "**What could not happen is what happens after it does. And the finding is that this district has spent nine weeks unable to move a piece of cast iron four hours up a bank, and that is not the finding, and everybody in this room knows what the finding is, and I am going to write it down and I am not going to like it.**"

---

And the same afternoon a stranger took a printed sheet cold off a trestle table in the yard of Lot 17, and it is the timetable the market voted for six to four on the twenty-seventh of May, and it came off a press in Alder Reach on the first of this month, and the days on it run from the ninth of this month to the twenty-third, and the train is not going to move before the twenty-seventh of this month at the earliest.

The finding is that the first timetable this district ever printed is a list of days on which nothing was going to happen, and that the market voted for it on the twenty-seventh of May, and that the market knew on the twenty-seventh of May that there was a train on a siding and did not know on the twenty-seventh of May that the train would move, and that the sheet does not say so anywhere, and that about four hundred people have taken one of these cold in a fortnight.

"You have printed a service," said the woman of about sixty with a stick, from the back of the counting room, and nobody had asked her and she was not in a row and was not on a roll.

"We have printed a sheet," said the secretary.

"**And a stranger has taken it cold and read a day on it, and a day on a sheet is a promise, and I have been in four rooms in nine weeks finding out where the holes in this district's constitution are and I have not found this one because you have printed it very well.**"

The registrar struck one sentence out of a printing on the second of this month and did not strike it out of a man's book, and **what she could not strike on the tenth was nineteen days of the month, and the finding entered is that a document can have a sentence taken out of it in four seconds and a calendar cannot, and that this district has learned to say what it cannot do and has not yet learned to print what it cannot do.**

Nobody in that room corrected her, and the market has not voted a second printing, and **the five hundred sheets are on eleven walls and on about four hundred tables in nine miles, and the first thing this district is going to have to do about a railway that moves is publish a correction, and it does not own a press and has bought one run.**

---

And on the ninth of this month, on the fourth day of a board that went up on the eighth of last month, a man who had come up the bank on foot wrote four coppers in chalk in the middle of the empty column of the toll board at the ninth place. **The finding of the tenth is that the man who printed that column's argument in April was right about the day and wrong about what the day was for.**

He wrote it at about two in the afternoon, standing up, on a shoulder eleven feet above a drop of nine feet, in the rain, and he wrote it in the four-foot column between the two pencil lines where a stranger can see it is empty on purpose, and what he wrote was **4** and nothing else.

Nobody stopped him. There were about twenty people on that shoulder and every one of them watched a stranger write a figure in the column this district has printed empty on three boards and five hundred sheets, and the keeper of the board came out of the hut with a cloth.

He is a man of thirty-nine and he is a signalman of a bridge administration and he is not a bearer and he is not paid, and he has said in three rooms that an office is not a person and a slot is not a person and a line of stones is not a person and a keeper of a board is none of those four things. **He wiped it off in about four seconds and he said nine words to the man who had written it, and the nine words are in the minute because the man who wrote the figure wrote them down himself before he went.**

"It is not what it costs to ride. It is what it costs to arrive."

Then he wrote one sentence on the bottom line of the board, which anybody who stands in front of a board may do and which nobody will be asked to explain, and the sentence is the eighteenth on that board, and it is eleven words long.

**THE COLUMN IS EMPTY BECAUSE NOBODY CAN STATE IT, NOT BECAUSE NOBODY HAS.**

The man of about thirty who sells nothing and has a use was told about it in Alder Reach on the tenth and read the entry, and the finding he put in the book in his own trade's paper that evening is that he has been right about four things for four months and has now been shown which one of them he was wrong about, and that **he was wrong about a price and right about a person, and that he printed both in his own type on the same sheet in April and did not notice that one of them was about a different day from the other.**

He came up to a counting room in Alder Reach on the eleventh of this month and said that he would not be thanked and that he was not going to argue about the four days, and then he argued about the four days for about eleven minutes anyway, and the entry says that he is the only man in this district who has never once been wrong about a date.

---

The train on that siding had stood a hundred and nineteen days on the tenth of this month. The figure is in a boy's hand in an index at Alder Reach, and beside it there are now five dates on one line, and the fifth of them is the twenty-ninth of this month, and **not one of the five has anybody's name against it, and the boy who keeps the index has been asked twice in nine weeks by people in a room whether that is an oversight and has said twice that it is not.**
