# Chapter 84: Beneath the Board

On the fourteenth of this month a boy of seventeen took the Harrow line book off a dry board on the wall of a signal hut nine miles west of Alder Reach, and while it was in his hands another thing came off the board with it, and he did not know for about a minute that it was a separate piece of paper.

He is Orren Pike. He is paid four coppers a quarter for an index and it is a standing item and not a service, and he is not Adrian Vale's apprentice and has said so in a yard in front of more than one person. The index has twelve stalls in it and the line book has eleven, and the difference is ninety-four yards of embankment with no signal in it, and the reason the number moved was a man with a tape in his pocket on his second morning because he had been told to walk the section twice a day.

What he was up there for on the fourteenth was a second copy. The ninth-place board went up on the eighth of this month and it quotes the line book in eleven lines, and a board that quotes a book nobody can see is a board that asks a stranger to take it on trust, and somebody in a counting room in Alder Reach said that sentence out loud on the ninth, and eleven coppers came out of the two hundred the ninth place had paid, and a man is binding the copy.

**And he got up there at about half past three in the afternoon and it took him five and a quarter hours with four changeovers in it, and he slept in a room over the counting office that has a bed in it and a window that shuts and has been empty for two years, and he was the only person in Alder Reach who had asked to go, and nobody sent him.**

The book was on a dry board above the stove because a book kept low in that hut gets damp in February. There were two ledgers on the board in clips, and behind the two ledgers, folded once, was a sheet of printed paper about the size of a large hand, and it came away with the book because it had been behind the book for longer than either of the ledgers had been on that board.

He put it on the table and went on copying for about forty minutes, and the fire was in and the window was grey, and the boy of nineteen was asleep on the bench with his boots off, and the keeper of the hut was out, and a man of thirty-nine came in at about ten with a lamp and looked at the two of them and went out again.

---

He did not open it until about a quarter to nine, when the lamp was lit and the rain had come on the west window, and he opened it because he was looking for something to lay the book on and the table had the ink, the string, a chisel and a loaf on it.

The keeper came in at about half past nine. She is fifty-one and she has held that hut for eleven years and she has held the only pair on that line for eleven years, and she had been down at the water column and up the bank in the wet, and she was in the room for about forty minutes while a boy of seventeen explained to her what he had found behind her own book.

"You have had that on your wall for eleven years," he said.

"I have had a lot of paper on that wall for eleven years."

"That one is a notice about your column and there is a mark on it where the name goes, and the office says it has never printed it."

She looked at it for about half a minute and handed it back, and then she said the thing that is in the book, and it is four sentences long and it is the finding of the whole of that night.

"There were four of those in my book in eleven years and I have carried every one of them and I have never asked anybody about any of them, and I did not write them down because I did not know what a person writes when they do not know. So I will tell you what I would have written if I had known. I would have written *the same shape twice*. **And I have been asked four times in eleven weeks whether I would hold something, and I have said yes three times, and I have never once been asked a question about a piece of paper on my own wall, and the reason is that a piece of paper is not a job, and a job is the only thing anybody in this district knows how to ask me about.**"

The boy of nineteen was awake for that. He lay on the bench with his boots off and his head on his arm, and he said one thing, and it is in the minute because nobody was there to take a minute and the keeper wrote it down herself afterwards and said so.

"He said, that is the one you said you were sorry about," the keeper wrote. "On the eighteenth of March. Not the iron. That." And then she wrote, in the same hand: *he says the iron is iron and that is a thing a person can be sure about, and he is nineteen and has never been sure of a thing in his life.*

---

---

And the sheet is a water office notice, and it is dated the second of the sixth year, and the office has no copy of it, and the mark on it is in the place where a name goes, and there is no name on it.

> **NOTICE. THE METERED COLUMN AT MILE ONE AND THREE QUARTERS.**
>
> **THE COLUMN IS METERED. READINGS ARE TAKEN QUARTERLY AND CARRIED TO THE RETURN. THE WATER IS NOT TO BE DRAWN BETWEEN SIX IN THE MORNING AND SIX IN THE EVENING WHILE THE PUMP IS WORKING.**
>
> **A PLACE IS A PLACE. A COUNTER IS A COUNTER. SET FOR THE NINTH PLACE.**

And above the fold line, where on a printed notice of that date a man's name or an office's name would go, there is an impression of a circle that has been broken in one place. It is about the size of a thumbnail. It is not printed with the text and it is not in the same ink, and it has been pressed into the paper rather than laid on it, and the paper around it is dry and brown and the impression is the same brown as the paper around it.

The finding entered on the sixteenth is that the impression is old and that nobody can say how old, and that the office's own paper is nine years old and the notice is eleven, and both are in the same state, and that the state of paper in this district is not a date.

---

**And three people said what it was on the afternoon of the fifteenth, and one of them was wrong, and one of them was right, and the one who was wrong got there first and is a woman whose whole trade is other people's documents.**

She is called Jessa Olwin and she is about thirty-eight and she buys old paper off four houses once a month, and the fifteenth is the fifteenth, and she was on that bank for her own trade with a hook and a sacking sheet and was in the hut for about twenty minutes.

"That is the water office's mark," she said. "It is a water office notice. Offices have marks. **Put it back and nobody will ever look at it again.**"

"No," said Petrus Nye, who is a bridge clerk of thirty-four, and who had come up with the office's second quarterly copy in a satchel, and who has the keys to a room over a counting office. "The water office has no mark. I have been in that office eleven years and I have read the standing instructions and I have the returns, and there is not one mark on one sheet out of that office, because a mark has to be cut and we have never had a die. Everything out of that office is a man's name and a man's hand and a man's initials in the corner, and I will put all three on this sheet in about a minute and you can go and look at them."

He did it in about ninety seconds and put four sheets down on the dry board, and all four had a man's name and a man's hand and initials in the corner, and the notice in Orren Pike's hand had none of them, and a circle broken in one place where a name would go.

The woman with the hook looked at the four sheets for about a minute and then said, "Then whose is it."

"I do not know," said the bridge clerk. "And I have written that sentence in this district's books before this month, and I wrote it about a pilot line, and I would like it noticed that a man who writes *I do not know* four times in three months is either the most honest clerk in this basin or the worst, and I have not worked out which and I have not asked anybody."

---

And the boy of seventeen was right about what it was and wrong about what it meant, and both halves went into the public lot book on the sixteenth, in two lines that were not joined, and the registrar's reason for keeping them apart is the reason she gives every time.

What it is: **an impression on a thing. Not a name, not a signature, not a letter, and not part of the notice.**

What it means: **that two impressions of one thing on two papers that have nothing to do with each other are evidence that one body made both. They are not. They are evidence that one thing was pressed into two things. Those are not the same statement, and the difference between them is the whole of what anybody in this district has ever been able to say about a mark.**

"That is a child's argument," said the woman with the hook. "Two nails are not the same nail."

"It is not a child's argument, it is a registrar's argument, and I am seventeen and I have been wrong in public four times in eleven weeks and I have written all four down." He put his hand flat on the table. "**And I still think it means the same thing made both of them, and I am not taking it back because a woman in a room told me I could not, and the reason I am not taking it back is that I am seventeen and I have been right four times in eleven weeks and I would like to be right a fifth time.**"

"You may think it," said the registrar. "You may not enter it."

---

And on the afternoon of the sixteenth she stopped him in a room with a door on it, in Alder Reach, nine miles from the hut, after he had walked it and got back in at about eight in the evening, and she said the thing that is now in the book in nine words and it is the fifth time she has said a version of it.

"Two things that arrived separately stay in two lines until somebody puts a thing on the paper that joins them. That is the reason, and it is the reason I give every time. I gave it in March about a name in a column of sixty-one. I gave it in April about a form with twenty-nine columns and not one of them a place. The first line is five impressions on five documents that have nothing to do with each other. The second line is a sixth, in a hut, on a board, on a notice about a water column. **The boy found it on the fourteenth of this month and the clerk could not account for it on the fifteenth and nobody has joined them and I am not going to.**

"And I am going to say the other thing, because it is the first time I have said it in a room instead of in a book. The first line has five impressions in it. The second line has one. And there is no name on the second one, in the place where a name goes, and that is on the sheet, and there is nothing else to say about it tonight."

"I do not agree," said the boy.

"**I know. That is why it is in the book in two lines and not in one, and that is the entire function of a book and it is the reason I have one.**"

---

**And he asked a second time in a yard on the evening of the sixteenth, standing up, and was refused in about four seconds, and the refusal is in the book, and he is seventeen and was not argued with, and the finding is that a second asking in the same month is not persistence and is not manners and is a thing a book exists for.**

"Can the two lines be printed on the same page."

"No."

"Why not. It is the same thing."

"You do not know that it is the same thing. You know that it is shaped the same. Those have been different since the eighth of March and I have said so four times and this is the fifth and it is the shortest."

And the answer he had in the first week of March is in the book and has not changed: **a book that joins two things because they are the same shape is a book that has made a claim about something it cannot check.**

---

---

**And what the district did with a second impression, on the eighteenth of this month, was nothing, and the nothing is in the book at length, and the registrar wrote it, and it is the fourth page of hers on that subject in nine weeks and she has said in public that she is the fifth person in this district who has written a rule about a page and not about a person.**

The page carries six impressions. It says what each is, where it is, who found it, when, and in what state of paper. It says that the first five are on unrelated documents and that the sixth is on a notice that relates to a water column at a place where there is a water column, and that the relation is a relation and not a claim. It says that nobody in this basin can find a body that made any of the six, and that the bridge administration has no die and never has had one, and that the ward market has a stamp and the stamp is a rectangle with a number in it, and that the Valuers' Guild has a seal and the seal is a circle and unbroken, and that the only circle anybody in this district has ever pressed into paper is a circle with a break in it.

And then, under all of that, four lines in her hand, and the third of the four is the reason this page exists.

> **A SECOND IMPRESSION IS NOT A SECOND ANSWER. I HAVE BEEN ASKED THREE TIMES IN TWO DAYS WHETHER THIS CHANGES ANYTHING AND THE ANSWER IS NO AND I AM GOING TO WRITE THE NO DOWN INSTEAD OF THE ARGUMENT, BECAUSE THE ARGUMENT IS SIXTY WORDS EVERY TIME AND THE NO IS TWO, AND SOMEBODY IN THIS DISTRICT WILL ONE DAY NEED A BOOK THAT HAS SAID NO IN A WAY THEY COULD FIND.**
>
> **AND WHAT THIS CHANGES IS ONE THING, AND IT IS THIS. IT CHANGES WHAT WE KNOW ABOUT WHERE THE FIRST ONE IS NOT. THE FIRST IMPRESSION IS ON FIVE DOCUMENTS IN FIVE ROOMS. THIS ONE IS IN A HUT. WE DID NOT KNOW THAT BEFORE THE FOURTEENTH OF THIS MONTH. THAT IS ALL THAT HAS HAPPENED AND IT IS NOT NOTHING.**

And the boy of seventeen read that page in a room in Alder Reach on the eighteenth, standing, and said one sentence about it, and the sentence is in the book because it was said in a room with a reader in it.

"It is the first page in this district that says *we were wrong about where it is not*, and it does not apologise for the eleven years."


And the ninth-place toll board has eleven sentences on the bottom line and not one of them is a complaint, and one of them is a question, and the question has been on the board since the ninth of this month and nobody has answered it, and the keeper who is supposed to answer the bottom line is thirty-nine and he is in an office and he says he cannot.

Three of the eleven are worth putting here because two of them are about the same two findings and the third is about the bushel of paint.

> **A MAN WHO HAS STOOD IN EVERY OFFICE IN THIS CITY SINCE THE EIGHTH OF MARCH SAYS THE NAME ON THE TOP OF THIS AND THE NAME IS NOT IN ANY OF THEM. I AM NOT COMPLAINING. I AM SAYING THAT HE IS RIGHT.**

> **I FOUND THIS BY ACCIDENT CARRYING A PAIL IN APRIL AND I TOLD NOBODY FOR A MONTH AND NOW I AM WRITING IT HERE BECAUSE SOMEBODY PAINTED IT.**

> **THE COLUMN IN THE MIDDLE IS EMPTY AND I HAVE BROUGHT MY SON TO LOOK AT IT AND HE ASKED ME WHY AND I SAID BECAUSE NOBODY CAN WORK IT OUT AND HE SAID THAT IS NOT THE SAME AS BEING SECRET.**

And the binding of the second copy of the line book came back down the bank on the eighteenth of this month, and it is a bound book, and it is not in Alder Reach and it is not in the ward market, and it is in the hut at the ninth place on a shelf beside a stove, and the finding entered on the eighteenth is that a book which is kept nine miles from the only office that keeps a copy of it is a book that can be lost, and that this district has decided that is the correct place for it, and that the reason is the same reason the board is there.

**And the second impression is in a hut nine miles west, on a document nobody in this city has ever seen, under the heading of a notice about a water column, and the office that printed the notice has no copy of it and has had it behind a book for eleven years, and nobody laid a stone, and nobody put a mark in a wall, and somebody pressed a circle into a piece of paper, and then the paper went behind a book.**
