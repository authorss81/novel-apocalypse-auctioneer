# Chapter 53: The Room With a Door On It

The second floor of the water office has four rooms on it and three of them are in use and the fourth has a door on it, and the door has a plate, and the plate says **STANDING ORDERS — DRAFT**, and the plate is the only thing on the door that has been cleaned in nine years.

Selik Marne went up that staircase on the morning of the fifth at about ten past eight, which is four minutes earlier than he has gone up it in nine years, because he was going up it with a book, and a man carrying a book does not walk at the speed of a man who has decided to go and look.

He had known about the room for the entire nine years. He could not have said, if anybody had asked him in the eighth year, what was in it. He knew there was something in it, and he knew it had been started in the third year, and he knew that it had been touched twice since, and that a fair copy of it had been made in the tenth year and not signed, and that was the whole of his knowledge, and he had arranged his professional life for nine years so as not to be the man who had to go and read it.

**The reason he gives is good.** A man who wants an office should not be the man who writes one. A man who has spent nine years telling a district that a crossing needs a permanent responsible office cannot also be the man who holds the paper, because the moment he holds the paper every reasonable man in the basin is entitled to ask him whether he wanted it, and he would have to say yes, and then the paper is a monument instead of a proposal. He said this to two review tables in nine years and it was accepted both times and it was, he understood on the morning of the fifth, **not a reason at all. It was the most careful piece of housekeeping he has ever done in his life, and it was done in a room he had never entered, about a document he had never read, and the housekeeping was the point.**

He went in. There is a table, four chairs, a press on a low shelf, a window that looks at the river wall, and about two hundred leaves of paper in four bundles, and the bundles are tied with tape, and the tape has gone brittle.

---

**He took three people and two of them were the wrong people and one of them was not supposed to be there at all, and he wrote all of that down at the top of the first page of his own hand before he opened anything.**

The water office clerk was **Ilse Markham**, who has been in that building eleven years and is not a Warden and has never claimed to be, and who is the person who has been told four times in nine years that the room is not to be touched and who has obeyed all four times without once asking why.

He took Nessa Pell because the standing order has to end up in a book and Lot 17 has the only book in this district anybody can take cold, and because she is a clerk and he wanted a clerk in the room who was not his.

He took Adrian Vale because Selik is a Warden and everything in that room is a Warden's, and because Adrian Vale is a reader, and because a draft that concerns an object ought to have one person in the room who is neither a Warden nor a Warden's clerk and who is also not the man who would profit.

**Adrian was not on the list of anybody. Selik added him in his own hand, with the time, and entered that the addition was his and not anybody's, and that if the addition was improper then the addition was improper in front of a witness, which is the only improvement anybody has been able to make to his method in nine years.**

"Before we open anything," he said, "I am going to say three things. Write them down and read them at the end as well as now.

"**One. I have never read this. Nine years. There is no version of that which makes me look careful and I am not going to stand in this room and pretend that I have been resisting it. I have not been resisting it. I have been walking past it.**

"**Two. Whatever is in here is a draft of my office, and not mine personally, and I am about to find out whether the difference survives contact with the paper, and I do not know the answer, and if the answer is no then the standing order of the ninth is a personal order and I have been hiding behind a word for nine years.**

"**Three. I expect to be argued with in this room, and I expect the person who does the arguing to be Mrs. Pell, and I have arranged the chairs for it.**"

---

**It is four drafts and it is seven years, and the four drafts were written by four men in three rooms, and not one of them knew the others existed.**

The first is from the third year and it is four pages and it is a good four pages. It proposes that one office be answerable for the crossing at St. Orra; that the office be staffed continuously; that it hold the instrument certificate; and that it be given the power to close the crossing on a stated ground.

The second is from the fifth year and is eleven pages and it is a good eleven pages. It has a timetable in it, which is the sentence that made Selik sit down on the edge of a table. It says the office is to be **continuously staffed and is not to lapse, is to be renewed by no body, and is to be continued until the crossing it administers ceases to be a crossing.** It is four sentences and it is the whole of the problem and nobody in seven years has struck four sentences out of anything.

The third is from the eighth year and is nineteen pages and it has a schedule. **A schedule of places.** St. Orra, the far receiving ramp, the two banks, the line-holder's post at the head of the service cut, the Ward Nine clinic, the lower terrace — eight places, each with a duty attached to it, and the duties are things a person could stand in the rain and do.

The fourth is a fair copy of the third, made in the tenth year, with three changes. One: the words *until the crossing ceases to be a crossing* are gone. Two: in their place is *for the duration of the need*, and **the duration of the need is not defined anywhere in nineteen pages, and Selik read that sentence three times and had to put his hand on the table.** Three: the closing power now reads in full, and here it is, and Selik read it out loud in a room that had a door on it:

> **THE OFFICE MAY CLOSE THE CROSSING, SUSPEND IT, OR REFUSE ANY PARTY TO IT, WITHOUT GIVING A GROUND, AND A CLOSURE WITHOUT A GROUND IS A SAFETY MEASURE, AND A SAFETY MEASURE IS NOT SUBJECT TO APPEAL.**

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds.

"That is mine," said Selik Marne.

---

**And that is where the chapter turns, and it turns on a sentence he gave a room in March of the second year, and the sentence is the March evacuation: *a span that reports a movement is a span that is shut, and I would rather be wrong in a yard than right in a coroner's room, and nobody is to tell me afterwards that I was cautious.***

He had said it in a yard in the rain to about two hundred people, and it was in the minutes, and it was the sentence that had ended an argument in his favour in about ninety seconds, and it was the sentence everybody in Alder Reach had repeated at each other for two years as the reason the span is safe.

**And it is now the closing clause of a document that would let one office shut the only crossing in this district forever, on no reason, with no appeal, and be right by definition, because the safety of a crossing is not a matter anybody is permitted to argue with.**

"I did not write it," Selik said. "I want that said first. **I did not write it and it is mine.**"

"It is yours," said Nessa Pell. "And I want to say the next thing, and you are going to hate it, and I am going to say it anyway because you gave me the chairs for it.

"**In the fifth week of this year that sentence cost a man his ankle.**"

"I know what it cost him."

"Then you know it is the sentence that is in that paragraph, and that a person standing in a yard saying it out loud on a bad day is a person being *careful*, and that a clause that says the office may do it forever without a ground is a person being given a permanent excuse. **You have been the best safety argument in this district for two years and somebody has written you a reward for it, and you did not ask for the reward, and the reward is in a room you never went into.**"

"I did not go into," said Selik Marne.

---

**And the answer to the room is four lines long and it is at the back of the fourth draft, on page nineteen, under a rule, in the hand of a man who died in the sixth year.**

> **THIS ORDER IS NOT SUBJECT TO REVOCATION BY ANY BODY EXISTING AT THE DATE OF IT, AND THE BODY WHICH MAY REVOKE IT IS CREATED BY THIS ORDER, AND IT CONSISTS OF THE ADMINISTRATOR AND TWO PERSONS NAMED BY HIM WHO HOLD NO OFFICE, AND IT MAY BE DISSOLVED ONLY BY THE BODY WHICH MAY REVOKE IT.**

Nine years. Four drafts, four authors, three rooms, no one told the next. And a document that cannot be ended by anybody, and that creates its own ending, and that the ending can only be done by the ending.

"There is no answer to that in this room," said Adrian Vale, who had said nothing for about forty minutes. "**There is no answer to that in any room. You cannot vote it out, you cannot repeal it, and the only way it goes is if the administrator simply stops.** And that means the whole of it is one man holding a piece of paper, and you have spent nine years telling this district that a limit checked by one man is a man and not a limit, and **this order is not even a man. It is a sentence with a man's name in it, and the sentence says the sentence does not end.**"

"Yes," said Selik Marne.

"So the thing you have been refusing to read is worse than the thing you have been refusing to sign, and I do not know which of us should be more frightened of that."

"Neither do I," said Selik. "**And I have been frightened of it for seven years and I did not know it and I want that written down as it is and not tidied.**"

---

**And then there was the name, and it is the reason the room changed, and it is the last thing in the four drafts that anybody found, and it was found because Adrian Vale walked to the press copy and read the head of the page, which is the oldest habit in his working life.**

The fair copy of the third draft carries a head, and the head names the authority the order rests on, and the authority is a pre-Fracture office, and it is named four times in nineteen pages in three different forms:

**THE STANDING OFFICE OF CROSS-DISTRICT PASSAGE. THE CROSS-DISTRICT PASSAGE OFFICE. THE OFFICE OF CROSS-DISTRICT PASSAGE (ESTABLISHED).**

"**CROSS-DISTRICT PASSAGE**," said Ilse Markham, from the window, in a voice that was not loud. "That is the head of the file. I typed it out in the eighth year. I have typed that head about nine hundred times for eleven years and I have never once been asked what it means and I did not ask, because it is a head on a file, and heads on files are the oldest thing in any office."

"Do we have it?" said Selik Marne. "The office. Do we have it in a register anywhere we can reach."

They looked. The register of pre-Fracture municipal authorities in that building is on a shelf and it is complete to within about a third, and it was complete to within a third in the year before the Fracture and had not been added to since, and nobody in the room could find a single line of it that said Cross-District, and there were four other *-District* entries and none of them was that one.

"No," said Selik Marne.

"**Then I am going to say the sentence I have not said in seven years, and I want it written down exactly.**" He put both hands flat on the table, and his hands were the hands of a man who has driven a heavy thing across a bridge for a living. "**My office has been citing a pre-Fracture authority that I have never been able to find, for seven years, in a document I have never read, and I stopped asking about it, and the asking stopped some time before the reading stopped, and by the time anybody put a piece of paper in my hand about it I had made a rule in my own head that the thing was too old to be worth a clerk's afternoon.** And it may well be too old. It may well be a thing that never existed. **And that is not a reason I am willing to put in a minute on my own office, because a man who cannot find the authority his rule rests on has not got a rule, he has got a habit with a seal on it.**"

"Then what do you want done?" said Nessa Pell.

---

**And the part of this that Selik Marne did not expect is on page eleven of the third draft, and it is a schedule of eight places, and when he got to it he had to stop reading for about half a minute and stand at the window.**

> **THE SCHEDULE OF PLACES, THIRD DRAFT, FIFTH YEAR. 1. ST. ORRA, THE SPAN AND ITS APPROACHES. 2. THE FAR RECEIVING RAMP. 3. THE EASTERN BANK, THE WARD NINE TRAM LINE AND THE DEPOT. 4. THE OLDER CENTRAL BANK, THE CLINIC AND ITS RECEIVING STATIONS. 5. THE HEAD OF THE SERVICE CUT, WHERE A LINE IS HELD. 6. THE LOWER TERRACE. 7. THE RIVER STAIRS, BOTH HEADS. 8. THE RIVER WALL, ABOVE AND BELOW THE WALL.**
>
> **AND ATTACHED TO EACH PLACE IS A DUTY, WRITTEN AS AN ACTION AND NOT AS AN OFFICE, AND THE DUTIES ARE: TO BE OPEN. TO COUNT. TO BE COUNTED. TO CARRY THE TOLL. TO HOLD. TO LIGHT AND TO BE SEEN. TO GO AND COME BACK. TO STAND ABOVE THE CUT AND BELOW IT AND TO SAY WHAT IS AND IS NOT GROUND.**

"It is good," said Selik Marne.

"It is very good," said Ilse Markham. "It is the only page in this bundle that I have ever read."

"**And it is a document about places.**" He put his finger on it and did not look round. "Nine years. Nineteen pages. Four drafts. Nine hundred leaves, or near enough. And the one page that is worth keeping is a list of eight places with a job against each, and it was written in the fifth year by a man whose name is on it, and it has been in a room for five years."

"Then it should be on a board somewhere," said Adrian Vale.

"It should be in a book somewhere," said Nessa Pell. "**And there is a boy of seventeen who asked me yesterday whether this book has ever had a place in it, and I told him it had not, and I was right, and I have been sitting in this room for an hour finding out what that is worth.** A charter is about a lot and a use and a bearer and a toll and a term. A count is about people. **The page on the desk in front of us is about a river, and neither of the two instruments this district has built can hold a river.**"

---

**What Selik Marne did about it took about nine minutes and it is in the book and it is the first thing he has done in nine years that anybody has called generous, and he did not call it that himself and was annoyed to be told.**

He withdrew the draft. He instructed Ilse Markham to take the four bundles off the shelf and tie them together with a new tape and write on the tape the date and the words **FOUR VERSIONS, SEVEN YEARS, NONE SIGNED**, and to send the bundle to the ward market's counter and to the notice board of Lot 17 and to the review table's file, and to keep one copy himself in his own office and not in that room. He instructed that the order be published with the finding of the fifth, which is that it cannot be ended by any body, and with the finding of the second, that its own closing clause is his own sentence of March, and with **the one paragraph he had not expected to find, which is the schedule of eight places on page eleven of the third draft, which is the only part of the four drafts that any of them ought to keep, and which is going to be read by somebody who has never seen it before somebody has to sign anything.**

And then he did the thing that made it publishable, and he did it because Ilse Markham refused it, and she refused it in one sentence and she was right.

He had begun, at about half past eleven, by instructing that the order be **withdrawn and rescinded**, and Markham had said: "**No, because then it is a piece of paper that a man took back, and in four years the district will believe a thing was never written, and the district will be wrong, and it will be wrong in the direction that is comfortable.**"

"Then it is not rescinded," Selik said. "It is **published**, and it is published with my name against it as the administrator who let it sit in a room for nine years, and with one objection in it that is mine, and the objection is on the fourth page, and the fourth page is the one about the word *need* not being defined, and I want the ward market to read the fourth page first and the nineteenth page last, because the fourth page is the sentence that lets it be argued about and the nineteenth is the sentence that stops anybody arguing."

**And he said one more thing at the door, to nobody in particular and to Nessa Pell in particular, at about twenty to twelve.**

"Mr. Vale. You have not read anything since the eighteenth of last month and everybody in this district knows the ledger and I have read it as well as anybody. Read the fourth page for me."

"No," said Adrian Vale.

"Why not?"

"**Because a reading is not a review, and because if I read it I become the man in this basin who has an opinion about whether that document is any good, and I have been the man with the opinion for five weeks and this district has had quite enough of him.** It goes on a board and eleven people read it out and Stenn puts a rate on it or he does not, and if I put my name on it then the only thing anybody will be able to say about it in a year is that the man who wanted a public lot in Alder Reach thought the Warden's permanent office was a good idea, and **they will be half right, and half right is the most dangerous thing a sentence can be.**"

Selik Marne looked at him for a moment with an expression that nobody in that room could read and that Ilse Markham, who has worked beside him for eleven years, described afterwards as *a man finding out that a thing he had expected to argue with had already given him more than the argument.*

"Mr. Vale," he said. "Then say the other thing, which is that the sentence in the fourth page has a **duration of the need** in it with no end, and that the Warden's own span has been shut since the twenty-first of the month before last and there is no word in this order for what happens to a duty when the thing it is duty for is shut."

**And Nessa Pell answered that, and it is the reason that answer is in the book at all, and she answered it without moving, standing by a table in a room with a door on it with her hands at her sides:**

"Because nobody has written the sentence, and because we have spent five weeks building an instrument for it and it is called a **standing term**, and it is seven days and it is renewed in public and it expires whether anybody remembers it or not, and **a duty with no end is the only thing in this basin that has never been in a book with a date on it, and the whole of the difficulty of that bundle of paper is that nobody wrote down what happens on Thursday.**"

And she said the last part with no particular weight at all, and it went in the minute, and Selik Marne read it twice, and then he took the bundles himself and carried them down the staircase himself, and **the room with a door on it was locked at about ten past twelve and the plate was taken off the door and put in the bundle, and there is a line in the book at the foot of the fifth which says that the room is now a room and the door has no plate on it and nothing else about it has changed.**

---

**And on the second landing he met the second fitter, who is not a Warden and has never been, and who suspended his certificate on the afternoon of the twenty-first of the month before last out of her own office, and who was annoyed not with him but with a slip of paper.**

"Six bundles," she said, looking at them.

"Four bundles and a press copy and a plate."

"Then it is five." She was carrying a toolbox and did not offer to help. "**I am going to say one thing to you on this landing and then I am going to go and fit a housing, Warden Marne, because the replacement housing is four weeks off and I am doing it in a shed, and I want whatever gets written down to say shed and not a room with a door on it.**

"Your department has a paper. I have read it — Markham lent it me on the nineteenth, which is the day you sent her up at two o'clock with two words on a slip, and I have been angrier about it since than about the pilot line, and I have not said so, and I am saying so now because you have come down a staircase carrying paper in front of a registrar and a reader and let these three know that a fitter has read it.

"**Clause four. *A closure without a ground is a safety measure and a safety measure is not subject to appeal.*** That is your March. I was in the yard in March. I was on the deck when you cleared it and I thought you were right, and I have thought you were right every day since, and I have also thought every day since that you are a man who would empty a deck in the rain over a bent float and you were **right** to. And I want it on the record, in front of these two, that the man I have agreed with for two years has written a paragraph that would let him do that for ever over nothing, and **I do not think you did it, and I do not think you would do it, and it is in a bundle, and I am going to have to look at it for the rest of my life.**"

Selik Marne stood on a landing with four bundles of paper under his arm and did not say one word for about six seconds.

"Put my objection to it on the front," he said.

"That is not mine to do."

"**It is going to be in your hands in ten minutes because I am taking it to a ward market counter, and if you want a sentence of your own in it then you will have to say it to me in the next four minutes and I will write it down and you will be in the book with it.**"

She said it in about fifty seconds and it is the fourth page and it is in the book exactly as she said it, and it is the fourth page that he told the ward market to read first, and nobody in Alder Reach has ever known that the paragraph on the front of the standing order of the ninth was written by a fitter on a staircase landing in about fifty seconds while holding a toolbox, and that is the correct order for it to be in.
