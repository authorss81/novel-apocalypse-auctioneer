# Chapter 52: A Place on the Stair

The top of the river stairs is not a place anybody would call the top of anything. It is a widening of a cut in the bank where a stone stair comes up out of the water line, and there is a wall on the east side of it about four feet high with a lamp-hook in the wall that a man unscrewed and screwed back eleven times in four years, and above the wall the ground goes flat for about twenty yards and then it stops being flat and goes up in steps toward two blocks of Alder Reach that are not in anybody's ward.

The stair is two hundred and sixty-one steps and it took the engineers who built it, who are dead, a number for it that is on a plate at the bottom. Fen Ambrose has never once been to the bottom.

**Nessa Pell's standing failure, entered in the book on the nineteenth of the month before this one, is that the person holding the light at the top of the river stairs can see nine feet up a stair and cannot see the turn.** The turn is at a hundred and forty steps, where the stair goes behind the wall and comes out thirty yards along facing the wrong way, and the reason for the failure is not the light. The reason is that from the top of a stair with a lamp you can see a person for the nine feet below you and then the stair is a wall.

So on the morning of the third of this month there were two lamps at the top, which was a thing that had never been done, and the second one was at the head of the turn and it belonged to Oswin Tace, who is forty-one and holds the lamps on the Ward Nine line and who volunteered for the job knowing what it was.

"You are the light and the count," Fen said, on the flat bit, at about ten past four in the morning.

"I am the light and the count," Oswin Tace agreed, "and I want it said now, while we are both awake, that I have never taken a count of people before and I have only ever taken a count of lamps, and there are thirty-one lamps on that line and I could tell you which of them are lit and I could not tell you a man from a coat."

"Then say it there as well."

"I will say it there as well, and it will be in the book, and I would like it read out afterwards in case anybody upstairs is frightened of a number that came off a lamp-man." He was setting the second lamp on the wall at the head of the turn. "**And I would like one thing added, and Mrs. Pell wrote it down when I said it, which is that the second lamp is the whole of the difference and there are two of them because I asked, and nobody has costed the second one.**"

---

**The plan was three days long and it was Fen Ambrose's plan and it was on a sheet and it had been read aloud to nine people at the exchange on the second and was on the page in the book, and here is the whole of it as it stood at ten past four in the morning on the third.**

> **THE PASSAGE, THE THIRD TO THE FIFTH OF THIS MONTH. THE STAIR IS A WALK AND NOT A CROSSING. ONE PERSON AT A TIME. ABOUT THREE HOURS DOWN AND ABOUT THREE HOURS UP AND BOTH IN DAYLIGHT, AND A PERSON WHO IS ON THE STAIR AT DUSK IS ON THE STAIR AT DUSK AND THE COUNT IS PUBLISHED IN THE MORNING WHETHER IT CLOSES OR NOT.**
>
> **THE COUNT IS SEVENTEEN AND THE SEVENTEEN ARE THE SIX CLINIC HANDS AND THE ELEVEN OFF THE ROLL WHO WENT OVER ON THE EIGHTEENTH OF LAST MONTH AND THE COUNT IS ENTERED AS A COUNT AND NOT AS A HOUSEHOLD.**
>
> **THE LIGHT IS HELD AT THE TOP BY ONE PERSON AND THE COUNT IS THAT PERSON'S. THERE ARE NOW TWO LAMPS.**
>
> **AND THE PLAN CONTAINS A STEP WHICH READS: *ANYBODY WHO DOES NOT WISH TO BE COUNTED WILL BE ASKED, AND A DECLINE GOES IN THE COUNT AS A DECLINE.***

"That last one," said Oswin Tace, "has no hour on it."

"It has no hour on it."

"That is the step nobody did."

"Yes," said Fen Ambrose. "And I have been arguing for it for nine years, and I did not notice that I had written it as a favour."

---

**The first two went down at about a quarter past five in the morning and the count was taken going up.**

There is not much to say about a two-hundred-and-sixty-one-step stair three times, which is the sentence every person who has done it says to you beforehand and the only sentence that is true, and the reason it is true is that the stairs do the work and the doing of the work is the same every time. The wall on the east side is about four feet high at the top, where the lamp-hook is, and it is nine feet high at the hundred-and-fortieth step, where the stair turns behind it and comes out thirty yards along. Below the turn it is a cut with a river at the bottom of it and a smell of cold stone and, from about the eightieth step down, a smell of something sweet that nobody has ever satisfactorily explained. There is a kink at a hundred and ninety that puts a person's weight wrong and every person who has done it knows the kink and nobody has ever named it. There are eleven places on that stair where a person can stand off the steps and put a hand out and let somebody go by, and they were made for that, and there is no writing on the wall about it, and on the fourth of this month about four people leaned on three of them.

The first two went down at a quarter past five and were at the bottom at about half past eight, and the fog that morning came up off the water at about the hundredth step and stayed to the top, so that the people above could not see anybody coming up for the last hour and a half and the people coming up could not see the wall until they were on it. That is the whole of the difficulty of a stair, and it is not the light. **It is that the top of a stair in fog is a place where you cannot tell a person from the last twenty feet of nothing.**

> **AND IT IS ENTERED BECAUSE IT WAS THE FOG AND NOT THE STAIR: ON THE FOURTH OF THIS MONTH THE STAIR COULD NOT BE COUNTED FROM ITS TOP BY ANY PERSON IN THIS DISTRICT AT ANY HOUR, WITH ONE LAMP OR WITH TWO, BECAUSE A PERSON APPEARS OUT OF FOG AT ABOUT EIGHT FEET AND THE PERSON IS VISIBLE FOR EIGHT FEET. THE ANSWER WAS NOT A LAMP. THE ANSWER WAS A PERSON AT THE BOTTOM SAYING THE NAME ALOUD WHILE THE PERSON IS STILL OUT OF SIGHT, SO THAT THE COUNT IS TAKEN ON A VOICE AND NOT ON A FIGURE.**

That is in the book too, and it was the first time anybody in this district had written down that a count can be taken on a sound.

That was the whole of the design and it was not Fen's original design. Her original design had been to take the count going down, on the far side, where the eleven people and the dog were and the floor was and somebody could be asked a question. The original design was abandoned on the second of the month at about eleven in the evening in a kitchen under the Ward Nine tram line, by a woman who said one sentence about it.

"You cannot count seventeen people on a floor where four of them are not allowed to be asked," said Bett Ruhl. "**I have counted plates in this kitchen for thirty years and I can tell you that a number taken in a room where some people cannot speak is not a smaller number. It is a different number, and it is the number of the room.**"

So the count was taken at the top, going up, by a lamp-man who had counted lamps, and the people going up were called out by the names on the roll and the names were called out because a name on a roll can be read aloud and a person on a stair in the dark can be answered to. Two names on the roll belonged to households. The other nine were on the roll as the roll of the shelter group, which is a count and not a household, and the steward of that roll is Fen Ambrose, and a steward counts the group and may not count the people in the group who are not in a household unless they ask.

The nine went up. The two went up. The six clinic hands went up, one at a time, with their cold boxes empty and their hands free, and Oswin Tace called every one of them by the name on the roll and got an answer from all of them, because all of them were asked.

**And then the count was seventeen, and the count was correct, and the count was wrong.**

---

**The person on the ramp was a woman of about forty and she had not been asked to come up and she was not on any list in any book in Alder Reach as a person, and she had a place on the roll, and the place was on the stair, and the place had come up.**

Her name is entered as **not asked**, and that is the whole of the entry, and the reason it is entered that way and not as a person is that she has not given a name and nobody asked her for one, and because she is in a household and the household is on the roll, the roll's steward counted the household's place and not the household's second person, and **that is not a mistake. That is the rule, working exactly as it was written, and Fen Ambrose wrote it, on the second of the month before this one, at a table in a room with a candle on it, and argued for four lines of it.**

**The count going up is in the book step by step because a count is only worth anything if a stranger can walk it, and this is the one in this district that anybody could walk.**

Two hundred and sixty-one steps. The two household names were called from the bottom by the man at the bottom, a lamp-man's mate called Obadiah Fenn who does the river end and who has four hundred and six lamps in his own count and who was told twice that the count was not his job, and who then kept it perfectly, and who said afterwards that he had been frightened of getting it wrong and that the fear had been useful.

Then nine off the roll, in the order the roll had them, which is the order they had been in since the twenty-sixth of the month before last and which is not the order anybody would have chosen, and which the roll's steward has never sorted because **the roll is the order they stood in a queue and the queue is part of the count.**

Then six clinic hands with empty boxes, and Oswin Tace got all seventeen and every one of them answered, and the last of the clinic hands was up at about ten past three in the afternoon and could not get the last eleven steps and was helped by two people, which is entered, because **a count that does not record the helping is a count of people who walk up steps.**

And the number of voices was seventeen, and the number of figures that came out of the fog at the top was seventeen, and they matched, and the two men who had been counting said so, and it was the fourth of the month and about a quarter past three in the afternoon with a wind getting up, and everybody in that yard thought the hard part was over.

"I argued that a group may be a household or may not," she said, later, to a yard, "and that writing the group means every household in it and also every person in it who is not in one of the households, and I said the second half was the half that had never been written in this city and I was **right about that and wrong about the mechanism.** I thought the half that had never been written was about people who are not in a household. **It is about the second person in a household, and that is a person I invented an exception for and then never asked anybody to use.**"

The plan's step read: *anybody who does not wish to be counted will be asked.* And there was no hour on it, and the hour was the whole thing, and the hour was missing because a plan is a thing that can be given a date and asking is not a date. Nobody had asked because there was nobody whose job it was to ask, and the whole of the district's last five weeks of argument about who counts whom had been conducted by people who were free, on a good day, in a room, with time in it.

**So the count that was published in the morning on the fourth of this month was seventeen, and it was right, and one of the seventeen lines on that roll stood for a house with two people in it, and the other one of them was standing at the top of a ramp in the dark two hundred and sixty-one steps below with a blanket over her shoulders and no idea that anybody was counting her at all.**

---

**The rule that came out of it is one sentence long and it is in the book in Fen Ambrose's own hand, and it is the second sentence the count rule has ever had.**

> **A STEP IN A PLAN THAT ASKS SOMEBODY A QUESTION IS NOT A STEP UNTIL IT HAS AN HOUR AND A PERSON, AND A STEP WITH AN HOUR AND A PERSON IN IT IS THE ONLY THING IN THIS DISTRICT THAT HAS EVER WORKED.**
>
> **THE FIFTH OF THIS MONTH, AT SIX IN THE MORNING, IN A KITCHEN UNDER THE WARD NINE TRAM LINE, THE HOUR WAS GIVEN: A QUARTER OF AN HOUR BEFORE THE FIRST LAMP, EVERY DAY OF A PASSAGE, SOMEBODY WALKS THE RAMP AND ASKS EVERY PERSON WHO IS IN NO HOUSEHOLD AND EVERY PERSON IN A HOUSEHOLD WHOSE PLACE IS ON THE ROLL, AND THE ANSWER OR THE SILENCE GOES IN THE COUNT AS AN ANSWER OR AS *NOT ASKED*, AND A SILENCE IS NEITHER A YES NOR A NO.**

She gave it the hour and she gave it herself. Then she went down the stair, which takes about three hours, and stood on a goods-shed floor on the far bank at about half past ten in the morning with a sheet in her hand, and asked one woman one question, and the woman said yes, and then said that she would want to know whether the yard had asked the others too, and Fen said that it had not and would not, and that nobody was going to be asked anything she did not want to answer, and that the count would say *not asked* about everybody else and that nobody would ever be able to tell which of them had not been asked and which had not been there, and **the woman said that was the first time anybody had offered her that and that she would be up on the fifth.**

---

**And on the fourth, in the morning, the count was published, and the count was seventeen, and it was not struck.**

That is the rule and it is in the book and it is the reason the fourth of this month is a page anybody can go and read. The wrong count and the right count go in the same entry with the reason underneath, because a book with one number in it and a stair with the other number in it is two authorities and two authorities end in a fight about which of us is lying and neither of us will be lying.

And so:

> **PUBLISHED THE FOURTH OF THIS MONTH IN THE MORNING, WHETHER IT CLOSED OR NOT: SEVENTEEN UP. THE COUNT WAS CORRECT WHEN IT WAS PUBLISHED AND IT WAS WRONG WITHIN FOUR HOURS OF BEING PUBLISHED, AND THE REASON IS UNDERNEATH IT AND THE REASON IS NOT A MISTAKE.**

**Rutt read it at the ward market counter at six and again at nine, in the words people did not bring with them, and the rule of the counter held and one man brought in a slip on which somebody had written the number seventeen and underlined it twice and said that seventeen was the number of children in the shelter group and therefore the number of people, and Rutt read him the four lines, and he did not argue, and he said, "Then somebody has written seventeen twice in two books and both of them are right," and Rutt said that was the first correct thing anybody had said at that counter all week about a number.**

Then it was read in a yard at about eleven with about a hundred and forty people in it, and it was read by Bett Ruhl, who cannot read, and who was given it by Nessa Pell and who read every word of it including the line about the fog, and stopped twice, once at the phrase *a count can be taken on a sound* and once at the phrase *which is a person the rate has never met*, and after the second one she looked up for about four seconds and then went on, and nobody in that yard has ever asked her what those four seconds were and Bett Ruhl has volunteered them exactly once, to a cook, in a kitchen, and what she said was: *"That is me. That is the two blocks west and it is me, and I have never once been on a rate sheet in this city and I have paid the four coppers every quarter for nine years into a fund that made no paper."*

**The count was not struck, and neither was the seventeen, and the two are in the book with the reason underneath, and on the fifth of this month the entry was added to and the number became eighteen and the addition says who added it and on what authority, and the authority is a plan with an hour in it.**

---

**And the money is the last part of it and it is the part that nobody in the district has a place to put, and it is entered in three columns because that is what is done.**

One day of eleven people walking a stair and carrying what they carried: food for a day, oil for two lamps at the top and one at the bottom, and the keeping of a count.

The three columns were kept by three people and none of them spoke to the other two, which is the practice, and the middle column is where the argument was supposed to be and on this day the middle column had nothing in it. Ivo Stenn was asked for a sheet and had to go and look for one and came back with his own face.

"I have got nothing," he said. "**I have not got a sheet for that ground because there has never been a trade in those two blocks in my book, and there cannot be, because they are not in a ward, and a market cannot levy a household that is not in a ward, and I have known that for six years and I have never once had to write it down.**"

"Then write down that you have never had to write it down," said Marda Osk.

"That is a different sentence and it is true, and I would rather not have said the first one in a room."

> **FORTY-FOUR, on the payer's own figure. NO SHEET, on the ward market's rate sheet, and the reason is that the ward market has never had a rate sheet for the two blocks west of the depot. FIFTY-EIGHT, in the room.**
>
> **AND THE MIDDLE COLUMN IS THE FINDING AND NOT A BLANK: THOSE TWO BLOCKS ARE NOT IN A WARD. THEY ARE IN NO WARD'S BOOK AT ALL, AND THE STAIR COMES UP AT THE BOTTOM OF THEM, AND A PERSON WHO LIVES AT THE TOP OF A STAIR THAT IS THE ONLY WAY OUT OF THEIR OWN TWO BLOCKS IS A PERSON THE RATE HAS NEVER MET.**
>
> **AND A RATE BUYS A SERVICE AND DOES NOT BUY A SEAT, AND A RATE CANNOT BE VOTED FOR A PLACE THAT IS NOT IN A WARD, AND NOBODY HAS ASKED THE PEOPLE IN THE TWO BLOCKS WEST WHETHER THEY WOULD LIKE TO BE IN A WARD.**

And the second lamp is entered separately, and it is the only line in that entry that anybody argued about, and the argument was not about the cost. It was about whether a second lamp is a service or an instrument, and it is a service, and here is why, and the why is Bett Ruhl's and it is in her words:

"**A thing you need to do the job is an instrument and you buy one and you lose it and you buy another. A thing that has to be there for other people to be able to do anything at all is a service, and a service is somebody's job, and the somebody has a name and a wage and a day off.** The second lamp is a woman with a name and eleven coppers a week and the two of us are building an argument about whether we have to pay her, and if we do not pay her then she is a lamp, and I have been a lamp in three kitchens in this city and I know exactly what that is."

Nobody has paid the eleven coppers. It is entered as unpaid and not as nil, and there is a line under it that says the entry will be read again in a month, and it was read again in a month.

---

**And at about four in the afternoon on the fourth, on a goods-shed floor on the far bank, with the count at seventeen and the addition to it not made yet, and the second lamp still unpaid for, Fen Ambrose was asked where the water comes from.**

It was the woman who had said yes, and she asked it because they had been talking about the eleven and the dog and the floor and the fact that a floor with eleven people on it and no crossing has one tap, and that the tap works, and that it has worked every day since the plank went into the service cut.

"Where does the water come from?" Fen said.

"Down the line and up," said the woman. "**Nine miles west. There's a column at Sarrow Water and a siding and a hut and about thirty of us, and the column's been metered to a road house in Alder Reach for as long as anybody can remember, and the water comes down the line in a pipe you can walk beside, and it comes up the stair in a pail, and it is the only reason this floor has anybody on it.**"

"Who is the road house?"

"That," said the woman, "is on a paper in Alder Reach and not in this room, and I have been on this floor since the thirteenth of the month before last and I have not asked anybody for three weeks, and I am asking you now because you are the first person who has come down here since the plank went and asked me a question I could answer no to."

Fen Ambrose wrote it down on the sheet, in the margin, in her own hand, and she did not know then what it was worth and she has never been sure, and the margin of that sheet is in the book with the number on the sleeve and the number on the sheet, and the entry at the foot of the page in the registrar's hand reads:

> **ENTERED THE FOURTH OF THIS MONTH, IN A MARGIN, BY F. AMBROSE: THAT THE WATER ON THIS FLOOR COMES NINE MILES WEST ON THE NEEDLE LINE FROM A PLACE CALLED SARROW WATER, AND THAT ITS COLUMN HAS BEEN METERED TO A ROAD HOUSE IN ALDER REACH FOR AS LONG AS ANYBODY CAN REMEMBER, AND THAT NOBODY IN THIS DISTRICT HAS ASKED ANYBODY AT SARROW WATER ANYTHING. AND THE ENTRY IS ENTERED AS ASKED, ON THE FOURTH, BY FEN AMBROSE, AND IT IS THE FIRST TIME IN FIVE WEEKS THAT A QUESTION WENT OUT OF THIS BOOK AND LANDED SOMEWHERE ELSE.**
