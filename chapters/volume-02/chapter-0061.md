# Chapter 61: The Six-Foot

The tenth hand of the signal crew put a piece of chalk on the rail at the fourth place at about seven in the morning on the twenty-second of this month, and Tarin Kest said *don't*, and he took the chalk off the steel himself, and it took him about four seconds, and it is the first four minutes of the only rehearsal anybody in this district has ever held for a date it gave itself.

The sheet is nailed up on eleven boards and it was read out at a ward market counter on the morning of the twenty-second and again in the yard on the twenty-first to about four hundred people in a warm rain, and this is the second version of the sheet and the first version is not struck.

> **THE WEST END IS TO BE WALKED ON THE TWENTY-NINTH OF THIS MONTH, FROM THE FOURTH PLACE TO THE NINTH, ON FOOT, ON THE BALLAST, IN DAYLIGHT, AND NOBODY PAST THE SECOND RAIL ON A STRAIGHT AND NOBODY INSIDE THE FOUR-FOOT ON A CURVE.**
>
> **THE COUNTS ARE TO BE TAKEN GOING AND COMING.**

"**On the ballast,**" said the tenth hand, "**and that is the whole of what is wrong with it, and I put the word in, and I read it out in a yard in the rain, and The record is to show that I put the word in.**"

Because the ballast on this line is the ground between the two rails, and that ground is called the four-foot, and the four-foot is four feet and a bit across on a straight, and it is soft in nine places between the fourth place and the first, and on a curve the inside of the curve is where the ballast stands highest, because a curve throws the stones outward and they bank up against the inside rail.

And the fourth place is on a curve. It is on the last of the embankment before the line comes down onto the bank, and the curve there is tight enough that a man standing outside it can see the inside of it, and the ballast on the inside of that curve was up to the tenth hand's chest when he put a tape in it on the ninth of this month and wrote a sentence on the back of his hand about it.

"Nobody inside the four-foot on a curve," he said. "**And the walk begins at the fourth place, and the fourth place is a curve, and the walk is on the ballast, and the ballast at the fourth place is the four-foot.** So the sheet forbids the walk at the first step, and I did not see it, and I have written four hundred words about the distance on a curve and I did not see the four words at the top of my own sheet."

Tarin Kest got the chalk back and looked at the ground for a while.

"**Then you are not walking on the ballast,**" he said.

"There is nowhere else to walk on a railway."

"You are walking on the **six-foot**," said Tarin. "**The six-foot is between the running rail and the cess. It is six feet wide on a straight on this line and it is the only place on a railway a man is meant to be standing, and it goes round the outside of a curve because the outside of a curve is where the cess is and the cess is always there. And on this line the six-foot has a cess on the outside of it and nothing on the inside of it at the fourth place, because the inside of the fourth is a wall of ballast four feet high with nowhere for a man to go.**"

He put his boot on the ballast of the inside curve and looked east down the straight.

"And I will tell you the other half of it before you write anything down. **The six-foot is not level. On a straight it is level and it is a foot and a bit below the top of the rail, and on a curve it climbs, because the whole track climbs, and where it climbs it is stepped in places with old setts that have not been re-laid since before the water came.** So the six-foot is a walkable place for about a mile and a half and then it is a place where a man of fifty puts a hand out."

---

**And so the four of them walked five miles of it in about six hours on the morning of the twenty-second, which is a rehearsal, and there is no word for a rehearsal in this district yet, and the tenth hand entered the word.**

> **REHEARSAL: A WALK DONE BEFORE THE DAY IT IS FOR, ON THE SAME GROUND, BY SOME OR ALL OF THE PEOPLE WHO WILL DO IT, FOR THE PURPOSE OF FINDING OUT WHETHER THE WALK CAN BE DONE. THE FIRST ONE IN THIS DISTRICT. ENTERED BY THE TENTH HAND, WHO HAD NOT GOT A WORD FOR IT AND HAS NOW.**

The fourth person was a woman of forty-five from the ninth place who has never given her name to anybody in Alder Reach, and she was on the ground because the sheet says so, and she had walked the length of it four times in her life and could not read.

"**I have not been asked what I am going for,**" she said, at the third place, where they stopped for a moment, "**and I have answered it in a yard in front of four hundred people, and my answer is on a sheet, and I want to say one more thing about it out here where there are four of us instead of four hundred.**

"**That clause is about a person who holds a thing and has to stop and wait. And I have been thinking about it for a day, and there is no thing on this line that anybody out here holds.** The man at the hut holds a lever. The man in Alder Reach holds a book. The man who runs the water is a road house and it does not hold a thing, it pays for one. **So I have written a clause about a lever and I am going out on a ballast in walking shoes, and if that is a foolish place to have put it then I put it in a foolish place and I would rather it was on a sheet than in my head.**"

Nobody argued with her. Tarin Kest, who is forty-three and has said no to this district about nine times in five weeks, said, "**Then say it on the ballast, and it will be on the ballast, and there will be four of us and one of us will stop.**"

---

**And they found the four places where the six-foot stops being a six-foot, and the tenth hand marked all four, and the fourth one is the one that matters.**

There is a changeover at each place where the cess opens out, and the cess is where the water goes, and the cess is not flat. The first is two hundred yards past the fourth, in the open, and it is four feet deep and about two feet wide at the bottom and it has standing water in it in the morning. The second is a brick culvert at the fifth with a headwall either side of it and eleven inches of silt across the whole width. The third is at the seventh place, where the cess is interrupted by a length of green pipe lying across it that somebody put there before the water came and nobody has ever taken away.

**The fourth is at the eighth, and it is the worst of the four, and it is a step down of two feet six inches into about a foot of standing water, and Tarin Kest went down it in a walking shoe and came up with the shoe full.**

"Right," he said. "**Nobody at the eighth goes down there. They go round on the outside of the cess on the shoulder, which is four feet wide and has a drop of nine feet off it on the river side, and it is a place where a man walks facing the drop because there is nothing else to face.**"

"Write that down," said the woman. "**Write down that I said that I walk facing the drop, because a man of forty-three in good boots says the same thing and a woman of forty-five in walking shoes needs it printed.**"

It is printed. It is in the sheet, in her words, and the tenth hand entered that it was said by the fourth person of the four and that he was not going to attribute it to a trade or a seat.

---

**And between the eighth place and the ninth place there is a post, and it is not a signal, and nobody on that line has looked at it in this district's lifetime.**

It stands on the cess side in a concrete socket with about a foot and a half of it above the ballast, and it is octagonal, and it has a number cut into the top of it in a face that has had forty-odd years of weather on it and about two hundred winters' worth of somebody's boot heel.

"It is a boundary post," said the tenth hand. "**It is a section post, and it is not a place post, and it is not a signal, and the difference between those three things is the whole of this district's problem and I have got my hand on it.**"

"How do you know the difference?" said Stenn.

"**Because a place post says a name and a section post says a number of a thing and a signal post says a lamp and a frame.** And nobody in this basin has ever read this one and it has been here the whole time, and the line book has got a column for the lamp and a column for the place and the column for the number of a thing, and there is no column for the number of a thing."

"What number is it?"

He wiped the boot heel off the top of it with his thumb and read it, and it is in the book on the twenty-second in his own hand with the finding under it.

> **ONE MILE AND THREE QUARTERS FROM THE EAST END OF THE SECTION. WHICH IS THE LINE BOOK'S FIGURE FOR THE NINTH PLACE, AND THE LINE BOOK'S FIGURE FOR THE NINTH PLACE IS A MILE AND THREE QUARTERS, AND THE POST SAYS THE SAME, AND NOBODY HAS EVER COMPARED A POST WITH A BOOK.**

"So the line already knows where this place is," said the woman of forty-five. "**It has had a number on it since before the water and nobody ever asked the number to say anything.**"

"The number is the post's job," said the tenth hand. "**And that is the trouble, and it is not this morning's trouble, and I am going to write it on the back of this page and not on the front, because it is a thing I have got to think about for a week.**

"**A mark may not carry a job.** That is our own rule and it is a good rule and Fen Ambrose fought for it and it is the reason a protected person has not been made into an officer of this district by accident. And a post with a number cut in it is a mark. Not a person's name — a mark is a number where a name was refused — **and the post carries the only job the line has ever given it, which is to say where this section ends, and it has been saying it for forty-odd years, and under our own rule it is not allowed to.**"

"And who is going to hold the end of the section?" said Stenn.

"**Nobody knows,**" said the tenth hand, "**and the answer to that question is what the twenty-ninth is for, and I have got seven days to find out whether there is an answer that is not a mark.**"

---

**And at about half past ten they came to the sixth place, and the tenth hand stopped, and he stopped for a reason he had been walking toward for five miles and had not said out loud.**

The drain comes off the top of the bank at the sixth place and it has been coming off the top of the bank since before the Long Fracture, and it goes onto the ballast, and it has been going onto the ballast since before anybody alive in this district was born, and it is the reason the top of that bank is the best ground for two miles, and the reason that is a good thing is not the drain, the drain is a bad thing, and the reason the ground is good is that the water that comes down the valley has been going somewhere for a very long time, and the somewhere was here.

The tenth hand put his hand flat on the ballast and looked at it and then looked down the cess, and the cess was running, and it was running brown and about four inches deep, and it came out from under the cess lip about a foot below the six-foot and went along the ballast for about a yard and a half and then went into a hollow and did not go anywhere else.

"That is what takes the water off that bank," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"**It has been taking it off that bank since before the water came, and I have walked past it eleven times since the nineteenth of last month and I have never once looked down, and it is because it is not a signal and there is nothing on a signal to see.**"

And Ivo Stenn, who is a freight clerk and had been walking for three hours without speaking because he had been given an instruction about counting, crouched down in the six-foot and put a finger in the four inches of brown water on the ballast.

"**I am going to have to put this in a rate sheet,**" he said, "**and I have got a piece of paper in my coat and there is nothing on it yet, and I am standing on the water.**"

---

**And the fourth-place stall was measured for the third time in twenty-two days, and it came out the same.**

The tenth hand had a thirty-yard tape and a stick and the ballast at the west end of the stall and the ballast at the east end of it, and the marks were his own, put there with chalk on the ninth and on the eighteenth, and he measured it in about eleven minutes with the woman of forty-five reading the figures out loud because he asked her to and because he has never once asked anybody to do anything and had not known until the twenty-second that it was available.

"**Three hundred and sixty-one yards,**" she said.

He wrote it down and put the tape away and then stood for a moment with his back to them.

"Three hundred and forty-four on the ninth. Three hundred and sixty-one on the eighteenth. **Three hundred and sixty-one again this morning, and I am the same man with the same tape and the same two chalk marks, and a number that repeats is a number anybody can check.**"

"You want it to have moved," said Tarin Kest.

"**I have been telling this district for three weeks that I do not know which of those two numbers is the lie, and that has been the honest position, and it has been the honest position because a number that frightens me is a number I have to keep going back to look at.** And it is three hundred and sixty-one twice, on a day when I can find nobody who has seen a train go through that ground since the eighteenth, and so the stall has not moved in eleven days, and that is the first figure I have had in a month that I can put a stranger's hand on."

He wrote it on the page and turned the page over, and on the back there were two other numbers, and the two other numbers were not a number at all, and he read them out because he reads everything out.

> **THE STALL AT THE FOURTH PLACE, THREE HUNDRED AND FORTY-FOUR YARDS ON THE NINTH. THREE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-ONE ON THE EIGHTEENTH. THREE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-ONE ON THE TWENTY-SECOND. THE NUMBER THAT REPEATED WAS THE ONE SAID SECOND, AND I HAVE SAID SO OUT LOUD TO FOUR PEOPLE BEFORE I WRITE IT IN, BECAUSE A NUMBER THAT REPEATS ONCE IS NOT A RULE AND A NUMBER THAT REPEATS TWICE IS A FACT, AND I AM NOT GOING TO LET THE SECOND ONE GO IN A BOOK WITHOUT SAYING IT TWICE FIRST.**

---

**And they came up the bank at about a quarter past one in the afternoon and the ninth place was there, and the hut was there, and a woman of fifty was at the column with two pails and a boy of about nine was holding the second one, and the woman of forty-five went down the bank first and did not go into the hut.**

She stood on the hard ground for about two minutes and looked at the train, which is nine vehicles and has been on that siding since the eleventh of last month, and she said one thing about it, and she said it out loud and in the first person, which is the standing practice at that place and is not the practice of any book in Alder Reach.

"**Thirty-nine days,**" she said. "**I have counted them off the calendar on the chapel wall and the count is right, and it is the only number in this place that anybody has got right in nine years.**"

Then they turned round at the ninth place and came back east, and at about a quarter to three the drain at the sixth place was four inches deep and running, and nobody said the word, and the four of them stood in the six-foot at the sixth place for about a minute and a half and then went on, and Ivo Stenn took the piece of paper out of his coat at the third place and wrote on it, in the order he had thought of it, which is a freight clerk's order and not a railwayman's.

> **THE SIX-FOOT. RUNNING WATER ON THE BALLAST AT THE SIXTH PLACE, FOUR INCHES, OUT OF A DRAIN OFF THE TOP OF THE BANK. THE BANK IS THE GROUND BEHIND THE COLUMN AT THE NINTH PLACE. I HAVE WRITTEN IT DOWN BECAUSE SOMEBODY WILL ASK ME WHAT THE NINTH PLACE COSTS TO BE A PLACE AND I DID NOT KNOW AND NOW I HAVE ONE ITEM OF IT AND THE ITEM IS A DRAIN.**

---

**And Ivo Stenn had come out that morning with the freight clerk's list in his head, because that is the only shape a market has: a street, a number of houses on it, a number of people in the houses, a rate on each house. He had it ready at the fourth place and he read it off the top of the embankment and got through two items and stopped at the third.**

"There is nobody at the fourth place," he said. "**There is a lamp on a post and a nameplate that says Harrow and there is no keeper and there has not been a keeper since the flood year because there is nothing coming from that end, and the reason there is a nameplate is that the plate is the thing and not the place.** I have written *fourth place, no keeper, lamp unlit*, and that is the first line of a rate sheet for this line and it is not a household and it is not a rate."

"What were you going to do about the ninth place?" said the woman of forty-five.

"**I was going to count houses.** Four houses, a chapel, a man of fifty with two pails, a boy of nine. And I was going to walk up to the doors and ask how many people live in each one and I was going to write four households, and then I was going to take that back to a counting room and put fifty coppers against each of the four and have a rate sheet for a place nine miles from anybody's street."

"And you have not done it."

"**I have not done it, and I have walked five miles of railway with a piece of paper in my coat, and I have got four marks on it and one of them is a drain and one of them is a post with a number cut in it.**" He folded the paper away. "And I have found out that I do not know how to ask the question, and I am not going to stand here and pretend the piece of paper is the difficulty. **The difficulty is that a man asking a man how many people are in his house is a man asking a man what a house is, and I have never in six years found a way of asking that which does not insult both of us.**"

"Then do not ask it," said the woman of forty-five. "**Ask us what we are.**"

---

**And the walk came back at about four in the afternoon and the yard at Lot 17 had four sheets on the wall, and the second version of the walk sheet went up at about six, and Rutt read it at the counter at six the next morning, and about ninety people heard it in two days.**

The second version says on the ballast nowhere at all. It says **on the six-foot, or the shoulder, or the cess where the six-foot is gone, and the four changeovers are marked, and the two of them at the eighth and the seventh are the ones that will get somebody killed**, and the last line of it is new and it is the tenth hand's and nobody argued with it, because he argued it once in a yard in the rain and it held and he is not allowed to argue a rule twice.

> **AND THE FIRST VERSION IS NOT STRUCK. THE FIRST VERSION SAID ON THE BALLAST. THE FIRST VERSION ALSO SAID NOBODY INSIDE THE FOUR-FOOT ON A CURVE, AND BOTH OF THOSE WERE TRUE AT THE SAME TIME, AND A SHEET THAT CONTRADICTS ITSELF IN TWO PLACES ON THE SAME MORNING IS A SHEET THAT WAS WRITTEN BY SOMEBODY WHO HAD NEVER STOOD ON A CURVE, AND THAT IS THE FIRST ENTRY IN THE BOOK THAT IS ABOUT A PERSON, WHICH IS ME.**

And there was one more thing on the wall on the twenty-second, which was not on the walk sheet, and which Nessa Pell wrote up at about four in the afternoon in about nine seconds because it was nine words and it was true.

The case of Lot 17 stood shut. The term of the key had run out at midnight on the twenty-first and the board had not named anybody, because the board could not name anybody, and the second day in a month that the case has stood shut on a term that has run out is not entered as a theft either.

Mrs Halder had held it from the seventeenth and would not open the case alone and had asked for a second person every single time and had been given no power to get one, and the second lapse is on the notice board under the first one, and nobody in this district has yet worked out what it says about a term of four days, and Nessa Pell wrote nine words under it and they are what they are.

> **A ROTATION IS ONLY REAL WHEN IT TURNS. A BOARD THAT ASKS AT THE END OF A TERM HAS NOT ASKED IN TIME.**
