# Chapter 71: Nine Minutes

The man off the lower terrace put the key to the case of Lot 17 on the crate in the yard at a quarter to four in the afternoon on the sixth of this month, squared it against the edge of his own hand the way a man does a thing he has done four hundred thousand times, and asked whether anybody was going to come and get it, and nobody was, because it was the day his term ran out and the board had not asked him a second time and he had not been asked whether he wanted a second time either.

He is about fifty-six. He has kept a chandler's second counter for thirty-one years. He asked in writing on the second that he does not read figures and that a reader at a counter is the only person who may read anything to him, and he asked for four days, and he got all three.

**His term began on the second of this month at about ten past two in the afternoon and it runs to midnight on the sixth, and it is the sixth, not the fifth, that he has come on, and he has come a day early, and he has come early on purpose, and the reason is the only thing on that day that anybody in this district did not already know.**

"I want to ask a question," he said, "**and I want it asked in front of people, because I have been in three kitchens in this city and Bett Ruhl has been one of them and nobody has ever mentioned it and I am not mentioning it. I want to ask it in this yard.**"

Nessa Pell put her pen down. She is a clerk of nineteen years and the sitting steward of this lot and the only person in the building who can read what a person says and write it down in the same hand, and she is also the only person in the building holding a key to anything, and she has not been in a hurry about anything since the sixth of the month before last.

"**Go on,**" she said.

"**How do you ask a person whether they will do a job.**

"**I have been on this key since the second and I have been asked twice and both times a man stood in front of me and told me what had happened and then stopped, and both times a piece of paper arrived afterwards.** And on the sixth of the month before last a man in his coat asked me to hold a drawer in this city for four days and I said no, and my no was correct, and it went in a book, and nobody has asked me since. **So I know what a no is worth. I do not know what a yes is worth, because I have not had one, and I would like to know whether anybody in this city knows how to get one.**"

Nobody in the yard had an answer inside about nine seconds, and Orren Pike, who is seventeen and was at the rail with a sheet of index under his arm, said afterwards that it was the first question anybody had asked in that yard in five weeks that was not about a figure.

---

**And the yard did it in nine minutes, and the nine minutes are the method, and the method is not Adrian Vale's and it is not the tenth hand's and it is not the board's, and it is now the district's, and the only reason it is anybody's at all is that a chandler asked a question nobody had thought to ask.**

It began because the board could not do it. The board of the public lot at Lot 17 has five powers and the fifth of them is that it may set or renew a short public custody term, and every one of the five is about a thing in its own building, and the fourth of them is that it cannot name a person. It had been asked four times in a month to name somebody and it had not managed it once, and the reason it had not managed it once is that naming a person is not the power. Setting the term is the power. Deciding who holds it is not.

"So we cannot name him," said the tenth hand, "**and we are not going to be able to, and if we spend the next hour trying we will be doing the thing we have done twice this month, which is standing in a crowd hoping somebody comes out of it.**

"**So we stop trying to name him. We ask a room. And a room is nine minutes long, and here is the whole of it, and I am going to say it out loud once so that you can all hear whether it is a cheat.**"

> **A JOB IS ASKED IN FIVE THINGS.**
>
> **ONE. WHAT IT IS, IN SENTENCES SOMEBODY WHO HAS NEVER DONE IT CAN UNDERSTAND, AND NOT IN THE WORD THE OFFICE WOULD USE.**
> **TWO. HOW LONG, WITH A HOUR ON IT.**
> **THREE. HOW YOU SAY NO, AND THAT NO REASON IS OWED AND THAT NOBODY WILL ASK FOR ONE AFTERWARDS.**
> **FOUR. WHO ANSWERS FOR YOU WHILE YOU ARE SAYING IT.**
> **FIVE. WHERE YOU STAND WHEN YOU ANSWER.**

"And the fourth one is the whole of it," said the tenth hand, "**and we have never once in six weeks thought of it, and I have been the only person on this line for eleven years and I have wanted it every single one of those eleven years and I did not know it had a shape.**"

---

**And then it was done, in daylight, in the open, at about ten past four in the afternoon, and five people said no inside four minutes and the sixth said yes, and it is in the book in the order it happened and none of the five reasons was argued with.**

The tenth hand read the job first, in the yard, out loud, to about sixty people who had come in off the terraces because there was a key on a crate.

"A key to a case. Four days. You open it when you are told it is open and you are in the room when it is open and you write down what you find. That is the whole job and it is four days long and **it begins at midnight tonight, the minute the man in front of you walks off, and it ends at midnight on the fourth night from tonight.**"

"How long is that in days," said somebody at the back, and the tenth hand said **four**, and somebody else said **"five, there's tonight,"** and a chandler at the side of the yard said **"Four. He has said four days four times now. Four. And there is no hour tonight in which nobody holds it and there is no hour tonight in which two people hold it, and that is the first time in six weeks that either of those has been true, and it is because he came a day early and it was not an accident."**

How you say no. "**You say no by not taking the key. That is all. There is no form. You do not have to give a reason and nobody is going to ask you for one, and if somebody asks you for one afterwards it goes in the book against them and not against you.**"

And who answers for you while you are saying it: "**The registrar is in this yard and the registrar will write down every name that is said, and if a person says no the registrar writes no and does not write a reason, and the reader at the counter reads the list out afterwards so that a person who said no and was not heard knows they were heard.**"

And where you stand: "**There. Where you are standing now, in daylight, where four hundred people can see you. Not inside. Not at a rail. Not at a counter in the morning with a queue behind you. Here.**"

Five noes in four minutes, and they are in the book in their own words, and the finding underneath them is that four of the five reasons are ordinary and one of them is the best sentence anybody has said in that yard in a month.

The first was a plasterer who said he was going to be on a roof at the far end of the terrace and would not get down twice in a week for anybody's drawer. The second was a woman who kept a counter of fish and said that the last person who had held a thing for her had been gone four days and had come back and told her the thing had been open the whole time, and she would not do that to anybody else. The third was a young man who said he was nobody's second and would be nine days out of his own county. The fourth was a man who had a hand that would not hold a pen in the cold and said so, plainly, in eleven words, and would not be asked about it twice.

The fifth was a man of about thirty-eight who said, in about nine seconds:

"**I have read your minute. You set a term of four days on the twelfth because a rule nobody has had to keep is not a rule, and the day after that you let the case stand shut for four days because a board that asks at the end of a term has not asked in time. I have a shop and I open at six and I cannot close for a day and a half, and if somebody dies of a thing I was holding then I will have it for the rest of my life.**"

Somebody in the yard started to say something and he went on without stopping.

"**So: no. And I want it written down that I do not think the nine minutes was a waste.**"

And then a woman of about fifty-two who had come in off the tram line, who had been in the yard for the whole of it and had not been at the front, said **"I'll take it,"** and everybody turned round, and she said it four times because nobody believed it the first one.

"**I'll take it. I've got a stall and I've four days and nobody to leave it with, and I want that written down, because that is the reason and not that I am brave. I don't read figures and I'll have the reader read them to me at the counter, same as him.**"

The entry says that **the asking was done by the tenth hand of the signal crew and written down by Nessa Pell, and that it took nine minutes from the asking to the answer, and that the board did not name anybody and was not asked to and could not have been, and that a rotation is only real when it turns, and that this is the first turn of that key in this district that anybody could call a turn and not a weather.**

---

And then it was read out, and it is not one of the five things on that slate and it is not the sixth one either, and it is the thing that happens afterwards, and nobody had thought of that part because nobody had ever done it before.

Rutt reads at the ward market counter four days a week, at eleven coppers a quarter, and the term has no clause for a shoulder, and she had been in this yard since about a quarter to six the same morning and she was still there at half past four because a woman of about fifty-two who has a stall had just agreed to hold a drawer for four days and somebody had to read the list of who had said no back to the people who had said no.

**Twenty-two names went on the sheet and nineteen of them had said no, in a yard, in daylight, out loud, and every one of the nineteen had said it to about sixty people, and the other three had said they would ask somebody at home, and asking somebody at home is not a no and the sheet says which is which, and Rutt read all nineteen out at six the next morning at the counter in the ordinary voice, with the reason where the reason had been given and with the word NO in front of each one, and it took about four minutes, and about forty people stood at that counter and listened to a woman of about fifty read out a list of people who had refused to hold a drawer.**

The man of thirty-eight was the eleventh on it. He stood at the back with his hands in his pockets and heard his own reason come back at him in a clerk's voice, word for word, the way he had said it, and he did not move, and afterward he asked one question at the counter, which was whether the reason was in the book, and Rutt said that it was in the book and on the wall and in the counter's own book of read-alouds, and that the three copies were the same three words and the book was the one that counted, and that if anybody changed it in one of them the difference would be found.

"**Then it was worth saying,**" he said, and went to work.

And the finding under that, entered by Nessa Pell in the minute of the seventh, is that **the reading-back is not a courtesy and is not for the person who said no, and is for the person who did not hear them, and that a refusal made in a crowd is not heard by a crowd, it is heard by the eleven people nearest it, and that this district has had three refusals in six weeks in a book and has never once read them out.**

---

**And then the yard found out the rest of it, in about four minutes, standing in the same place, and what it found out is that nobody in this district had ever written down the method, and that four people had each had a piece of it, and that the reason it took nine minutes is that it took nine minutes and not four hours because a man who has held a drawer once knows what a drawer needs and a man who has never held one does not.**

Adrian Vale, who had been at the back of the yard the whole time and who has not spoken in this yard in public about anything since the twenty-second of the month before last, said the thing that is in the minute and that he did not want said.

"**It is not mine,**" he said. "**I have been in this yard five weeks telling anybody who would listen that a board cannot name a person, and I have been saying it as though it were a tragedy, and it is not a tragedy, it is a sentence with a full stop at the end of it, and the five things on that slate are how a room does the thing a board cannot do, and somebody who has never held a drawer worked them out inside nine minutes today and I could not have worked them out in nine weeks.**"

"And the sixth thing," said Nessa Pell.

"**What sixth thing.**"

"**A person in the room who is not the one being asked and is not the one asking, whose job is to say what the question was.**" She had the pen down and the page turned. "**Otherwise the entry is a memory of a question and not the question, and the man of thirty-eight above has just told this yard what happens to a person who finds out afterwards that the thing they were asked was not the thing that was written down.**"

"And who is that," said somebody.

"**Me,**" said Nessa Pell. "**And I have been it for nineteen years, and it is not a power and it is not a name and it is not on any of the five, and I have never once been asked to do it and I have never once been thanked for it, and I am not going to be thanked for it now, because I do not want to be, and because the moment I am thanked for it somebody will ask me whether I would do it in the second floor of the water office as well and I would have to think about it.**"

She said that in the ordinary voice and about nine people did not laugh and about thirty did, and the tenth hand wrote it into the minute exactly, including that she had said no to a thing nobody had asked her for, and the finding under that is that the district has had six things for six weeks and has been sitting on the sixth one, **and the sixth one is a woman of nineteen years' standing in a room with a pen, and she is not a power and she is not on the board and she cannot be named onto anything, and the reason is that she does not need to be named, because a clerk is a job and a job is a thing somebody gives you every morning.**

Nobody had a name for the sixth thing. It goes on the wall on the seventh of this month with the other five and with no name on it, and the reading-back is a seventh thing that nobody had a name for either and it is not on the wall, and the reason it is not on the wall is that you cannot describe to a person in advance what it is to hear your own no come back at you in a clerk's voice, and it will be done anyway, and the finding printed under the wall on the seventh of this month is that THE BOARD CANNOT ASK A PERSON WHETHER THEY WILL DO A JOB. THE YARD CAN. AND THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THOSE TWO THINGS IS THE WHOLE OF WHAT HAS GONE WRONG IN THIS DISTRICT SINCE THE EIGHTH OF LAST MONTH AND NOBODY HAS SAID IT OUT LOUD, INCLUDING ME, AND I HAVE SAID THE OTHER HALF OF IT FOURTEEN TIMES.

---

**And at about ten past six in the evening on the sixth of this month a boy of fifteen came into the yard of Lot 17 off the bank road with his coat full of rain and the sleeves of his coat wet past the elbow, and he had walked nine miles, and nobody had asked him to, and the first thing he said about that was the first thing he said.**

"**I wasn't asked. I want that first, in case anybody writes it down later and gets it in the wrong order.**

"**And Sef says the frame's empty.**"

The yard went quiet in the way a yard of sixty people goes quiet, and the boy of fifteen stood in the middle of it with the water coming off him onto the boards and did not sit down.

"**The iron's gone. The two levers are there and the frame is there and the Tongue is not in it. She looked in the hut, under the bench, behind the stove, in the box the spare wire is in, and in the eleven places a thing the size of your forearm can be in a hut that size, and she was up from six in the morning, and she is fifty-one and she has been on that bank eleven years and she knows where a thing goes in her own hut better than anybody in Alder Reach knows where a thing goes in a room.**"

"And when did she last have it in her hand?" said the tenth hand.

"**On the eighteenth of last month. She says she had it out on the bench because the wedge end was catching on something and she was going to look at the wire loop, and she put it back, and she knows the words she used, which were *that'll do till the end of the month*, and she said them in the hut that night, and there is nobody in this district who can check that except her, and it is going in the book as her own account of her own hand and not as a fact about a hut.**"

"**And she didn't send for anybody for eleven hours. She looked for it until about five o'clock and then she said out loud that if it was gone then it was gone, and she'd tell them herself, and she gave me this and a piece of paper with the frame on it that she copied out, and she said don't run.**"

He put the piece of paper on the crate and everybody in the front of that yard looked at it, and it was a sheet of claim stock with a signal frame drawn on it in a careful hand, and it was drawn from the side, and it had the two levers in it, and the levers were the right length, and there was a bracket in the top right-hand corner of it with nothing in the bracket, and under the bracket she had written the word **TONGUE** and then a line and then nothing.

Orren Pike got to it first, because he is seventeen and cannot help himself, and said, "**She's drawn where it goes and not where it is. That's the bracket and that's the right height, because she's had her hands on it eleven years. But she can't draw how big it is, so she's drawn it a quarter the size it'd actually be, and I know that because I've held it and it's about the length of my forearm and four pounds of it, and she couldn't have held it and got that wrong unless she'd never once thought about how big it was.**"

"She'd never once thought about how big it was," the boy of fifteen said. "**She said it doesn't matter what size it is, it's a lever, levers are all about the same, and she said it like she was telling me off for asking.**"

"**And the bracket is empty,**" said the tenth hand, "**and that is the part that is going to matter. She has drawn a hole the size of the thing in the place the thing goes, and she has done it from memory, on a bench, in a hut, with no book in front of her, and she is the only person in this district who has the right to draw it at all.**"

"**And she told me to say that she is not sorry about the eleven hours,**" said Teo, "**and that she is sorry about the eighteen, and that the two things are not the same and I am to say both of them.**"

Nessa Pell wrote all of it down, including the bit about the eleven hours and the bit about the drawing, and the finding under it is entered in the thirteenth part of the results and it is nine words long and it is the shortest thing the board has published and it is not a complaint.

> **THE LOT IS NOT IN THE CUSTODY. THE CUSTODY HAD NO CASE, NO COUNTER AND NO KEY, AND THE PERSON WHO HELD IT ANSWERS TO NOBODY HERE, AND NOBODY IN THIS DISTRICT HAS THE POWER TO MOVE A LOT OR THE POWER TO FIND ONE, AND THE BOARD HAS FIVE POWERS AND ALL FIVE OF THEM ARE ABOUT A THING IN ITS OWN BUILDING.**

And the boy of fifteen did not get a cup of anything and did not ask, and the tenth hand wrote his name against the entry as the person who brought it, in his own hand, with the finding that **he was not asked and he came, and not asked is in the count.**

The finding that is under all of it, written by the tenth hand at about nine o'clock at night on a bench in the yard with a lamp beside him, is that **a custody with no case in it has one hole in it and the hole is the size of the distance between a thing and the person who is holding it, and this district built that custody on purpose to be worse than a case, and it worked, and nobody has a name for the fault because there was no case to lose the key from.**

**And the last line of the entry is that the next thing anybody has to do is to walk to the fourth place, and that the fourth place is four hours on foot from the ninth place with four changesovers in it, and that the walk there and back is nine hours in this weather.**
