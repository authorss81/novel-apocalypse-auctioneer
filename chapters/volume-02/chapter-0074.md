# Chapter 74: Two Sheets, Upside Down

A drover of about sixty-one stood at the wall of the River and Carriage notice board on the fifteenth of this month with two sheets in his hands, one in each, and he had them both upside down, and he had been upside down for about four minutes, and a woman of about sixty with a stick stood four feet off and let him get on with it.

He is called Abner Skell. He came into the district on the fourteenth with eleven tons of lime out of the uplands, which is a load of about four days' work and a road that does not come back this way again until the twenty-first. He is not a trade, he is not on a roll, he is not a household in this district and he has never been asked anything by anybody in this city. The door was held for him by a chandler, as it is held for anybody who asks.

He had been in the city since about six in the morning. He had eaten once. And he was standing in front of two pieces of paper trying to work out which of them was telling the truth.

---

**And the first thing anybody in Alder Reach found out about a stranger holding two sheets is that nobody in this city knew what day the fifteenth of this month was, and that all three people he asked gave him a different answer, and that two of them were wrong.**

The ward market's sheet had gone out on the fifth, five hundred copies, one to each of the eleven boards, one to the notice board of the River and Carriage, one to the tram arch, and the rest to the wards and the counters, and it had a day against every train and no weekday anywhere on it, because the district does not have a calendar and had never needed one.

Halloway Reed's sheet was dated **MONDAY THE FIFTEENTH OF THIS MONTH** across the top in the same size of type as the price, and the secretary of the ward market read the date out in the counting room on the fifteenth at about ten in the morning and asked the room what Monday was, and the room found out.

"The chapel at the west end has a calendar on a wall and a sexton of about sixty-eight in that place has counted forty-six days off it in six weeks and got every one of them right," said Ivo Stenn, "**and I have been in that chapel once and I did not look at it, and I have been a clerk of this market for six years and I can tell you what the market's half-year is worth and I cannot tell you what day today is.**"

"**I make it Monday,**" said a chandler.

"**I make it the day before the day the haulier comes up the bank,**" said a haulier, "**and that is not a day and I have just heard myself say it and I am not taking it back.**"

"**Then the fifteenth is the fifteenth,**" said Stenn, "**and a man on a tram arch has printed a day on it, and the market printed the same thing on the third of this month without knowing there was a question, and both sheets are in this city and the day is on one of them and not on the other, and that is the whole of the difference and I am putting it in the minute and I am not going to be able to sleep.**"

---

**The arch's sheet came off the press at about eleven on the fifteenth and it was better printing than the market's, and it was cheaper per copy, and it was the third thing in this district's history a stranger could take cold and use.**

It is one sheet, four columns, the toll in its own column so that a person who cannot read a sentence may compare two. In the middle of the page, at full width, the market's toll column is reproduced with nothing in it, and the finding printed inside it, and underneath that, in the same size of type, Halloway's own column with four coppers in it and a line under the four coppers that says **A PLACE, A TRAIN, A DAY, AND FOUR COPPERS, AND THE FOUR COPPERS IS THE WHOLE OF WHAT YOU PAY.**

And under the whole thing, at the foot, in type one size smaller and not one size smaller than anything else, is the line that the district has argued about for a fortnight and the reason a stranger cannot use any of it.

> **AND WHAT YOU ARE BUYING IS A PLACE ON A TRAIN WHOSE ORIGIN IS A PLACE. I CANNOT PRINT WHAT A PLACE COSTS. I HAVE PUT FOUR COPPERS IN THE COLUMN ANYWAY. A COLUMN WITH FOUR COPPERS IN IT IS A DIFFERENT THING FROM A COLUMN WITH NOTHING IN IT AND A STRANGER CAN TELL THE DIFFERENCE, AND THAT IS WHY I PRINTED IT, AND IT IS THE ONLY REASON I HAVE PRINTED IT, AND I WOULD LIKE SOMEBODY TO ARGUE WITH IT.**

The print cost nine hundred and six coppers for a run of five hundred, four for the stock and two for the setting and nine hundred quoted on the fourteenth of last month, and the market's sheet of the third had cost the same and the gap over the twelve coppers the printed-matter column buys is eight hundred and ninety-four, and it was carried on a trade for the second time by the same chandler in a stall on the lower terrace, and it has now been carried for the third time, and her stall's name is at the bottom of all three in type the size of the date, and she was asked on the sixteenth what happens if it is carried a fourth time and said that she would rather be asked.

---

Abner Skell read them both a third time with them the right way up and then said the thing that is the finding of the fifteenth of April and that nobody in Alder Reach had thought to ask about.

"**Which of these two is telling the truth.**"

"That is the question," said the woman with the stick.

"**No it isn't,**" he said. "**That's the question I would like answered. But I'm not going to get it answered, and I worked that out in about a minute, and here is how.**

"**The middle one is the market's. It has got the empty column in the middle with the reason in it, and the reason in it is that they cannot say what the origin costs, and that is a true sentence, and I believe it. And the other one has four coppers in the same column, and four coppers is a price, and I have been buying places on carts and on boats and on men for forty years, and a man who cannot say what a thing costs is a man who has not decided what it is.**

"**So one of these two sheets is a man who has not decided, and the other one is a man who has, and I would like to put my eleven tons of lime down somewhere and go on to the uplands, and I am going to buy from the man who has decided.**"

Nobody in the yard said a word.

"And then I am going to do the thing I have been doing for four days," he said, "**which is read the bottom of it. And it is at the bottom because he put it at the bottom, and the bottom of a thing is not a warning, the bottom of a thing is where the truth goes so that it does not stop anybody buying.**

"**And it says he cannot print what a place costs. And he printed it anyway. And that is the same answer as the middle one, in a different voice, with a price in it, and so both men have said the same thing and there is no difference between them except that one of them has decided what to charge you for not knowing.**"

The woman with the stick said, "**And what happens to a person who reads it.**"

"**Ma'am?**"

"**You have asked which of them is telling the truth, and it is a good question and it is the wrong one, and I have been coming to this counter for a month and I have learned one thing and it is the only thing.**" She had the stick in front of her with both hands on it. "**You have not asked what a person does with a sheet. Both of those are about the price. Neither of them is about what you are.**

"**There is a printed line at the bottom of the middle one. It says the train at the west end has stood fifty-one days and cannot be moved east, and that the west end is held by two named people until the twenty-ninth of this month, and that a timetable is not a promise and this one knows it. It is at the foot of the page in the same type as the price. I have had it read to me four times and it is the only sentence either man printed that is about a person.**

"**So: the middle one is telling you that it will not be able to help you on a day, and the other one is telling you that it will be able to help you for four coppers. And I have been in this city since six in the morning, and I do not know which of those is a lie, and I know which one I would want to be standing at.**"

---

**And what she wanted to be standing at took the district two days to be able to say, and the count of strangers is four, and it is entered, and nobody in Alder Reach has a column for it.**

Nobody in the district had ever asked what a stranger does with a printed sheet, and the reason nobody had asked is that for eleven years this city printed nothing. The first thing this market has printed since the flood year went up on eleven boards on the fifth of this month, and a man in a good coat on a tram arch put one up on the fifteenth, and by that afternoon there were two sheets in this city and a man of sixty-one holding both of them, and in one afternoon four people who are not in this district came to the ward market counter and asked one question, and Rutt read the same answer to all four of them, and the answer is at the bottom of a sheet.

"**What do I do with this,**" said the first, a man with a load of slate.

"**What happens if I go,**" said the second, a woman with a basket who had a house built on the far bank in the fourteenth year and no way off it.

"**Is this a ticket,**" said the third, who was about nineteen.

And the fourth was a man of about forty who had already bought two places at four coppers off the arch on the fifteenth, before the market's carts came round, and who came to the counter at about four in the afternoon and said:

"**I have paid eight coppers and I have got a piece of paper and the piece of paper does not say when the train goes, and it does not say whether it is going, and it does not say what happens to me if I stand on a platform and it does not come. Can anybody tell me those three things.**"

And Rutt, who is a reader at a counter at eleven coppers a quarter for four days a week and whose term has no clause for a shoulder, said the thing that is in the book in her own words and that is the finding of the whole afternoon about strangers.

"**No,**" she said. "**And I will read you the whole of both sheets, in order, including the bottom, and I will read them twice, and I will do that for anybody who comes to that counter for the rest of the time I have got, and I want it entered that I have said no four times in one afternoon and that three of the four were disappointed and one of them was not, and that the one who was not is the one I would have given my own name to.**"

The man of forty said, "**Then I'll take the two back and I'll go and look at the west end, because a man who cannot tell me three things but will tell me all three that he does not know is a man I can do business with, and I have been on this road since I was nine.**"

And Abner Skell went and looked at the west end, on the sixteenth, at about ten in the morning, and nobody sent him and nobody offered to show him the way, and he asked one person on the terrace where the railway was and the person said east and then said that he would not go, and Skell went.**

He got to the fourth place at about two in the afternoon and could not have told anybody which place it was, and he says so, and the reason he says so is the finding of the two days.

"**I have stood at the first, the second and the third as well, coming up, and I could not tell one from another and I have been a man who notices a thing for forty years. They are four huts on a brick wall with a lamp on a post outside each one and a plate under each lamp with a figure on it, and I could not read the figures, and there is a bench in each one and a stove in each one and the same two holes in each roof.**

"**And there was a boy asleep in the third one and I woke him up and asked him what place this was and he said four.**"

He said that last part three times in the yard on the seventeenth and nobody wrote it down the first time and the tenth hand wrote it down the second.

"**And there was a lamp-man on the viaduct with a can of oil and a hook, and he was doing the lamps, and I asked him whether the lamps were lit, and he said they were not, and I said then why are you doing them, and he said a lamp wants doing whether it is lit or not, and he has asked twice in his life that it be said that he is a lamp-man and not a signal hand, and he said it to me, a stranger, at about four o'clock, unprompted, and it is the first thing in this city that anybody has told me about themselves without wanting anything.**

"**And I have been in this city two days and I have learned three things and none of them was about a price, and I have carried lime over four counties for nineteen years and I could not tell you one of them about a road.**"

And what a man of sixty-one learned on a viaduct on the sixteenth of April is in the book because he said it in a yard and Rutt wrote it down and it is nine lines long, and four of the nine lines are about lamps.**

> **FOUR HUTS. FOUR PLATES WITH FIGURES ON THEM I COULD NOT READ. FOUR BENCHES. FOUR STOVES. THE SAME TWO HOLES IN EVERY ROOF. I HAVE BEEN A MAN WHO NOTICES A THING FOR FORTY YEARS AND I COULD NOT TELL ONE FROM ANOTHER, AND I HAVE BEEN CARRYING LIME OVER FOUR COUNTIES FOR NINETEEN YEARS, AND NOBODY EVER ASKED ME WHETHER I COULD TELL ONE HUT FROM ANOTHER, AND THE ANSWER IS NO.**

---

**And Halloway Reed came into the yard of Lot 17 at about ten past two in the afternoon on the seventeenth of this month, in daylight, in front of everybody, which he has done twice in this city, and he did not sell anything, and the minute says so in its first line.**

He came to see the two lines under the column. He stood in front of the wall with a sheet of his own in his hand for about four minutes and read the market's two lines, both of them, and then his own two lines, which are the same two lines with a line above them.

"**You have printed a date and an hour under a hole,**" he said. "**I have printed a price under a hole. And I have printed your two lines under my price, and a stranger holding my sheet now knows that four coppers is not a price for a thing anybody has decided, it is the price of not knowing, and I have given that away for nothing and I would like it noticed and I am not going to be thanked for it in a street.**

"**And the reason I printed it is that I have been beaten by a column and I have had a fortnight to work out how, and I have worked out that the market's column is empty and mine is full and the empty one is worth more, and that is the first time in nineteen years anybody has beaten me by being worse at me.**"

"Then it is settled," said Stenn, "**and the sheet is four coppers and the market's is nothing, and the district's position of the twenty-sixth of last month is on a wall, and the market has nothing to add to it.**"

"**You have not understood what I said,**" said Halloway Reed, "**and I am going to say the rest of it and it is about a fortnight and not about a sheet, and then I am going back to the arch.

"**I am right about four things. One of them is the column, and the column is the one I have just given away. I am right about days. I am right about speed. And I am right about the morning a public road fails, when a book does not open a door and the only purchasable thing is speed. That is four, and I have said so, and it is printed on the arch in the same type as the price and nobody prints that.

"**And I am wrong about one thing, and it is the thing you found on the twenty-sixth, and I have printed it myself at the foot of my own sheet in my own type. It says that a place on my timetable costs four coppers and what it actually costs is a place you cannot name losing the memory of its own departures, and I have printed that and I have not changed my price, and I am not going to.

"**And here is the whole of what I came to say, and it is not about a sheet. A column with nothing in it is worth more than a column with four coppers in it until about the fourth day. And after the fourth day a column with four coppers in it is worth more, because on the fifth day a man needs to be somewhere. And I have both of those columns on one sheet and the market has one, and I have not worked out which of us is right, and I have had fourteen days and I have had nothing but the days.**"

Nessa Pell entered the whole of it, and entered that the counterfeiter of this district came into a public yard and sold nothing, and that the finding underneath is that a man who has been beaten by a column and has printed the beating on his own sheet is not a good man and is not a converted man and is not a friend, **and that the district's advantage over him is one column and about eleven days, and that eleven days is not a margin, it is a postponement, and this entry is the first one this district has made that gives a number of days and does not say what happens on the last one.**

---

And the entry says that the four of them are the first strangers this district has ever had about anything it printed, and that three of the four arrived holding two sheets, and that nobody in Alder Reach had put the two sheets together and thought what they would add up to, and that what they add up to is wrong.

> **TWO SHEETS, BOTH TRUE ON THEIR FACES, ADD UP TO A PRICE FOR A THING THAT HAS NOT BEEN DECIDED. THE MARKET'S SHEET SAYS IT CANNOT SAY WHAT A PLACE COSTS. THE ARCH'S SHEET SAYS IT CANNOT SAY WHAT A PLACE COSTS AND HAS PUT FOUR COPPERS IN THE COLUMN. A STRANGER CANNOT TELL A HOLE FROM A SMALL PRICE. THIS DISTRICT HAS BEEN ARGUING FOR A MONTH ABOUT WHETHER A COLUMN PRINTED NARROW LOOKS LIKE A COLUMN THAT HAS BEEN KEPT QUIET, AND A STRANGER IS NOT GOING TO MAKE THAT DISTINCTION ON A ROAD IN THE RAIN, AND THE ANSWER IS NOT A BETTER SHEET.**

---

And the answer came on the seventeenth of April, in a counting room, in about eleven minutes, and it was two lines of type under a column that is still empty, and the two lines have a date and an hour on them, and the date is the seventeenth of this month and the hour is about eleven in the morning, and they are the first hour anybody in this district has ever printed.**

The market printed a second line under the toll column and it reads, in the same type as everything else:

> **THIS COLUMN IS EMPTY ON THE SEVENTEENTH OF THIS MONTH AT ELEVEN IN THE MORNING. IT WILL STAY EMPTY UNTIL SOMEBODY IN THIS DISTRICT CAN STATE THE AMOUNT OF AN ORIGIN, AND NOBODY CAN, AND THAT IS NOT A SECRET AND IT IS A GAP. THE OTHER SHEET HAS FOUR COPPERS IN THE SAME PLACE AND FOUR COPPERS IS A PRICE AND A PRICE IS AN ANSWER, AND WE ARE NOT SAYING THE OTHER SHEET IS WRONG. WE ARE SAYING THAT A STRANGER CAN TELL WHICH OF THE TWO HOLES IS A HOLE, AND ON THE THIRD OF THIS MONTH HE COULD NOT, AND THE ONLY THING THAT HAS CHANGED IN THIS CITY IN A FORTNIGHT IS THAT SOMEBODY PRINTED A DATE AND AN HOUR UNDER AN EMPTY COLUMN.**

And the arch printed the same two lines, one underneath his own four coppers, in the same type, with a line above them that says **AND I HAVE PRINTED THIS BECAUSE THE MARKET PRINTED IT FIRST AND I HAVE SAID SO ON THE ARCH AND I AM NOT GOING TO BE THANKED FOR IT IN A STREET.**

"So he is right about the column," said Stenn.

"**He is right about the column and he is right about the price and he is wrong about one thing and he has printed the one thing himself, in his own column, in his own type, at his own cost, which is the first honest thing a counterfeiter has ever done in this basin and it does not make him a good man and it does not make him wrong about the other four things he is right about.**"

"And the district's position."

"**The same as it was on the twenty-sixth of last month and it is entered on a wall, and nobody has taken it down. We can prove he is wrong in one sentence and we cannot prove it in a book. And he has now printed the sentence himself, at the bottom of a sheet, where a man in a hurry will not read it, and the only person in this district who is helped by that is a man who is not in a hurry, and that is not a market.**"

---

**And at the rail on the fifteenth, the sixteenth and the seventeenth, Selik Marne said that that was not enough, three times, in the failing light, to a yard that had stopped counting, and the minute of the seventeenth records that the count of how many times a man has said a thing was not taken, and the reason is the tenth hand's, and the reason is in the minute in his own words.**

"**I am not going to write down how many times he has said it. A count of how many times a man has said a thing is a measure of him and not of the thing, and this district has spent six weeks being the best counter of things in the basin, and it is the first time I have understood that there is a thing you get better at by refusing to count it.**"

**And the last thing on the seventeenth of April is a number and it is four, and it is the number of strangers who came to that counter in one afternoon, and it is the first number anybody in Alder Reach has ever entered about people who were not asked anything, were not on a roll, and did not have to pay a rate, and the entry says in its own words that the district has been counting its own for six weeks and has now been counted by four.**
