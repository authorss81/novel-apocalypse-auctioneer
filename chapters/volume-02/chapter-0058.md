# Chapter 58: The Village That Is Not a Stop

Rutt's shoulder had been bad since the flood year and it was the reason she was a reader at a counter instead of a clerk at the signal cabin, which was a job she had wanted for eleven years, and on the fifteenth of this month she walked nine miles with it and came back worse and told nobody, because a reader's term is four days a week at eleven coppers a quarter and the term does not have a clause for a shoulder.

She went with a boy of fifteen because the keeper of the ninth place had asked for somebody who was not a reader, and because a woman at the ward market counter had said, when the request was read out to her, "Then send the boy who asked the question in a school gym. **He is the only person in this district who has ever asked a question out loud and got an answer he did not like.**"

Nobody could say why she was right. She wrote it in her working page at the counter, which is not a public record, and it is the only line on that page that is about a person.

They left at about twenty past five in the morning because the walk is three hours going up and the light in March — and this is March, the month has turned twice since the river came — is not long and does not pretend to be. Teo carried the two bags. There is one bag with the line book in it, copied, and one with chalk, a spare lamp, oil, bread, and a sheet of the ward market's own blank claim stock, because Rutt said you never go to a place with nothing to give away, and Teo said we have not got anything to give away, and Rutt said then take the claim stock, because a sheet of paper with nothing on it is a thing you can hand to a woman and say this is for a thing you did not know you were allowed to do, and she would not understand the second part and would keep the first.

They went out on the ballast, because that is the only way, and for the first hour the viaduct is on their left and the whole of Alder Reach is behind them and the ground under the brick arches is a street that used to be a street, and Teo asked three questions about it in the first hour and got two of them answered.

---

**The fourth place has a lamp on it and the lamp was not lit, and Rutt said the reason, and it is a keeper's reason and not a reader's.**

"It is not lit because there is no train to look at," she said. "**A ground signal is a lamp you leave burning for something that is coming. There is nothing coming from the west end, and the west end is under water, and a man who kept that lamp burning for eleven years would be burning oil to tell a train that does not exist that the line is clear.** And I have wanted to ask her about that for two days and I did not, because I am a reader and readers ask about things and keepers tell you about things."

**And the stall was on their right for three hundred and ten yards and Rutt did not look at it and Teo did, all the way, because the tenth hand had told him in Alder Reach on the Saturday that a man who looks at a train in a stall loses his place, and there was no train, and he looked at it anyway for three hundred yards because he was fifteen and could not help it.**

At the ninth place they came up the bank at about a quarter to twelve and there was the siding with nine vehicles on it and the column and the four houses and the chapel, and the boy of about nine was at the column again with the two pails, and he recognised Teo, and he said, "**You're the one that came with the big one.**"

"**I'm the one that came with the big one**," said Teo, "and this is the reader, and we're here because the keeper asked us to come on the fifteenth and I want you to know that she asked for me and not for him.**"

And the boy said, "She said. She's said every day since the sixth." And then he went and fetched his mother again, which is what happens in a place of two hundred yards.

---

**Sef Anyard had a table under the window with three chairs at it and no room for four, so two of them sat on the bench with the book and Rutt read out of the hut on the step, and about fourteen people stood about nine feet away, and by the time it was finished there were thirty-one people at the ninth place and a dog, and the dog is not a figure and is not in the count and Rutt says so aloud when she gets to it.**

She read the column. That was the whole of what she had come for and it took about an hour, and the standing rule held and the practice held and the rule about a courier never being in a room where a reading happened was not touched because nobody was enrolled and nobody carried anything.

> **THE COLUMN HEADED ORIGIN HELD, FROM THE LINE BOOK AT THE EAST END, READ ALOUD AT SARROW WATER ON THE FIFTEENTH OF THIS MONTH BY THE READER AT THE WARD MARKET COUNTER, IN WORDS THE ASKERS DID NOT BRING.**
>
> **THE BOOK IS INDEXED TO THE NINE HUNDRED AND SIXTH MOVEMENT SINCE THE FLOOD YEAR. OF THOSE, ONE HUNDRED AND FORTY-ONE NAME THIS PLACE AS THE ORIGIN HELD.**
>
> **AND THE READER SAID, BEFORE SHE READ ANY OF IT, TWICE: I CAN READ YOU WHAT A BOOK SAYS AND I CANNOT READ YOU WHAT A BOOK DOES NOT SAY, AND BOTH OF THOSE ARE TRUE, AND ONE OF THEM IS THE ONE YOU HAVE ASKED ME FOR.**

One hundred and forty-one. And the finding that the yard in Alder Reach did not have and the book did not have and that Rutt found by accident because she had read the whole column out loud in a place where the people could hear her:

> **THE LINE HAS NINE PLACES WITH PLATFORMS. IN NINE YEARS, ON THE RECORD OF THIS COLUMN, THIS PLACE HAS HAD NO PASSENGER STOP ON IT. AND IT IS NAMED ONE HUNDRED AND FORTY-ONE TIMES, WHICH IS MORE THAN ANY PLACE ON THE LINE EXCEPT ALDER REACH AND THE FOURTH PLACE.**
>
> **AND THE REASON IS ENTERED AND IT IS NOT A MYSTERY: SINCE THE FLOOD YEAR THE WEST END OF THE LINE HAS BEEN UNDER WATER AND THE ALDER REACH YARDS HAVE BEEN FULL, AND THEREFORE ALMOST EVERYTHING THAT COMES AND GOES ON THIS LINE IS STANDING ON THIS SIDING, AND THE LINE BOOK COUNTS MOVEMENTS AND NOT PASSENGERS, AND A MOVEMENT IS NOT A PLACE, AND A PLACE IS NOT A VICTIM, AND NOTHING IN THE COLUMN ASKED THE QUESTION.**

---

**And then the boy asked the question, and it was not a good question in the way that questions are usually called good in this book, and it was the best one anybody has asked in five weeks.**

He had been standing at about nine feet with his hands behind his back for an hour and he had not asked anything, and after the reading was finished and the people had not gone, he walked the nine feet.

"Where do the trains come from?"

Nobody answered him, and about four people looked at one another, and a man of about forty by the column said, "**Off the west end.**"

"Where's that?"

"It's Harrow. It's under the water. They've been under the water since the flood year, that is a thing everybody knows—"

"Then how do they come from it if it's under the water," said Teo.

And about half the people at that place laughed, and it was not unkind, and it is entered in the book as a fact about the place and not as a fact about the boy, and the reason it is entered is that **a question that is obviously absurd to everybody standing in a place is very often the shape of a question that nobody standing in it has ever been able to ask properly, and this district learned that on the sixteenth of last month in a school gym, and it learned it from a boy who had been asked twice and refused twice.**

---

**And the count, and it is the number this chapter is for, and it was taken by the person who asked the question and not by a clerk, and it is entered with the reason underneath.**

He asked thirty-one people two questions, and the first was *where do the trains come from* and the second was **has anybody ever gone from here on a train, ever**, and he asked the second one eleven times because the first one is a question about a railway and the second one is a question about people, and eleven people needed it asked twice, and one of them was a woman of about forty-five who said the first time, "Gone where?" and he said, "**Anywhere. On a train. Ever. Since before the water came.**"

> **ASKED ON THE FIFTEENTH OF THIS MONTH AT THE NINTH PLACE BY T., AGED ABOUT FIFTEEN, OF THIRTY-ONE PEOPLE PRESENT: HAS ANYBODY EVER GONE FROM HERE ON A TRAIN, EVER, IN ANY YEAR ANYBODY IN THIS PLACE CAN REMEMBER.**
>
> **ANSWERED BY TWENTY-THREE. NOT ASKED BY EIGHT, AND NOT ASKED IS IN THE COUNT AND IS NOT A HOLE AND IS NEITHER A YES NOR A NO.**
>
> **THE COUNT OF YESES IS ONE.**
>
> **AND THE ONE IS A WOMAN OF ABOUT SIXTY-ONE WHO WALKED TO ALDER REACH IN THE FLOOD YEAR, ON THE FOOTBRIDGE, WITH A CHILD, AND DID NOT GO BY TRAIN, AND IS ENTERED AS A DEPARTURE BECAUSE SHE LEFT, AND THE ENTRY SAYS SO, AND IT IS THE ONLY YES AND IT IS NOT ONE OF THE HUNDRED AND FORTY-ONE.**

The twenty-three people who answered no gave reasons, and Teo wrote all twenty-three down because he had a page and nobody had told him not to, and the reasons are in the book in full, and they are ordinary, and the book prints them at length because ordinary reasons are the ones a stranger will not believe:

*the young ones go, the young ones always went.* — four people.
*there's a road in the other direction.* — three people.
*my mother went to her sister in the fourth year and it was a cart.* — two.
*my son went to the coast and he was sixteen and that was nineteen years ago and I have not seen him and I know exactly where he is.* — one, and this one is entered at length because it is the only answer in twenty-three that contains a person who left and cannot be accounted for, and the clerk reading it out at a counter in Alder Reach on the seventeenth had to stop, and Rutt does not stop, and the reason Rutt does not stop is in her own line at the counter and has been since the sixth of last month.

*and four people gave the answer that is the answer.* It is nine words long and it is in the book with the name of the place and not the name of the person, and it is in Bev Tollard's mouth and he did not want it in the book and then he did:

> **I HAVE A BROTHER AND I CANNOT FEEL THAT HE WENT.**

---

**And then somebody did the count, because that is what this district does, and the count is the reason the whole of this chapter had to happen.**

It was Rutt and it took her about four minutes and it was done out loud, standing on the bank, with the thirty-one answering and Teo writing, and it is entered in the ordinary form, and here it is whole:

> **THE NINTH PLACE. THIRTY-ONE PEOPLE. FOUR HOUSES, OCCUPIED: ELEVEN, SEVEN, NINE, FOUR. A CHAPEL WITH A BELL THAT IS NOT RUNG: —. AND THE REST: —.**
>
> **AND THE COUNT IS ENTERED AS FOUR HOUSEHOLDS AND A CHAPEL, BECAUSE THAT IS WHAT THE FORM ASKS FOR, AND THE FORM WAS WRITTEN IN THE THIRD WEEK OF THE MONTH BEFORE LAST BY F. AMBROSE AND APPROVED BY A YARD OF ABOUT SIX HUNDRED, AND THE FORM ASKS FOR A HOUSEHOLD, AND EVERYBODY IN THIS PLACE ANSWERED LIKE A HOUSEHOLD, BECAUSE THAT IS WHAT THE FORM IS.**

The woman who waited — the one of about sixty-one who had walked to Alder Reach over the old footbridge with a child, years ago, and who is the only yes in the count, and who is the person the book is about tonight without anybody having planned that — stood on the bank with her arms folded until Rutt had finished, and then said:

"There are thirty-one of us and four houses and we have never once been a household. **We are a place.**

"And I have been a house for as long as I have been filling that form in, because a house is what the form says, and I have filled it in twice in nine years and both times it wanted four houses and a chapel. And I am sixty-one and I have a sister in the west end of this valley who is not in this place and who I see, and I am entered here as a house.

"So when your book says the toll goes to the origin, and the origin is a place, then you have just told a village of thirty-one people that **a book is going to come and take a house out of us**, and there is no house in this place to take, and **you cannot lose what was never entered, and that is not a mercy. That is a hole with a shape of our own making.**"

Nobody had an answer to that. Rutt wrote it down and said, "**That is the second thing I cannot read, and it is a larger one than the first.**"

"Write it in her words," said Teo. "**Not yours. In hers. She is the one who is in the form.**"

---

**And at about seven in the evening they ate bread on the bench and Rutt put her shoulder against the wall and did not mention it, and a boy of about nine came to the hut with a lamp he had been asked to bring and stood in the doorway and asked Sef Anyard a question that is in the book because she wrote it down afterwards, from memory, in her own hand, on the same page as the origin column.**

He asked her whether she had ever been to Harrow.

"No," said Sef Anyard.

"Everybody says they've been to Harrow. My gran went to Harrow in the flood and she says the water came up in about an hour and she was in the top of the station."

"That sounds right."

"**What was it like before?**"

And Sef Anyard, who is fifty-one and has kept a hut for eleven years and had been asked four questions by this district in eleven years, and one of them on the seventh of this month was whether she wished to speak at all, looked at a boy of nine in her doorway for long enough that her visitor looked up off his page.

"There were people on the line," she said. "**I have not thought about that for a long time. There was a fella used to come down the bank twice a week with a barrow and he would say good afternoon to the boy, and I used to think that was a waste of a good afternoon, and it is the only thing I have got that is not in a book.**"

And the boy said, "Right," and went off with the lamp, and Rutt entered the whole of it, because it is the only entry in the history of that public book that is a piece of local knowledge nobody in Alder Reach had, and it is about a barrow, and it is the reason this district is going to have to say the word *village* out loud in about nine minutes' time and be frightened of it.

---

**And the boy said the word, and it was the third time, and it was about himself, and nobody asked him for a reason and nobody got one.**

The rota was the thing that did it. It was not a rota anybody had made: it was a trestle table on the bank and two people who wanted to know whether the train was going to be moved in the night, and a woman who wanted to know whether anybody had told the Road House, and a man who wanted somebody to go and stand in the yard at Alder Reach and say, in a room, that a train on a village's hard ground was a train on a village's hard ground.

"There are three things," said the woman at the column. "**We want to say no to the train. And we want somebody to go and say it where the train is kept. And we want somebody to be here at night and see if the bank moves.** And there is a man of twenty-six who says he will see the bank and a woman of forty-five who says she will say no, and neither of them can read."

"I can read," said Rutt.

"**You can read and you are the ward market's and you will be asked for a fourth day by somebody who is not us.**" The woman said it without any edge at all. "We want one of ours. That is all it is. That is the whole of it and I would not have said it out loud in front of you if your keeper had not told me you had said the word in a yard twice and meant it."

Teo looked at the column for a while.

"No," he said.

Nobody said anything. He did not give a reason and he was not asked for one and the answer owed him was *accepted* and none, and Nessa Pell entered it on the eighteenth in the ordinary way, as a decline, with the time and the place, and neither is struck.

And then Sef Anyard said the only thing anybody said about it, and she said it from the step of her own hut with her hand on the frame, and it is in the book because she asked for it to be:

"**You have said that word three times now and every one of them has been the right thing to do, and I want you to hear what you have actually bought.**

"In four years a stranger is going to take this book cold and read that a boy of fifteen at the ninth place said the word, and he is going to be impressed with you, and he is going to write something about a district that trains its children. **And nobody is impressed. And you have just put a boy into a village's book who cannot be asked to do a thing for a year, in a village of thirty-one people, on a line with no timetable, and the next time that bank moves at night the only people who will be asked are the woman of forty-five and the man of twenty-six and me.**

"That is not a punishment I am handing you. **It is a job you have made unavailable, and I would like you to be fifteen about it for one more day and then be as angry as you like, because there is a train on my bank and I am not sentimental.**"

"I know," said Teo. "**It's mine to pay.**"

"Entered," said Rutt, from the step, with the slate on her knee. "**And I am entering the whole of that, and I am entering that he is about fifteen, and I am entering that a reader walked nine miles to hear it, and I am entering that it cost a reader's shoulder and nobody has mentioned the shoulder, and I am entering that, because a person who is asked to pay and a person who is not asked to pay are different and the district has been confusing the two for five weeks and I am not going to be the one who does it on my own page.**"

---

**And they came back east on the sixteenth in the wet, nineteen miles, and the boy carried both bags, and he was fifteen, and nobody thanked him, and he did not expect to be thanked, and he has said since that he was not thanked and will not be, and Bett Ruhl has said she would have been insulted if anybody had tried.**

The thing he carried back was not a number. It was nine words and a request attached to them, and the request is in the book in his hand and not in anybody else's:

> **ASKED FOR: THE SENTENCE TO GO IN AS THE PERSON'S OWN WORDS AND NOT MINE. GIVEN. ENTERED WITH THE PLACE AND NOT THE NAME, BECAUSE HE SAID THE PLACE AND NOT THE NAME, AND BECAUSE A MAN WHO LIVES AT A PLACE IS NOT A FIGURE, AND I AM FIFTEEN, AND I HAVE LEARNED ONE THING IN TWO DAYS AND IT IS THE ONE THE TENTH HAND LEARNT IN A YARD IN THE RAIN FIVE WEEKS AGO, WHICH IS THAT A NUMBER THAT CAME OUT OF A REFUSAL IS A DIFFERENT INSTRUMENT FROM A NUMBER SOMEBODY SAID OUT LOUD.**

And at the top of his own page, in a hand that is not a clerk's, above the nine words, he wrote the second half of the question he asked in a school gym on the twenty-sixth of the month before last, which has been published ever since with an answer attached about duty and not about duration, and which now has a second half that nobody had asked for:

> **WHO TELLS THIRTYY-ONE PEOPLE, AND BY WHOM, AND FOR HOW LONG, WHEN THE LINE TAKES THEIR PLACE AS AN ORIGIN AND THEIR PLACE IS NOT A PLACE AND THEY ARE NOT ON A RATE SHEET.**
>
> **AND: WHO TELLS THEM THAT IT IS HAPPENING. AND NOT ON A TRAIN DAY. AND NOT TO A MAN WHO WRITES THE TIMETABLE.**

He gave the page to Nessa Pell on the seventeenth at about nine in the morning, and she read it, and she did not put it in the book, and he said, "**You don't have to put that one in. I know it's a question and not a finding. I have four of them now.**"

"No," said Nessa Pell. "**I am not putting it in because it is a question, and I have never put a question in this book in my life, and that is not going to change because a boy of fifteen has four of them.**

"**I am putting the nine words in. And I am putting the count in. And I am putting the twenty-three reasons in at length, because that is the only part of this anybody will be able to check.**
