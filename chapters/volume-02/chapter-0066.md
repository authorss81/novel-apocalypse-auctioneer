# Chapter 66: One Hour and Eleven Minutes

Sef Anyard heard them coming at about a quarter to eleven in the morning of the twenty-ninth of this month and she had eleven minutes, and she used them the way she has used every morning for eleven years, which is: stove, kettle, book squared on the bench with the pencil on top of it, the lever wiped, and the two pails brought in off the tap by a boy of nine who does not know that anything is happening today and who was told nothing because nobody told anybody anything.

**They set out from the fourth place at about seven in the morning.** Four people: a mechanic of forty-three who signed a lease in about eleven minutes in the ninth year, a signal clerk of seventeen who counts things for a living and is a seat and not a person, a freight clerk of a ward market who has never been to the ninth place, and a woman of forty-five who has walked this ground four times and cannot read. **The walk is three hours on an ordinary morning and it took four and a half, and the tenth hand entered that the extra hour and a half was four changeovers, two tape measures, and one hour and eleven minutes of standing still in a drain.** **The reader is not on the walk and said so out loud in a yard on the twenty-first, and the reason is on the face of the sheet: the twelfth stall is to be measured by somebody who is not the reader, and a man who has been that man once does not get to be the counter on the second occasion.**

They were on the six-foot at about ten past seven and the light was hard off the river and good on the embankment, and the tenth hand went in front and did not speak for two hours, which is his practice, and which the other three had all learned to read as a man counting.

---

**And Selik Marne was at the fourth place at about ten to seven in the morning on the twenty-ninth, on the ballast, in his hat, with about forty people behind him, and he did not say a word for about six minutes, and then he said the whole of what he had come to say, which was that he was not going to stop it.**

"You are about to put four people on a railway in walking shoes," he said, "**and I have a standing rule that says nobody goes past the second rail, ever, and it is in Lot 17's terms as well as on my spur, and one of the four of you has argued it with me in a yard in the rain and I am not allowed to argue with it again and I am going to use it.**

"**So I am using it. And here is what it says on a railway, and I have been on a railway in my life twice, and the second rail on a straight is four feet off a man and the second rail on a curve is a foot and a half and the ballast is up to his chest.** I have read the twenty-second's version of your sheet. You are not on the ballast. **You have gone round the whole difficulty by walking on the outside of everything, and that is the correct answer and I am not going to pretend it is not.**

"What I am going to say is the other half, and then I am going to stand here.

"**Four people will be on a line with no keeper at two of the twelve places and no lamp lit at either of them, and the ground under them has been coming apart on and off for eight years, and if something happens on that railway this afternoon then nobody with a whistle is within three miles and nobody with a book is within nine.** I have been right about this for nine years and I have been right about it in a way that nobody wanted, and I have never once been wrong about a ground, and I am not going to be the man who stops four people walking, because **stopping people walking is a Warden's order and this is a public lot, and if it is done it will be done by a room of four seats with no owner and not by me.**

"**That is not enough. I have written it on my own hand for eight years and I am writing it on this, and I am going to say it at the rail this evening on a published day, and I would like the minute to say that I said it here first.**"

And about forty people behind him on the ballast heard a Warden administrator say that he was not going to stop it, and that the safest thing he had to say was *that is not enough*, and nobody argued with him, and one of the four said nine words to him, and the ninth word was *thank you*, and Tarin Kest said the thank you, and it is the first one anybody in this volume has heard him say to Selik Marne about anything at all.

---

**And the changeovers are what the walk actually was, and there are four of them, and the tenth hand marked all four on the twenty-second, and the four of them on the twenty-ninth took about nine minutes between them.**

The first is two hundred yards past the fourth, in the open, four feet deep and about two feet wide at the bottom with standing water in it in the morning, and everybody went round it on the cess lip and nobody minded it. The second is a brick culvert at the fifth with a headwall either side and eleven inches of silt across the whole width, and the woman of forty-five went across it and came back and did it again because she wanted to be sure the silt was the same on both sides, and it was.

The third is at the seventh place and it is not a changeover at all. It is a length of green pipe about four inches across lying across the cess, put there before the water came by somebody who was going to come back and take it away, and nobody ever came back. It is a foot above the walk and it is about nine feet long and it goes into the ballast on one side and stops in the air on the other.

"That is a dead end," said Ivo Stenn. "**A man in a counting room could make a schedule of that in about four minutes, and he could not make a schedule of the two blocks west of the depot in a fortnight, and I have walked five miles of railway today and the easiest thing in this district to put on a sheet is a length of green pipe.**"

The fourth is at the eighth, and it is a step down of two feet six inches into about a foot of standing water, and the woman of forty-five went down it in a walking shoe and came up with the shoe full and said the sentence about walking facing the drop, and then they went round it on the shoulder, four feet wide, with a drop of nine feet off it on the river side, all four of them facing the drop for about thirty yards, and Ivo Stenn did it in the way a man who has decided to do a thing does it.
**And at about ten to eleven they came to the sixth place and the fourth person stopped walking, and the tenth hand stopped because she stopped, and neither Tarin Kest nor Ivo Stenn asked her why, and about nine minutes passed before anybody said anything at all.**

"I am going to say the word," she said, "**and I want all of you to understand what I am saying the word about, because I have read that clause on a sheet and the clause is about a train and I am not saying the word about a train.**

"**I am saying it about that.**"

She was pointing down at the six-foot, and at the cess beside it, and at about four inches of brown water going along the ballast and into a hollow about a yard and a half long and then not going anywhere.

"That drain has come off the top of that bank since before the water came. It has taken the water off that bank for the best ground for two miles for about sixty years, and the man of seventy at the end of that bank looks at the wall behind that train every evening, and he has seen a bank go before and it went in a night. **And the reason he can see a bank going is that the water has got somewhere to go, and this is where it goes, and I have known that for eleven years and so has he and so has everybody in that place.**

"**So I am going to stand in it.**"

---

**And they stood in it for one hour and eleven minutes, and nobody worked out what the word was meant to do until about half past eleven, and the reason is that there was no train and no lever and no thing being held, and the clause says that whoever holds the thing that day has to stop and wait, and there was no thing.**

Tarin Kest worked it out first, at about twenty past eleven, standing in the six-foot with his boot in four inches of water and his hands behind his back.

"**There is no thing,**" he said. "**That is what has stopped the walk and it is not the drain. She has said the word and there is nothing to stop, and the clause is written about a train, and she has found a hole in her own clause in about four minutes and has used it.**

"And it has worked, because we are standing in a drain four inches deep, and a train cannot be on this line at all, and if a train were on this line it could not be here."

"So she has stopped a walk," said the tenth hand.

"**She has stopped a walk,**" said Tarin Kest, "**which is the first thing anybody in this district has ever done, and she has done it by putting a hole in her own instrument, and I have been a route keeper for nineteen years and I have never once seen anybody refuse a road by making their own paper weaker.**"

---

**And the hour and eleven minutes is the first figure anybody in this district has ever put on the cost of a refusal, and it was put on it by a woman at a ward market counter at two o'clock the following afternoon out of four figures and a page, and Bett Ruhl made her read it out twice.**

> **THE PRICE OF THE WORD AT THE SIXTH PLACE, ON THE TWENTY-NINTH OF THIS MONTH, IN A COLUMN HEADLED *WHAT THE THING GOES ON TO NEXT*, WHICH IS THE NAME BETT RUHL GAVE IT AND WHICH HAS SINCE BEEN ENTERED AS THE NAME OF A COLUMN IN THE PUBLIC LOT BOOK:**
>
> **ONE HOUR AND ELEVEN MINUTES OF DAYLIGHT, LOST, AND THE LIGHT GOES OFF THAT EMBANKMENT AT HALF PAST FOUR AND THEY CAME OFF IT AT TEN PAST FOUR.**
>
> **ONE MEASUREMENT NOT TAKEN, WHICH WAS THE LENGTH OF THE DRAIN, AND NOBODY WILL EVER KNOW THE LENGTH OF THE DRAIN, AND THE MAN WHO WOULD HAVE MEASURED IT WAS A SIGNAL CLERK WHO IS PAID NOTHING.**
>
> **FOUR PEOPLE STANDING STILL ON AN EMBANKMENT IN A DRAIN FOUR INCHES DEEP, INCLUDING A WOMAN OF FORTY-FIVE IN WALKING SHOES AND A MECHANIC OF FORTY-THREE WHOSE BOOT IS FULL.**
>
> **AND THE PRICE IS NOT ANY OF THOSE. THE PRICE IS WHAT SHE WAS TALKING ABOUT, WHICH IS A DRAIN THAT WAS THERE BEFORE ANYBODY MADE A DECISION, AGAINST A WALL THAT A MAN OF SEVENTY LOOKS AT EVERY EVENING, AND THE FIGURES ABOVE ARE THE COST OF THE ROOM AND NOT THE COST OF THE THING, AND BETT RUHL HAS SAID, IN HER OWN WORDS, THAT SHE DOES NOT KNOW HOW TO WRITE THE OTHER HALF AND WOULD LIKE SOMEBODY TO DO IT FOR HER.**

And the woman of forty-five gave the other half herself, at about half past four that afternoon on the bank above the ninth place, with four people standing round and the light going off the embankment three hours behind them, and it is in the book in her words and it is the shortest thing anybody has said about a railway in six weeks.

"**Then it should not have a train on it either. And it has had nine vehicles on it since the eleventh of last month.**"

---

**And they came up the bank at about half past one in the afternoon, and the ninth place was there, and the train was there, and it has stood on that siding for forty-six days, and about twenty people were out of the four houses and the rest were not, and the man of seventy was sitting on the low wall behind the fourth house with his back to the train and a stick across his knees.**

He got up when the tenth hand put a foot on the hard ground, and he looked at the four of them and then at the column and then at the man of fifty, and he said one sentence, and it was not to anybody in particular, and it is in the book because Sef Anyard was standing eleven feet away and heard it.

"**You have come about the water.**"

"No," said the woman of fifty. "**We have come about a line on the ground and there is water in it and that is the whole of what has happened today and I will tell you about it after.**"

He sat back down.

---

**And then they measured the twelfth stall, and the measurement is in the book with its method, because a number with no method on it is the thing this whole volume has been about.**

The stall in the floor of that hut is the only one in this district anybody has ever measured in inches. It is not a length of line; it is a floor, nine feet by eleven, with a stove in one corner and a bench along one wall and a table under the window with three chairs at it and no room for four.

**The tenth hand did it with a straightedge off a length of the mantel shelf and a carpenter's level from his own bag, and it took him about twenty minutes, and Sef Anyard was in the room the whole time and did not help and was not asked to and was watching the whole thing with the expression of a woman who has been frightened of something for eight years.**

"**The floor is not level anywhere in this hut,**" he said. "**It drops about four inches from the stove corner to the door, which is what happens in a hut on a bank over a river, and that is not a stall, that is forty years of a man shovelling silt in a door. And here — **"** he put the level on the floor at the left-hand end of the bench, and the bubble ran to the end and stayed, and he marked it with the chalk and did not scrape it, because the rule in this district is that a mark is not struck.

"**Eleven inches.** The floor is eleven inches out of level at the left-hand end of the bench, and it has been for a long time, and there is a crack in the lime putty along that wall about four feet long which goes the same way, and the bench has had a folded blanket under one leg for as long as anybody in this place remembers, and the blanket is under the leg at the low end and not the high end, and it has been under the low end since the winter before the water came.**

"**And that is the twelfth stall and it is in the book and it is measured, and it is the only one in this district that anybody has ever measured in feet and inches, and it has been under a bench leg in a hut nine miles from the nearest public book for eight years while a woman stood on it every morning of her life.**"

Sef Anyard said, "**I know.**"

"I know you know. **I am not asking you whether you know. I am reading a book out loud and you are in it.**"

"**I know I know,**" said Sef Anyard, "**and I have been afraid for eight years of somebody asking me about it, and I want it entered that the reason I was afraid is that if I entered a stall in my own book then I would be a person who had entered a stall, and a person who has entered a stall is a person who has to be asked about it, and there is not one person in nine miles who could have asked me about it and not made me into something.**

"**And it is measured, and it is in a public book, and nobody has made me into anything. I want the finder to have that in whatever he is paid, and I have been told he is paid nothing, and that is a sentence about this district and not about me.**"

---

**And at about half past two in the afternoon, with the light going and the four of them on the hard ground and about twenty people of the ninth place standing about nine feet off, Sef Anyard was asked the question that has been on a public sheet since the twenty-first, and it was asked in her own words and in front of about twenty people and not in a yard, because the sheet said it was to be asked on the twenty-ninth and nobody said where.**

Nessa Pell had written the wording and Bett Ruhl had heard it and Rutt had read it out at the counter four times in four days, and the tenth hand read it from the page.

"**You keep a piece of cast iron in this hut. It is in a remote custody in a public book in Alder Reach and there is no case and no counter and no key, and it is a worse record than a case, and the board has no power to end it and the term is yours. You were asked on the twenty-first whether you wished to hold it and you said yes and the hour was recorded. It is the twenty-ninth. The answer is recorded whatever it is, including no.**"

And Sef Anyard stood in the doorway of a hut with a pencil in her hand and about twenty people of the ninth place standing about nine feet off in the last of the light, and she took about nine seconds, and what she said took about forty, and it is in the book in her words and it is the eleventh part of the twenty-ninth.

"**Yes.**

"And I am going to say the other half and it is not a condition and I do not want it treated as one.

"**For eleven years I have held the only pair on this line and nobody has ever asked me what my job ends on, and the answer in every book in this district is that nobody wrote it down.** And on the twenty-first four people found out that the reason there is no case for that piece of iron is that there is no gap, and that a keeper is a custody that cannot expire, and the word they used was *hiding place*, and they were talking about me.

"**They are right. I have been a hiding place with a form on it since the seventh of this month, and I have had it for eleven years without the form, and the form is not worse and it is not better and it is the only thing I have ever been able to complain about.**

"So: yes, and here is what I want, and it is not a condition and anybody may argue with it. **When the person who reads my line comes to this hut, and reads, and the reading costs him an hour and takes something ordinary off him for about the same time after — I want to be asked whether I wish to speak, before he starts. Not after. Before.**

"**He asked me that at ten to five in the afternoon on the eleventh of this month, two hours and a half after he had sat down at my table, and it is the only sentence anybody in this district has said to me in eleven years that was about me, and I have not told anybody that I noticed it, and you are all hearing it now.**"

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds.

"Then it goes in," said the tenth hand, "**in those words, and it goes in as a thing the keeper has asked for and not as a term, and if he does it again wrong then it is a breach of something and there is no charter and therefore there is nothing to remedy, and I have written that down in four books this month and I do not want it to be true about this.**"

And the fourth person of the four said, at about ten past three in the afternoon, standing on the hard ground with her hands in the front of her cardigan and about twenty people of the ninth place standing about nine feet off, one sentence, and then went up the bank and did not come back down, and it is in the book and it is the last entry of the walk's daylight and it is not a rule and it is not an instrument.

"**Right. So it is a line on the ground and a woman of fifty and a woman of fifty-one and a word. And I have not held it and I am going to say the word on it at four o'clock this afternoon in front of the three of you and I am going to say it once, and then we will all go home, and none of you are going to write down that I was the fourth person.**"

---

**And they came off the bank at about ten past four with about forty minutes of light left on the river and none on the embankment, and the tenth hand wrote the day's four numbers on the back of his own hand on the way home, and one of the four is the only one anybody has ever asked him to keep, and it is not a stall.**

> **GOING: FOUR PEOPLE. COMING: FOUR PEOPLE. STALLS ON THE LINE, GOING, TEN. STALLS ON THE LINE, COMING, ELEVEN, AND I DID NOT COUNT TWELVE EITHER TIME AND THE FLOOR IS THE TWELFTH AND IT IS A FLOOR AND NOT A LENGTH. AND THE DRAIN AT THE SIXTH IS FOUR INCHES GOING AND FOUR INCHES COMING AND WAS FOUR INCHES AT TEN TO ELEVEN AND A MAN'S BOOT IS FULL OF IT.**
>
> **AND I WAS ASKED AT THE BOTTOM OF THE BANK BY THE FOURTH PERSON, IN FRONT OF THREE PEOPLE, TO SAY WHAT THE COUNTS WERE GOING TO BE COUNTS OF, BEFORE WE STARTED, AND I DID NOT KNOW, AND I AM A MAN WHO COUNTS THINGS FOR A LIVING AND I DID NOT KNOW, AND THE SHEET SAID THE COUNTS WERE TO BE TAKEN GOING AND COMING AND DID NOT SAY OF WHAT. I HAVE WRITTEN IT ON THE BACK OF MY HAND AND IT IS GOING ON THE SHEET TOMORROW.**
