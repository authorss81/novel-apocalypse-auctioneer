# Chapter 54: The Hut at Sarrow Water

The hut at Sarrow Water is a brick box about nine feet by eleven with a door that has a hasp and a stove pipe and one window facing the line, and it stands on the cess at the top of a bank eleven feet above the river with the water column on the other side of the siding and nothing else within a mile in any direction except thirty-one people, most of whom are not there in the daytime.

It is nine miles west of Alder Reach along the Needle. It is the ninth of twelve places on the near section. The first four are up on the raised part — a brick and iron viaduct about half a mile long that the whole of Alder Reach can see, and then embankment for a mile more — and the other eight places are at ground level, and a signal on a bridge is a different animal from a signal on a bank, and every keeper on this line has known that since before any of them was born.

**Sef Anyard has kept the hut for eleven years and she is fifty-one and she is the second person anybody in Alder Reach has ever met who could be trusted with a pair.**

They had come out on the ballast, which is the only way anybody comes out to a hut, because there is no cart road and there has not been a cart road to that place in living memory, and the walk is nine miles and takes about three hours going up and about two and a half coming down and most of the three hours is on the embankment, where the ground is soft and where you can see the whole of the line at once if the day is clear and cannot see the fourth place at all if it is not.

The tenth hand of the signal crew came with Adrian Vale and did not say one word for the first two hours, which is his practice, and which everybody who has been on a walk with him has learned to read as the man counting, and which on that morning was him counting **places**: two, three, four, five, and then a gap, and then six, and a gap longer than the first one, and then seven, eight, nine.

At the fourth place they both stopped for about a minute without meaning to, because the fourth place is on the last of the embankment before the line comes down onto the bank, and coming off the embankment at that point puts a man on his feet on ground that has not been walked on in this district for a long time, and the fourth place is a post with a lamp on it and a nameplate that has Harrow on it because everything west of the fourth place is called Harrow in the running line's mind, and **the fourth place is where the stall begins and the stall begins at the fourth place and runs about three hundred yards, and the third place is nine hundred yards back and the fifth place is four hundred yards on, and a train standing in it can be seen from the third place and not from the fifth.**

Orren Pike came out too, at nineteen, having been given the line book to index and having found the column in an afternoon, and he carried a sheet of his own under his coat and did not take it out. He was the third person on the walk and he was the only one of the three who had asked to be there, and he had asked because he wanted to see a hut, and Sef Anyard gave him tea and a dry cloth for his page and told him not to get it wet, and he was pleased about that in a way that a boy of nineteen who is paid nothing is allowed to be pleased.

"There are four things in a hut and there have been four things in a hut for as long as anybody remembers," she said. "The frame. The ground signal, which is a post with a lamp on it that shows a train the line is not clear. The book, which is mine, by hand, every day. And the lever that throws the points at this end.

"And a fifth thing, which is the water, and the water belongs to the road house and not to me and I have a key to the column and I have never once in eleven years opened it for myself and that is not modesty, that is because a key to a column is a key to somebody's bill."

---

**The stall is her word and not the district's and the district has adopted it, and she is pleased about that and says so.**

"A stall," she said, "is where the ground has stopped agreeing with itself.

"It is not a flood and it is not a wash and it is not a hole. The rail is there and the ballast is there and the four-foot is where the four-foot is. And a train can stand in it perfectly well. I have had a train stand in one for nine hours. What will not do is **the question of where it is.** In a stall, a train cannot be told where it is, and cannot be told where it came from, and cannot be told whether it has been somewhere, and the driver will tell you he knows perfectly well where he is, and he will be wrong in a way that is not a lie. It is a wrongness in the thing. **The ground has stopped agreeing with itself and so nobody in the stall can be agreed on.**"

"How many are there?"

"That is the sort of thing that goes in a book and not in a conversation." She put the kettle on. "Nineteen times I have thrown this lever to send a train back. All nineteen of them since the water came. **Nine months, and the west end went under, and everything on this line now stops here, and there is nothing to do with a train on a dead end but send it back the way it came, and I have done it nineteen times.**

"And the first two I did not know what I was doing, and a man came out from Alder Reach and showed me on a page, and he wrote it in the book after me, and I have his writing in it still, and his writing is very small.

"**Eleven of the nineteen left a stall.** That is what the line book says and I have never argued with the line book about anything. And the other eight did not, and I have never been able to tell you the difference, and I have been on a hut for eleven years and I have looked at this every morning of it."

---

**The Tongue is under the bench and it comes out onto a mat.**

It is cast iron, about the length of a forearm and the width of a hand, one end cut into the shape of a wedge, and the point of the wedge is worn to a shine, and the other end has a hole in it and a loop of signal wire through the hole, and the wire is red and the paint is off it. It weighs about four pounds and it is cold all the time, which Sef Anyard says is normal for iron that has been in a hut for eight years and which she nevertheless mentioned first, twice, before she mentioned anything else about it.

"It came out of Harrow in the fourth year," she said. "The low end of that interchange was under about two feet then and a salvage crew went in on a Tuesday with a pump and a rope, and they brought up this, and a lever frame for the east end, and four hundredweight of rail, and they brought up this because somebody on the crew said it would be wanted. **It is under nine feet now. The water came up in the flood and it did not go down, and the west end of this line has been dead since, and everything that goes west stops at Harrow and does not go into it.**"

"**And nobody has ever wanted it. It has been in eleven huts and three sheds and a box in a wagon for eight years, and I have had it in this hut for five.** The Road House men know it is here. The road house men have seen it. Nobody has ever asked me what it does and nobody has ever asked me to do anything with it, and I want you to understand that those are two different not-askings and the second one has been going on for eight years."

---

**And then she told him what it does, in her own words, at about eleven in the morning on the seventh, and it took her about four minutes, and she did not use the word origin once, and she has never in her life used it.**

"You take the point off the running line," she said, "into the loop that goes down the bank and round and up past the column and back onto the running line, which is a headshunt, and it is four hundred yards and it is not on the map because it was for water and the water stopped in the fifth year.

"And you send a train down it and round and up, and you put it back on the running line **facing the other way.** That is the whole of the mechanism and it takes eleven seconds and it does not need a great deal of cleverness. And I have done it twice in five years and the man from Alder Reach did it once in my first year and I have heard of it being done twice at the other end.

"And I am going to tell you what it costs, and I am telling you because **it is not a price and it is not a loss and nobody can point at it, and I have had eight years to point at it.**

"**The train comes back and it does not know where it came from.**

"Not that it is damaged. It is not. It comes back with its lamps and its brake and its chalk marks and its nine vehicles all exactly as they went down, and the men get off it, and they are not frightened and they are not hurt, and then somebody says *where did we come out from* and everybody on that train **knows the answer**, and they get it right, and **it is not the place.**

"It is the way. It is the road. It is four streets and a bank and a gate and the smell of the water column, and every man on that train could walk it in his sleep and has walked it in his sleep, and not one of them can tell you which of the twelve places it was standing at when it started, and there were thirty-one vehicles of us on that line in the first year and every one of us was asked where we started and **not one of us could say.**

"**And then they stop asking.** That is the part. In the first year they asked. I remember the asking. And by the third year nobody on my train asked anybody anything at all, and I thought that was the men getting used to it, and it was not, and it took me until about the sixth year to work out that **the question is what goes.** You cannot go on wanting to know where you started, because wanting to know is a thing you have, and the wanting is a thing you do not."

---

**And the eleventh reversal, she said, is the one that made a person in her hut say a thing out loud that she has not been able to unhear.**

"In the sixth year there was a train that had come down from the west and had gone as far as this place, and I had a man with me, a young one, and he was doing it for the first time, and when it came round and stopped on the running line facing east he got off it and he looked at me and he said, *that's us, isn't it,* meaning the hut, meaning here, and I said yes, and he was pleased, and he said — this is his word, not mine — he said, *I know it was us, I just couldn't have told you.*

"And I said *what do you mean, you could have told me, you were standing on it.* And he said, and I have it exactly: **'I know it was us. I could not have told you it was us from here. Those are two different things and I have been the second one all week.'**"

She looked at the window for a while.

"That is the toll and I cannot give it to you in a form, and I have tried for eight years, and this is the fifth time I have said it out loud to anybody and the other four times I have said it to a man from the water office who wrote it down and did not come back."

---

**And the book, and the column.**

She brought the hut book out. It is a hard-backed ledger about two inches thick and the leaves are stiff with the damp and about half of them are used.

"Every day, and every movement, in my hand, in the order it happened, with the time against it, and that has been the practice on this line since before I came and I am not going to be the one to stop it. Sixty-one movements since the water came, and I have got them all, and I have got the four that are wrong in a different ink because I found them wrong and did not want to rub out my own."

"The line book at the east end has got a column in it," said Adrian Vale, "that a copy clerk of seventeen has taken forty-one movements off and says about a fifth of the entries in that column are not places."

"Yes." She turned the hut book round. "I keep the same column. It is headed **ORIGIN HELD** and I have never had a use for it, and I have kept it for eleven years because the man who wrote it in before me kept it, and I asked him once in my first year what it was for and he said *you'll find out one day* in the voice men use when they do not know.

"And he was right in a way I did not like. **I have found out one day. About four of the entries in it are not places.**"

She put her finger on it. There were four lines in a column of sixty-one and Adrian Vale read them and read them again, because a man who has spent seventeen years putting figures on things does not read a figure once.

"Three of them are places and I know them," she said. "This one's Sarrow Water and this one's the fourth place and this one's Harrow, and Harrow is under nine feet of water and I have never sent a train from Harrow in my life, and **I did not write Harrow in that column, because I have never been able to see it, and I have never thrown a lever for it, and it is in my hand and it is in my ink.**

"And this one."

"**It is not a place and it is not a number and it is not a time.**"

The name in the column is in a small hand and it is spelled out in full rather than contracted, which nobody does on this line except when a keeper is copying something out of a book, and it is nine words long:

> **THE STANDING OFFICE OF CROSS-DISTRICT PASSAGE, ESTABLISHED, AND NOT AT THIS PLACE.**

"**And that is the fourth of the four and I have had it eight years and I have never once written to anybody about it,**" said Sef Anyard, "and you are going to tell me that it is a filing office, and I am going to tell you that a filing office is not on a line between the fourth place and a flooded interchange in a column headed for where a train started. **And I am going to tell you the other thing, which is that in the year I came here there were two more of them and I have never found out where those two went, and I have not looked, because looking has never been one of the four things in a hut.**"

Orren Pike took his sheet out at that point, because he could not help it, and put the two columns side by side on the bench, hers in pencil and stiff with damp and his in ink, and worked down them for about two minutes in silence, and then said:

"**Mine has four and hers has four and they are not the same four.**"

"Mine has four," said Sef Anyard, "and yours has four, and the name you have just read out is one of mine, and if it is not in yours then there is a fifth in a column of sixty-one that the line book at the east end has not got, and that means somebody wrote it in a hut and did not enter it, and **in this district that is either a keeper who did not want the trouble or a clerk who did not have a hut.**"

---

**And the water, and the lease, and the twenty days, which is the part of the morning that had nothing to do with a signal and everything to do with the volume.**

The column stands on the other side of the siding in a concrete base with a brass plate and a tap and a lock, and the lock is a road house lock, and the brass plate says in stamped letters the name of the **Needle Road House**, Alder Reach, and under it a number, and the number is a meter number and not a place, and nobody in Alder Reach has ever asked whose it is because it is on a piece of brass on a column in a field.

"There is a book in Alder Reach with the readings in it," said Sef Anyard, "because a column is metered and a metered column is a book, and I have never seen that book and I have never been asked to see it, and I have drawn off that column for eleven years for a road house that is four men and a foreman and a great deal of noise, and **the men come up here twice a week for water and they have never once asked me a question about anything except the water.**

"That is not their fault and I want it written down that it is not their fault. They are doing a job and the job is water. **It is ours, and mine, for having a tap nine feet from a hut and no reason to ever be in the same room as the people who use it.**"

The train standing on the siding is nine vehicles, an engine and eight, with the brake on and the cab locked and a man having come in on the eleventh of last month with a piece of chalk and said *stabled, she'll sit*, and the Road House men have been drawing water against that meter ever since. Twenty days of it. And there is no Road House shed, no Road House man and no Road House sign within a mile of the siding, and the siding itself is not in any public book in Alder Reach, and the lease by which it is held is in a room in a building in the ward market district, and **the lease is a lease and not a charter, and there was nothing to consent to at the time it was signed, because the thing being leased was a piece of ground with a siding on it and a water column and no platform, and nobody was going anywhere, and no one saw a village in it, because there is no platform to tell you that a place is a place a train is meant to stop at.**

---

**And the finding that made the day, and it is at the foot of the seventh and it is not a surprise and it is the whole of the volume in one line.**

There is a train standing on the siding at Sarrow Water. It has been there since the eleventh of last month, which is twenty days. It is nine vehicles and it came in on its own wheels with a man in the cab who said it was stabled, and the Road House men have been drawing water off the column against a meter for twenty days and the meter is in a book in Alder Reach, and the train has not moved and cannot be moved and everybody in the district knows it cannot be moved, and nobody has written down why.

**The section between the fourth place and the fifth has been in the line book since the third year as a stall, and it has never been entered as cleared, and it has never been entered as safe, and both of those are different entries and the district has never had a column for them.**

Sef Anyard said it standing at her window with her hand on the frame.

"You can only get that train east by turning it," she said. "**And to turn it you have to go round the headshunt, and the signal has to take the origin, and the origin of that train is this siding, because that is where it is standing and there is nowhere else on this line for it to have started from.**

"And I am not a clever woman and I am not a reader and I have never read anything in my life except a signal manual from before the Fracture. And I have been turning trains for nine months and I have never once asked where it goes, because I did not think that was a question anybody asked. **And I have been standing in a hut nine miles from Alder Reach for eight years being asked for water off a meter.**"

---

**And Adrian Vale had walked nine miles with a piece of brass in a bag and a boy of seventeen's sleeve in his hand and he had been in that hut since about half past nine in the morning, and at about ten to twelve Sef Anyard stopped answering him.**

"You have been in this hut since half past nine," she said, "and you have asked me about the lever and the stall and the book and the column and the train, and you have not asked me one single question about me, and I have been in this hut answering a reader for two hours and a half."

"No."

"Then I will not answer the last one." She put the book down on the bench. "**I will answer anything you like about the iron, and about the line, and about what the column does with a train, and I have told you all of it and more than I have told anybody in this district in eleven years. And you have not asked me whether I want to talk about it, and I am fifty-one years old and I have kept a hut on a line for eleven years and nobody has ever asked me that.**

"So ask me. And then I will tell you the number I have, and the number is not the number in your book, and I want the number that is mine to go in the book as the number that was said first by the person who lives here, and I want it read back to me in this hut in front of you, and **I want the number in your book to go in beside it even if it is different from mine, and I want it printed that they were different.**"

Adrian Vale put the bag down on the bench, on top of the hut book, and said, "**Mrs. Anyard. I am sorry. I have come nine miles with a piece of brass in it and I have been talking about brass. Do you want to talk about it?**"

"Yes," said Sef Anyard. "**And I want the first thing said to be the number.**"

"There are eleven stalls in the line book and I have been told there are eleven and I have not seen them."

"**There are twelve.**" She said it without any weight at all. "There is one in this hut. **It is the floor I am standing on, and it is not on your line book, and it is the only one I am certain of, and I have been certain of it for eight years, and I have never put it in my own book, because a stall that I put in my own book is a stall I have to be asked about, and I have been afraid for eight years of being asked about it.**"

And she got the ledger out and turned to the first leaf of the twelfth of the month before last and picked up a pencil, and her hand was not steady, and the entry she made took her about a minute and a half, and it is the eleventh page of the public lot book of Lot 17 for the seventh of this month and it is the first entry in the history of that book that was written in a different hand at a different table in a different room nine miles from the building, and there is a line under it in the registrar's hand on the eighth which says:

> **ENTERED BY S. ANYARD, KEEPER, AT THE HUT AT SARROW WATER, ON THE SEVENTH OF THIS MONTH, AT TEN TO TWELVE IN THE MORNING, AT THE REQUEST OF A READER WHO HAD ASKED HER FOR TWO HOURS AND A HALF BEFORE HE ASKED HER WHETHER SHE WISHED TO SPEAK. THE TWELFTH STALL IS IN THE BOOK. THE COUNT OF STALLS IS TWELVE AND NOT ELEVEN. AND THE NUMBER THAT WAS SAID FIRST WAS TWELVE, AND THE READER'S BOOK DID NOT HAVE IT, AND BOTH NUMBERS ARE IN THE BOOK AND NEITHER IS STRUCK.**
