# Chapter 56: The Tongue of Harrow

Adrian Vale had not read anything since the eighteenth of last month and it was the eleventh of this one, and the reason he had not was not a rule and not a limit and not a decision anybody had asked him to make.

It was that on the eighteenth he had read a piece of brass twice, an hour each time, and the second hour had left him with an hour in which he could not tell whether a thing was nearer or further than it had been, and he had sat on a crate afterwards and worked his way back into the ordinary world by counting the boards, and **the counting had taken him nine minutes and he had not written the nine minutes down in a book that anybody else can read, and that was the private part of it, and the private part was the reason.**

So when he went out to Sarrow Water on the eleventh with a satchel and a sheet of paper and a pencil, he told Sef Anyard on the doorstep what he was going to do, in the plainest words he had, which is the practice now and is not a courtesy, and which is the whole of the change in him that five weeks has made.

"I am going to read your lever," he said. "**I am going to do it on your table with your book shut, and it will take about an hour, and after it I will not be able to do some ordinary things for about the same time again, and I will not be able to hold a pair, and I am not holding one and I am not driving and I am not a witness on anything.** And I will write it down at your table and I will take the sheet away and it will go into a public book in Alder Reach on Tuesday, and the book is nine miles away and it is the first entry in the history of that book that will have been taken somewhere else."

"Then do it at my table," said Sef Anyard, "and I will be here, and if you go out of the room I will bring you back and I will not make a thing of it."

---

**The reading is entered in the lot book of Lot 17 on the twelfth of this month, on page eleven, from a sheet made at a kitchen table nine miles west, and the entry was made by two people and not by him, and the reason is in the entry and the reason is that he was not in the building.**

The sheet went into a sleeve. The sleeve has a number on it and the sheet has a number on it and they are the same number, which is what Fen Ambrose did with a margin on the fourth and what Nessa Pell did with forty-one movements on the second, and the practice is three weeks old and it is now the practice. **The reading was read off the sheet by Nessa Pell and Marda Osk together at about ten past eleven in the morning on the twelfth, against his own hand on page four and page nine, and the entry says so, and it says that a sheet that goes into a book loose is a sheet that comes out loose, and it is the reason the book can be taken cold by a stranger and still be followed.**

And here is the reading, in the book's own words, in the form it was given, and it is five lines and a finding and the finding is the whole of the trouble:

> **LOT: THE HARROW TONGUE. CAST IRON. TAKEN OUT OF THE FLOODED HARROW INTERCHANGE IN THE FOURTH YEAR. USED THIRTEEN YEARS. IN THIS HUT FIVE.**
>
> **USE: SEND ONE VEHICLE BACK ALONG THE SECTION IT CAME FROM. ONE MOVEMENT. ONE WAY. THE USE IS NOT GIVEN BACK.**
>
> **TOLL: THE ORIGIN.**
>
> **STANDING: A PERSON WHO CAN HOLD A SIGNAL.**
>
> **AND THIS IS THE FINDING AND IT IS ENTERED AS A FINDING AND NOT AS A CONCLUSION: THE OBJECT DOES NOT HOLD PLACES. THE TOLL IS NOT STATED IN A FORM THAT CAN BE READ AT A PERSON, OR A HOUSEHOLD, OR A TOWN, OR A WATER, AND THE OBJECT DOES NOT SAY WHAT THE ORIGIN IS, AND ASKED FOR MORE IT REPEATS ITSELF.**
>
> **AND: ELEVEN USES ARE ON THIS LINE AND THE OBJECT DOES NOT COUNT THEM AND DOES NOT KNOW THEM AND HAS NEVER ONCE BEEN ASKED.**

The ledger entry above it is in Nessa Pell's hand and it is the first reading entered in the new month and it is one hour, and the finding entered with it is the rule the district has not had before: **the reader is not to read again before the eighteenth of this month**, and the hour is published, and the reason is printed at the top of the entry in his own words, which is that the last reading cost two hours and he has not yet found out whether the second one cost him something he has not noticed.

---

**And then the yard asked what the origin was, and three people in about nine minutes gave three different answers, and a fourth arrived from the floor, and Adrian Vale refused to choose between any of them in a yard of about six hundred people on the twelfth of this month, and the reason he gave is the sentence this district has been arguing about for five weeks and has never once said in a yard.**

"It is three things," he said. "And I have read the object and the object says one word and I am not going to supply the rest of it, and I would like the yard to understand exactly how strange it is that a man who has spent seventeen years putting numbers on things is standing here refusing to do the only useful thing he is capable of.

"**One. The origin is the place the vehicle is standing.** That is what the man who came out from Alder Reach told Sef Anyard in her first year on a page, and she has had his writing in her book ever since, and it is the answer that the mechanics give you and it is the answer that makes the book work, because then a train at Sarrow Water costs Sarrow Water, and Sarrow Water is a place a person can be sent to.

"**Two. The origin is the first place the vehicle was entered into the line's book.** That is a signalman's answer and it is the right answer if you are a signal and it is a different answer if you are a village.

"**Three. The origin is the settlement whose hut knows about the vehicle.** That is not anybody's answer and it is mine, and I want it struck, and it cannot be struck, so I am going to say it and let the room decide whether I am a clever man or a man who has found a fourth possibility and does not like it."

And then he stopped, and the yard noticed that he had stopped, and waited for a fourth, and there was not going to be one from him.

The yard argued for about forty minutes and it argued well, and two people came up with better answers than any of the three, and one of those was a carter of about fifty who said that in his trade the origin of a thing is the place you can trace it back to and if you cannot trace it back then it has not got one, and that the object had just told them a train with no origin, and that this was not a riddle and was an inventory.

"**Then that is the fourth one, and it is not one of mine, and it is worse than all three of mine,**" Adrian said. "Because it means the toll does not land on the place the train is standing in. **It lands on the place the train can be traced back to. And a thing that cannot be traced back is a thing with no origin, and the only way a place is going to get a train with no origin is if the place put it there.**"

"And who put that train there?" said the tenth hand, from the rail.

"**I did.**"

And the yard made a sound, and Adrian Vale did not fill the silence, because the whole of the last five weeks has been him filling silences with a figure.

---

**And the eighth line, and it is the reason the reading is not a charter and never could have been, and it is in the book in full because the line is the standing line and the line is not struck.**

**No goods, no freight, and no carriage of any kind except the person who is carried as a person.**

It came out of a counterfeit's second page in the fourth week of last month and it was written about a bus, and it was argued by a trader and seconded by a barrow-hauler, and Adrian has not tried to soften it once, and on the twelfth of this month the yard found out that it does not so much complicate the Harrow Tongue as **stand outside it and refuse to have anything to do with it**, and here is what Marda Osk said about it and it is the best four minutes in that yard:

"**The line says the only carriage is a person carried as a person. This is not a carriage. This is a set of points in a loop with a lever, and what it moves is a set of wagons, and there is nobody in the use at all.** So the line is not broken by this. The line was never about this. **And the reason the line matters is that it was written about a vehicle that carries people, and the rule we actually want out of it is the small one, and the small one is: a person standing inside a vehicle that is being moved is carried as a person.**"

"Which is not the line."

"Which is not the line, and the line is not struck, and I would like the minute to say that the line has just been found to be about a bus and not about everything, and that it took an iron lever and a carter of fifty to find out."

**And then the yard asked the only question in the chapter, and it asked it in about nine seconds, and it was the tenth hand, and it is in the book as asked and not answered:**

"**If a person is carried as a person, what does a person pay when a train is turned round?**"

Nessa Pell read the reading again from the sheet, because that is what she does, and the fifth line says the toll is the origin, and the finding says the toll is not stated in a form that can be read at a person, or a household, or a town, or a water.

"So they pay nothing," said Orren Pike, from about eight feet away, in a voice nobody expected, because he had said nothing in a yard for a month and had been at the rail.

"They pay nothing," said Nessa Pell.

"**Then the passengers on that train are free.**" He put his hand flat on the table. "**And the Last Fare takes the memory of why from every person who crosses it and it takes it again on the way back, and this thing takes nothing from anybody who is on it, and we have been calling it the same kind of thing for two days.** The road charges the people. **This one charges a place, and it does it quietly, and it does not even charge the place directly — it charges where the place is in a book.**"

Nobody answered him for about eleven seconds, and then Nessa Pell said the sentence that is the finding of the twelfth:

"**Then the toll's destination is a place, and we have no instrument in this district that can be pointed at a place, and I have spent eight years writing instruments.**"

---

**And at about four o'clock in the afternoon of the eleventh, in a hut nine miles west, a man who had been reading for an hour got up off a bench and could not tell which way the river went.**

He did not fall and he did not say anything about it. He stood by the window with a sheet of paper in his hand and about eleven inches of a cast-iron lever on the bench behind him, and he could see the water column, and he could see the siding and the bank and the four houses and the chapel with the bell on it, and he could not say whether any of it was above him or below him, or whether the river was on his left or on his right, and it was on his right and he had walked nine miles along the top of it.

"You are all right," said Sef Anyard, from the far side of the hut, in the voice of a woman who had been expecting this. "Sit down. You have got about forty minutes of it and then it comes back in the order it went, and the order it goes is near, and then far, and then up, and then the rest of it, and there is no way to hurry it and I have watched a man do it."

"How many times have you watched a man do it?"

"**Three.**" She put the kettle on. "Eleven reversals that left a stall and three readings and all three of them were the same man and one of them was me, in the sense that I stood where he was standing and I could not tell you which way the river went for about half an hour, and I have never said that to anybody because there is no column for it and because I am the keeper."

"**Mrs. Anyard — you did not have to tell me that.**"

"No," she said. "**I did not have to, and I want it in your book that I did not have to, because in about six weeks somebody in this district is going to write a rule about who may read a signal, and I am the person who is going to be caught by it, and put one line on a page somewhere that says the keeper cannot tell which way the river went either, and did not want to be asked.**"

He wrote it down. It took him nine minutes to be able to hold a pencil properly and he wrote it down in the hut, and it is on the sheet, and the sheet went into the sleeve, and the sleeve has a number on it.

---

**And the thing that everybody argued about on the twelfth was not the reading, and it is entered first in the day's book because it came first, and it is the reason there is no charter and no proposal and no closing anywhere in this district on the twelfth of this month, and it is about a satchel.**

A satchel was a reasonable idea. The object is a piece of cast iron about the length of a forearm and it weighs about four pounds and it can be carried in one hand, and if it were in the case in the office behind the sale floor then it would be inspectable on stated days and describable at a counter by a reader, and the whole of the arrangement the district has spent five weeks building would apply to it, and nobody would have to walk nine miles ever again.

Three people put it in the yard and it was refused in about six minutes by three people who were arguing with each other while they refused it.

**The first is a custody rule.** The Last Fare was moved once in this book's history, on an inactive custody charter that named a finder, a receiving bonded depot, a term, two witnesses, and a remedy for an unclaimed delivery, and it moved custody only and never authorised use. A used object with a working standing is a different animal, and nobody in Alder Reach has ever written the paper for moving one, and the paper's absence is a hole and not a permission.

**The second is mechanical and it is the one that stopped it.** The tongue is the only thing at that hut that throws the points. Take it out of the hut and the points at the ninth place cannot be thrown at all, which means the ground signal at the ninth place cannot be cleared, which means no train can pass the ninth place, which means **the train on the siding is not a train that is parked. It is a train that is the west end of the line.**

"Write that in a way," said the tenth hand, "because I have been trying to tell people that for two days and nobody would have it. **That siding is not a place a train is kept. It is a place the line stops.**"

**The third is the one that the minute says is the reason and it took about ninety seconds.**

"It is in daily use," said Marda Osk, "by a person who is in no book."

There is a long entry about that and it is the first time the board has had to say it, and it says this: **Lot 17's case is a case, and a case is where a thing goes so that it is not in anybody's hands. The thing in that case has a condition, a term, a holder named before the key is held, and a public day on which anybody may be taken in to look at it. Every one of those five is a thing about the object.** The Harrow Tongue is not in a case. It is on a bench in a hut, in a keeper's room, and a woman uses it. **There is no custody of it because there is no gap. A keeper is a custody that cannot be inspected, cannot be described at a counter, and cannot expire, because nobody has written down when her job ends, and it has run for eleven years, and she has never been asked whether she would like it in a book with a term on it.**

"So the thing at the ninth place has no lot," said Mara Quill, "and therefore this board has nothing to sit on, and I am going to say the rest of it because nobody else in this yard is going to. **A board's powers are about a thing in a room. All five of them. A term, a holder, a witness, a publication, a refusal. And there is a thing nine miles west of this yard that has killed a bank, and a keeper, and a stall in a hut floor, and a village, and none of it is in this building, and I cannot enter it.**"

"What can you enter?" said Adrian Vale.

"**A reading.**" She said it slowly, because she was working it out as she said it and the yard could hear her working it out. "I can enter that a reading was taken, on a sheet, at a table, by a named person, at an hour, with its duration and its limit, and two other people compared the hand against his hand before it went in. **That is not a lot and it is not a charter and it confers nothing and it authorises nobody to move anything and it seats nothing, and it is the first line of the terms-closing with the lot taken out of it.**"

"Then it is a thing with no lot," said Sabin Tull, "and it is a thing this book has never held, and I want it given a name on the spot by somebody who is not me, because I have been in four rooms this month that did not have names and it is becoming a habit."

"**A reading with no lot**," said Adrian Vale. "Enter it that and put my name on it as the man who read and yours as the people who entered it, and put it in the first line that **it is not evidence, because evidence is a thing connected to a claim and we have not stated a claim, and it is not a relic, because nobody is going to look at it, and it is not a charter, because there is nothing to seat.**"

"It is four words," said Nessa Pell, "and I have been a clerk for nineteen years and note that it is the first instrument this district has ever made that is about **a thing and not about anybody.**"

---

**And he walked the nine miles back in the dark with Orren Pike, and it took four hours and ten minutes because he twice stopped in the middle of the ballast and had to be told which way the water was, and the tenth hand went as far as the fourth place and then turned back, and said why, and it is in the book.**

"I have walked this line eleven times since the nineteenth and I have counted it every time," he said. "**I am not going to walk it tonight with a man who cannot tell where the river is, and then have a book that says two people walked it.** That is not caution. That is the whole of what a count is. **A count with a man in it who cannot see is not a smaller count. It is a different number.**"

And the boy of seventeen walked the last four miles with a man who asked him, twice, which way east was, and who got it right the second time both times, and who said at the gate of the yard at about a quarter to ten at night, in a yard that was empty, to a boy of seventeen who was unpaid:

"Mr. Pike. I have been in this business seventeen years and I have never once had a person walk me home who was paid nothing and who did not want anything off me, and I have thought about it about four times in the last ten minutes and I have not got anywhere, and you noticed, and so did I."

"I noticed too," said Orren Pike, "and I have not got anywhere either, and my mother is on a piece of paper in my coat and it is not going in that book, so you are the only person in Alder Reach who does not know about it, and that is where I have left it."

---

**And at the door, at about twenty to five in the afternoon, on the eleventh, before he went, he asked her the question he should have asked on the seventh at half past nine in the morning and had not asked for two hours and a half, which is that she might not wish to speak at all.**

"Mrs. Anyard. Before I go. I should have asked you this two days ago and I am asking it now, and I am not asking you about the lever. **Did you want to talk about it?**"

"No," said Sef Anyard. "**I wanted somebody else to come.**"

"I will come back."

"**Come on the fifteenth,**" she said, "and bring somebody who is not a reader, and I do not care who it is as long as it is not you, and I will tell you exactly why and it is the last thing I am going to say today.

"**Because you will write down what I tell you and it will be true, and a district that can only hear about a village from a man who puts numbers on things is not a district that has heard about a village.** And I have been alone in a hut for eleven years and I would like one afternoon in the next four weeks where somebody walks up that bank who is not paid to be interested in me, and there is not one of those in nine miles."

And Adrian Vale said, "The fifteenth," and wrote it in the book on the twelfth, under the reading, in the finding, as a line with a name on it and not a duty: **A KEEPER HAS ASKED FOR A FIFTEENTH. THE FIFTEENTH IS ENTERED AND NOBODY IS BOUND BY IT AND IT WILL BE KEPT.**
