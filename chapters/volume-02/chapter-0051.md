# Chapter 51: The Third of the Month

The month turned on a Tuesday and nobody in Alder Reach noticed it, which was the first thing about it that the registrar entered and the second thing she was wrong about.

Nessa Pell had been the sitting steward of Lot 17 for five weeks and the only person in the building holding a key to anything, and on the morning of the first of the new month she came in at about half past six, unlocked the office behind the sale floor, and found that the thing she had been carrying in the front of her mind for two days was not a date.

It was an absence.

The public lot book lies on a table under eleven boards. The foot of the page for the twenty-sixth carried the entry she has written in her own hand about a crossing performed once, and a custody term, and a name, and four words. Above all of that, in the margin of the twenty-sixth, in Hester Vane's own block capitals, there was a line that said:

> **MY TERM IS SEVEN DAYS FROM THE TWENTY-SIXTH AT ABOUT ELEVEN IN THE MORNING AND IT EXPIRES AT MIDNIGHT ON THE THIRD. THE HOUR IS PUBLISHED. THE PERSON HOLDING IT ON THE DAY IT EXPIRES IS TO BE NAMED IN THIS BOOK BEFORE IT IS HELD OR IT IS NOT HELD.**

And below that line there was nothing at all.

Two days to go and no name. That was the whole of it, and it sat there on a fair page in a fair hand looking like an answer, and Nessa Pell stood and looked at it for a while and then went and did the ordinary work of the morning, which is: the counter opens at six at the ward market and she is not the reader at the counter, and the boards have to be up before the sale floor, and there were four lots in the flood week that had to be re-priced because a man was pretending he was not under pressure to sell.

She got the boards up at about a quarter to seven and she did not say anything to anybody, and at about ten past nine she walked out to the tram arch, where Halloway Reed's man had a bench and a stack of blank claim stock and was doing briskly what he always did, and she stood at the edge of it for four minutes and then went back inside, and the going and coming is in the book because she entered it at the time and the whole of the point of the hour being published is that a clerk who fails at a published hour is watched.

---

**The board sat at about eleven on the first, all four seats named aloud, and the one item of business was a key, and for the first time in the history of this lot it had nothing to sit on.**

Mara Quill as registrar, not a vote. Nessa Pell as sitting steward, with a key in her own hand that had stopped being hers on the twenty-sixth and had been in a cooper's wife's coat since about eleven that morning. Sabin Tull and Marda Osk in the two unclaimed places, and the unclaimed places were on a rota now, because a rota had been written out on the back of a page from the lease schedule Adrian Vale entered as a held asset on the twelfth of the month before last, which has four copies, and which is the only document in this district with four copies of a rota on it, and which nobody has ever thought was funny until this afternoon.

"There are five things this board can do," Sabin Tull said, reading them off the wall where they had been painted in the fourth week and never taken down. "Refuse to record a closing. Publish a result. Call a review. Uphold a challenge in part. Set or renew a short public custody term. And it cannot name a person."

"It can name a holder," Nessa said. "It named Hester Vane in under a minute and read her qualifications out loud to four hundred people and she said them in her own words and then took the key. That is inside the fifth thing. Setting a term means somebody holds it."

"Then somebody has to be in the book before the term, and the book is empty, and the board has to sit down tomorrow and do the same thing again, and on the third it will be midnight and there will be nothing to sit on." Sabin Tull put his hands flat on the table. "I want it understood that the board is not refusing. The board is sitting here with five powers and the one it needs is a sixth, and the sixth is *ask a person whether they will do a job*, and no board in this city has ever had that power, and if it did have that power then every board in this city would be a hiring office."

"That is right," Mara said. "And it is also not the problem, and Put in the minute that the problem is one day older than the board."

---

**Hester Vane came at about half past three in the afternoon on the first, with the key in a coat and nine pounds of somebody else's seed still in a canvas bag at her husband's, and she had come because the hour had been published and she is a person who turns up at published hours, which is the only qualification anybody has ever been able to give her.**

"Can I say a thing," she said, "and have it go in the book, and then not be argued with?"

"That is two-thirds of what this yard is for," Nessa said. "Say it."

"Right." Hester Vane took a breath. "**I will not name whoever takes it off me.**

"And I want it written that I understood the rule when it was read to me and I agreed with it, and I want it written that a rule nobody has had to keep is not a rule, because I have been in a yard for a month and I have watched three of them get argued into the ground by a man with a page, and if I am allowed to hand this key to a friend of mine in a doorway on Tuesday then this book will be a book with a drawer in it and the drawer is me.

"**Mr. Kest wrote a sentence in this yard and I have it in my head and I am going to say it back to you.** The moment one man is the man who says no to the road being longer, the road is his, and it is a queue with a timetable. A key is a smaller road. I have had it for six days and I have learned exactly one thing about it and the one thing is that a woman who cannot say why she set out is going to be handed a key by people who need her to be reliable, and being reliable is not the same as being answerable, and I want somebody else to have to think about that."

There were about sixty people in that yard and none of them said anything for a while.

"Who should it be, then," Marda Osk said, "since you will not say?"

"That is exactly the question I am refusing and you are asking it in the voice of somebody who wants to go home." Hester Vane was not being cruel. She had a way of saying a thing that made the room feel the size of it. "**I will not name the person after me. I will tell you how to find them.**"

---

**And the way to find them took until about ten at night on the second, and it took two people saying the obvious thing at half past six in the morning in a doorway, neither of whom was in the yard.**

Nessa Pell had walked in at about half past six and found Bett Ruhl on the step of the exchange with a bowl of something and a slate under her arm, waiting to be let in, which she was always allowed to be let in and had never once asked to be.

"He still hasn't said who," Bett Ruhl said. "I heard it from the chandler's boy. Hasn't said, and won't."

"He has said why. He has said it very well."

"Yes, and he is a cooper's wife and there is a cooper's apprentice in this district." Bett Ruhl put the bowl down on the step. "**Nessa. The book has to have a name in it. A mark can't do it, because a mark can't carry a job, and you all found that out the hard way five weeks ago and it is on the wall in here.**

"And Wren Ostler reads out everything anybody in this district puts on a counter. Six years. She is in no book in this city, and it is not because she is protected and it is not because she is hiding. **It is because nobody has ever thought to ask her whether she has got a name she wants in one.**"

---

**What happened at about ten past eight in the morning on the second is in the book in four lines and the registrar wrote all four of them herself and read two of them out in the yard afterwards, and the yard had about thirty people in it, and the thirty people were mostly people who had come to find out whether a key was going to be in a drawer.**

Nessa Pell had walked down to the ward market counter at a quarter to seven, which is not her counter and not her hour, and Rutt was not there, and the woman behind the counter was putting out the slate that said what the rate was this quarter and what the reading cost and that anybody might bring a claim and have the whole of it read out in words they did not bring with them.

"Mrs. Ostler."

"Warden Pell." She had been getting that jibe ready for about nine years and had never found a use for it, and it was the only joke she had. "If you have brought me a claim I will read it, and if you have come to ask me about the four coppers I will tell you the same thing I tell everybody, which is that it is four and it is what it has always been and it is not a conversation."

"I have come to ask you a question and it is not about the levy."

"Then ask it."

Nessa Pell had four sentences ready. She used none of them. She said, "**Mrs. Ostler, does Wren Ostler have a name that she is willing to have written in a public book?**"

The counter went quiet in the way a counter goes quiet, which is to say it did not go quiet at all, because there were nine people in there and a man at the far end reading a docket to himself.

"**What is it for?**"

"It is for a job. Seven days at a time, four days now, rotating. You would hold the key to a case in a locked office and nobody would be able to look in it without you, and anybody who turned up on a published day could be taken in to see the thing in it and you would describe it to them in words they did not bring with you. It is not dangerous. It is dull, and it is watched, and your name would be in a book on a board in a yard and anybody in this district could come and read it."

"**And if I say no?**

"Then I write down that I asked you and you said no, and the board sits on Wednesday and sets the term and nobody holds it, and the case stays shut, and the object stays where it is on a term that has run out, and that is not a theft and it is a lapse, and it is published. That is all. There is no second part."

Wren Ostler was about fifty-four and had been at that counter six years and had read out perhaps thirty thousand sentences in front of people who did not want to hear them. She put the slate down, and looked at the woman in the doorway for about four seconds, and then she answered the question she had not been asked, which is what she had been doing all her life, and which is why the counter exists.

"**I have been three things at that counter.**" She said it to the slate. "I have been Mrs. Ostler, which is my husband's name and my name, and I have been the reader, which I am not, because the reader is that woman with the shoulder on four days a week and eleven coppers a quarter, and I have been the second clerk, which is a job and not a name. **Not one of them was picked by me. Somebody decided and then it stuck.**

"And I will give you a name, and I want three things said about it and one thing said about what it is not.

"**One. It is Wren Ostler, and it is spelled with an e on the end, and it is the same as the name I have said out loud at that counter every day for six years and it is the first name I have had that I did not get from somebody.**

"**Two. You are to write down what it costs, and I will tell you what it costs and you are to write it in my words and not in yours.** A name in a book is one more way of finding me. My counter is public and I have always been findable at a counter, and a name is a second way, and the second way is the one that travels.

"**Three. I am not promising to turn up. I have been late. I was late on the ninth of last month and I read the toll three times and did not tell anybody, because I was ashamed and because nobody had told me to be there and there was no book that said I had to be.** So you are to write in that book that a name in it is not a promise to turn up, and if I am late it is entered that I was late, and not that I failed."

"And the fourth thing is what it is not." She put the slate down. "**It is not the road. It is not the last fare, it is not the bus, it is not any of it, and if in a year somebody comes to that counter and says the Ostlers hold the road then you are to take this page out and read them the four lines.**"

Nessa Pell wrote it in four lines. She read two of them out afterwards in the yard and did not read the third because the third was about being late and it was hers to give and not hers to publish, and Bett Ruhl read it to her later in a kitchen and said it was the whole of the argument and that she was going to chalk it up, and asked whether anybody would stop her, and Nessa said no and meant it.

---

**The board sat at about eleven in the morning on the second of the month with all four seats named aloud, and Wren Ostler was at the rail with a slate and no coat, because it had come on warm and she had come straight from the counter.**

Mara Quill read the four lines into the book and then read the fifth line, which was Nessa's, and which is the standing instruction written out again with one sentence added:

> **THE PERSON HOLDING THE KEY ON THE DAY THE TERM EXPIRES IS NAMED IN THIS BOOK BEFORE THE KEY IS HELD, OR IT IS NOT HELD. AND THE BOARD ASKS BEFORE IT NAMES, AND THE ASKING IS ENTERED WITH ITS TIME, AND A NAME IN A BOOK IS NOT A PROMISE TO TURN UP.**

Then the term. The board could set a short public custody term and it had set seven days four times and this time it set four, and Marda Osk asked why four, and Nessa Pell said it out loud and it is in the minute:

"Because we kept it once. That is the whole of the reason. **A rule that has been kept once is a rule, and a rule that has never been kept is a habit, and I have found out which of those two things I was running for five weeks, and it was a habit.** Four days is not a great deal less than seven. It is a great deal more often."

And the key changed hands at about ten past eleven on the second, in the open, on a table, in front of about forty people, and **Wren Ostler's name went into the book at ten past eleven and the key was handed to her at ten past twelve, and the minute says the two hours and the reason, which is that a name written in a room is not a name anybody has agreed to.**

**The term expires at midnight on the sixth of this month, and the hour of the renewal is published, and the renewal is to be on the fifth and not on the sixth, and the person holding it on the seventh is to be named on the fifth, and the board has asked nobody yet and has entered that it has six days to do it and that is more time than it wanted and less than it will use.**

---

**And the man who has said he will say something in a public yard on every published day was there at a quarter past twelve, because that is what he said he would do, and about twenty people had told him so far and he had done it nineteen times since the twenty-sixth of last month.**

"**That is not enough**," said Selik Marne, to a yard of about forty, in a hat, with a lamp he did not light.

"Say the rest of it," said Sabin Tull, from his seat, for the fourth time in a month.

"The rest of it is that a term checked by one board is a board and not a term, and the board is four people and one of them is the registrar and not a vote, and two of them turn over, and the one who holds the key cannot be asked to do anything by anybody, and a thing that cannot be asked to do anything is not a limit, it is a position. **I have been nine years writing down a limit that is a position and calling it a limit, because a position is cheaper to defend.**"

"Then it is a fair objection," said Nessa Pell, "and answer it in the room rather than in the minutes, because a minute is where objections go to be right with nobody present."

"**Then here is the answer and it is not mine, it is the book.** The object is in a case in a room with a door on it, and the key opens a door, and the door is not the dangerous part. The dangerous part is the nine days in five weeks that it stood open on the table while a hundred people looked at a crack in a piece of brass and nobody wrote down how long for. **You are not protecting a key. You are protecting the right of anybody to look at a thing without a schedule.** And that is worth a great deal more than my argument, and I have been losing it for nine years and Tell me who taught you to say it."

"Hester Vane taught it to me," said Nessa Pell, "on the twenty-sixth, in about thirty seconds, and she did it by talking about a count, and I have been a clerk for nineteen years and I did not have that sentence before she said it."

Hester Vane was not in the office. She had gone home at ten with the key under her coat, which was the arrangement for six days and would be the arrangement for four after the seventh, and there is an entry about that too, and the entry is that the holder's own address is not in this book and is not going to be, and that the holder is met in a public place, and that on the second of this month about eleven people watched a woman walk out of a gate and nobody followed her and the tenth hand of the signal crew, who was there for something else, wrote in the margin of his page: *the book says where the key is. It does not say where the woman is. That is the correct order and I want it kept.*

---

**And at about half past four, a boy of about fifteen who has said the word in a public yard twice and had a count go down by two because of it was sitting on the edge of the crate with a slate on his knees, and he had not said anything for three days, and he said one thing now, and it went in the book.**

"Mr. Pell. If her name is a promise to turn up and it is not a promise to turn up, then what is a name in a book at all."

Nessa Pell thought about it for longer than the room expected.

"**It is a handle.**" She said it out loud twice before she wrote it, because she was going to write it. "A name in a book is not a promise and it is not a person. It is a handle somebody can be found by, and a term is a period of time, and the two together mean that on a stated day there is a place a person is expected to be, and that if they are not there it can be said out loud whether they were late or whether they stopped coming. **Every other thing in this book is there so that a person can be found. This is the first thing in it that is there so that a person can be found on a day.**"

She entered it exactly like that, and Sabin Tull asked whether "handle" was a word anybody outside this office used, and she said no, and he said then it is a new word and put it under the three lines in charcoal on Bett Ruhl's wall that everybody in the district was going to have to learn by heart, and **the four lines under the fifth one in chalk are still up there, and the new one is under them, and nobody has ever rubbed out any of them.**

---

**And at about half past four in the afternoon of the second, a copy clerk of seventeen came and asked the registrar a question that has been sitting in this district since the flood year and had never once been asked.**

Orren Pike had been in the office for about nine minutes with a request in his hand, which was not a request for anything and was a piece of paper with forty-one movements on it in a column, and the column was in a hand that was not his and not anybody's in Lot 17.

"Forty-one trains," he said, "off a page, out of a book, in a cabin at the east end of the Needle."

"That is not a request I can answer," Nessa said. "What is it?"

"It is a question." He put the paper on the table. "**I have been indexing the marks for five weeks and every one of them is a thing somebody did in a room. Every one has a person in it, or a refusal in it, and a mark is a refusal and a refusal is permanent, and I can point at all of them.** And I have been asked by three people this month to find out whether the line ever had a mark on it, and I went and looked, because I am allowed to look at things, and there is not one mark on the line. **Forty-one movements and not one mark.**

"And it is not a column of numbers, Mrs. Pell. It is a column of *places*. Nine miles of line and forty-one movements and there is a column on that page that has got a name in it, and about a fifth of the names in it are places, and the other four are not places, and I do not know what they are, and the man in the cabin says he has never had a use for that column either, and he has been the keeper of it for eleven years."

Nessa Pell read the column. She was not a reader and she had never wanted to be one and she was extremely good at what she was.

"**Sit down, Mr. Pike.**"

He sat down.

"What is the question."

"The question," said Orren Pike, "is that this book has never had a place in it. Not a person, not a lot, not a man at a chain. **A place.** A road is a place. A yard is a place. And you have spent five weeks building the best book anybody in this basin has and every single thing in it is a person or an object, and **I think that is why it is one book, and I think there is a second one.**"

And Nessa Pell, who had been a clerk for nineteen years and had signed a custody page with the hand that was in the spill and had entered her own fear in her own handwriting, sat in a locked office on a warm afternoon in the first week of a new month and looked at a piece of paper with forty-one movements on it and a column of names in it, three of which were not places, and said:

"**Enter that as asked, on the second, and not answered, and put the paper in the book in a sleeve with the number on the sleeve and the number on the sheet, because a sheet that goes into a book loose is a sheet that comes out loose.**"

She wrote the number. It was 411, which was the number of the page and had nothing to do with the trains, and Orren Pike looked at it and did not correct her, and she noticed him not correcting her, and neither of them said anything about it for eleven days.
