# Chapter 96: Thirty Feet of Dark

At about two in the morning on the twenty-sixth of this month a sign-writer of sixty-one was on his knees at the west end of a platform with a rag and a can of white paint, painting four words on a board at the height of a man's hand, and the four words were **THE NINTH PLACE**, and he was doing it by the light of a lamp that a lamp-man had brought up the bank and would not light.

He is Abel Troth. He wrote the heading on the first toll board in this district on the sixth of last month in letters three inches high and it took nine minutes, and he has written the phrase that is at the top of three boards and five hundred printed sheets twice in his own hand in thirty-one years of lettering jobs and has no office and no customer and nobody who gave him the words, and on the night of the twenty-sixth of this month he painted a place's name on a board at the height of a hand so that a person who cannot see a heading can find it in the dark with their fingers.

"You have put it at the wrong height," said the man of about fifty who keeps that board.

"I have put it at the right height for the hand and the wrong height for the eye, and the difference between those two things is this board," said Abel Troth, "**and there is no money in this district for letters, and the answer to a board with no letters on it is not to paint it at the top like the other three. It is to put it where a person who is frightened in the dark can find it without having been able to see in the first place.**"

---

The lamp-man would not light the lamp and gave his reason in nine words, and the reason has been in this district's books since April and is the only calendar anybody in it has.

"A lamp-man does not start a lamp on a Saturday."

Nobody in that hut could say what day of the week the twenty-sixth of this month was. **The finding of the twenty-sixth is that this district has a man who will not light a lamp on a day of the week and cannot tell anybody which day of the week it is, and he is right not to, and the answer took thirteen minutes and is four words long and it is on the board.**

**THE DAY IS THE PERSON.**

"And that is not a joke and I would like it entered that it is not a joke," said the lamp-man. "**A lamp that needs a day of the week is not a lamp. It is a thing a man has to be right about.**"

So the lamp is lit by whoever is standing on the platform, and it is oil, and **the oil is the fourth of the eleven coppers a quarter that the ward market pays for the bench, the lamp, the chalk and the oil**, and it has been paid since the seventeenth of last month, and nobody had ever gone and lit it.

The oil itself is a small thing that took nine minutes and is worth putting down because it is the shape of the whole of this month. A card at a chandler's counter on Ash Row named a road house until the third of this month, and then it named a man, and the man is the one who has put his name on it after saying no for nine minutes, and a trader of fifty-one on the lower terrace has been paying out of her own till since the first of last month and has asked twice that it be said that nobody asked her either. **Nine gallons of lamp oil went up that bank on the twenty-sixth of this month against a card with a person's name on it, and it is the fourth of the eleven coppers, and it is the only line in this district's accounts that has been paid correctly in the whole of the last two months.**

---

And then the twenty-seventh of this month was spent on the ground, for the first time, and it is the fourth rehearsal in this district's history and the first one that was not on ground that could not do what a person said it would do.

It took about four hours and eleven men and a coil of rope and a barrow, because there are no nine vehicles on any piece of ground in Alder Reach and the three rehearsals before this one used ropes. The first was on the twenty-second of March and it was in the rain. The second was on the twenty-first of last month and it was up this bank. The third was in a yard on the tide, on cobbles, where a foreman of twenty-two years said in his own words that a yard cannot tell you anything at all about what nine vehicles do on a loop.

The rehearsal was done with eleven men on a rope, walking it out of a barrow in a straight line, standing in for a train, and the point of it was not the train. The point of it was the eleven yards that a string of people cannot walk in a straight line on a shoulder with a drop, and **the finding is that the loop at this place is eleven yards shorter than the standing train, and that the ninth vehicle therefore stands across the mouth of the loop and not in it.**

That is not a fault and it was not a surprise, and the man of sixty-eight who keeps a pumping engine eleven miles west found it in about half an hour with a piece of chalk and a tape he borrowed off a signalman, and said one sentence about it and the sentence is in the minute.

"**She fits. She has fitted for nine years and nobody has ever stood at the mouth and watched the ninth one come round.** And the reason nobody has ever stood at the mouth is that the mouth is a place a train is in, and this district has never had a rule about trains. **The rule it has is about people, and a train is not one. I would like somebody to write down that a district with an excellent rule about people and no rule about trains has just spent four hours with a rope.**"

And the fourth rehearsal found the thing that the third one could not. **The mouth of that loop is nine feet wide and the ninth vehicle stands across the shoulder, and the shoulder is the ground the boundary runs across sixty yards west, and a line of people and a line of iron cross on the same bank about nine yards apart and neither of them can see the other.**

Nobody had put the two on the same piece of ground before the twenty-seventh of this month. The boundary was pegged on the twenty-ninth of March and the loop has been there since before the flood year, and **the two of them have been eleven feet apart vertically and four hours apart in walking for nine years**, and a line of stones that is not a mark has been standing on the same bank the whole time and nobody laid it this year and nobody is going to.

The woman of the column said the only sentence anybody said about it, and she said it to a rope and not to a person.

"**I have been shut at a door by a river for eleven years and the two of you are about to shut thirty feet of platform between a person and a train, and the difference is that the river did not have a schedule.**"

---

And a firm that gave up a nine-year lease on this ground on the thirtieth of March arrived on the bank at about eleven in the morning on the twenty-seventh with seven vehicles a week and nowhere to stand, and it is the largest practical question anybody has brought up that bank in nine weeks and it has not been in a book.

He is Tarin Kest, forty-three, who founded the Needle Road House, signed the lease in the ninth year in about thirteen minutes and surrendered it four months before it ran out on a sheet headed surrender, assigned to nobody. He posted four hundred and ten coppers of standing salvage in May and watched it become a platform in June. **He came up the bank on the twenty-seventh of this month to ask whether the ground this place holds by nobody could take seven vehicles a week behind the platform, and the answer took four minutes and it was given by the woman at the column from her own doorway.**

"You are asking me to put seven carts on the ground my water stands on," she said. "**I have been shut by a river for eleven years and I know what it is to have a thing come and stand on you, and the answer is no, and I would like it entered that you asked me in four minutes and I answered in four seconds and that the two are not the same length and that mine was faster.**"

He did not argue with her and he said in a yard afterwards that he had expected to argue, and **a man who is refused in four seconds learns something in four seconds that a man who is refused in two hours does not.**

**The finding is that a Road House with seven vehicles a week and a stabling yard given up in the fourth month of the year has a foreman with nowhere to send them, and that the only offer anybody in this district has made him is a yard on the tide lent once a month for a rehearsal, and that a woman of forty-four insisted in May that it be entered as not a security.**

"So I have a yard once a month for a rehearsal and no yard for a cart," said the foreman, who is of twenty-two years and whose name is on the four hundred and ten coppers because he entered it at his own request in April. "**And the platform I built is a place a train stops at and not a place a cart stands at. The man who posted the salvage to make that platform knew it on the evening of the twenty-first, and I worked it out at about eleven at night on the twenty-seventh, and I would like both of those entered and I would like it entered that the second one is nine days after the first.**"

Nobody argued with him and the entry was made in his own words, and **the ninth hand is the foreman's word and not the mechanic's, and the two of them have not agreed about it and the minute says so.**

---

The half of Selik Marne's third thing that nobody did anything about was the half that is about the dark.

It is that the platform is thirty feet long and four feet of face and two feet above a shoulder with a drop of nine feet behind it, and that on the twenty-seventh of this month there is chalk on the platform at the height of a hand saying THE NINTH PLACE, and a lamp on a post that anybody can light, and a rail along the front edge, and nothing at all along the back.

The back edge is a drop, and there is nothing on that side of the platform at all, and that sentence is the whole of what the twenty-seventh of this month settled.

Nobody argued about it, and the minute enters that nobody argued about it, and that the reason nobody argued about it is that a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing had been in four rooms in nine weeks about this bank and had said in each of them that he could not get a person off that platform at night, and had been thanked once, and had asked for the thanking to be entered, and it was.

The rail along the back edge was cut on the twenty-seventh out of the ends of the kerb rail, and it took two men about three hours, and it cost nothing, and **the finding is that a rail is not a security and is not a mark and is not a job.**

**A rail is the only instrument in this district's history that keeps a person on a thing without carrying anybody's name, and nobody has ever written that down.** A woman of about thirty-eight with a hook and a sacking sheet wrote it down on the twenty-seventh in nine words on the back of a sheet she was going to throw away, and kept the sheet.

Then a man of sixty-one painted the four words on the board at the height of a hand, and a man of about fifty lit a lamp with his own hands, and a rail went along the back edge of thirty feet of platform at two feet above a nine-foot drop, and none of the three is a person and none of the three can be sent to anything.

A woman of forty-four who keeps a Road House on the tide, who lent her yard for the third rehearsal in this district's history, read the entry on the twenty-eighth and asked for one word to be added to it.

"**Say that a rail is free, and say it plainly, because in about four years somebody will write that a firm paid for the thing a person holds on a platform, and the firm that did it gave up a lease in March.**"

Nobody argued with her and the word went in and it is in her hand.

---

**And on the evening of the twenty-seventh the fourth bearer came to the ninth place for the first time in nineteen years, and he came at his own cost, and the cost is two days a month in somebody else minding a pumping engine, and the entry says he did not have to and came.**

He is Elias Rooke and he is sixty-one and he has held a pair on this line for thirty-four years and has kept the frame at the twelfth place, two miles west, since the fourth year, and he has never once been in this hut.

He carried his own book. It is a pocket book with a rubber band on it and the band has perished and the book has to be held shut with a thumb, and in it, on about nine hundred lines, four words appear and nothing else: **FRAME TURNED. NOTHING SENT.**

He put it on the table in the hut, opened it at a page in the middle, and nobody in that room had asked him to, and the room read about two hundred lines in four minutes and found four words and a date on every one of them.

"You are the woman who copied that line out nine hundred times," he said to the keeper of the only pair.

"I am."

"And you never asked what the frame turned."

"**I never asked what the frame turned and I never once asked who turned it, and I have said that in a room in Alder Reach and I am saying it in my own hut, and the reason is that a person with a lever and a book is a person with a document, and there was nobody in nine miles who would have asked me about a document and not made me into something.**"

The minute carries that, in her words, for the second time, and enters that a man had heard it in a room in Alder Reach and had come two miles to hear it in a hut as well, and that the entry about it is in the woman's hand and not the man's.

He was asked, in the five things, on a wall, in about nine minutes, and he gave the first thing in his own words, and it was the same nine words as on the sixteenth of this month, and he said he would not say a different thing twice.

Then he was asked the second thing, which is what a person ought to know before they agree, and he gave one sentence about the loop and the water and the hours, and it is eleven words long and it is in the book. **THE RIVER COMES UP OVER THE DRAINAGE CUT AND I CANNOT MAKE IT GO DOWN.**

"That is the whole of what I know about the ground," he said. "**Nineteen years and a lever twice a month and I have never once been able to say what the frame is for, and I know one thing about the water, and it is in the book, and it is the only sentence in nine hundred lines that is not four words.**"

And then a man of about thirty-one who is a signalman of the district's own crew and holds the second of the three weeks asked him a question nobody had thought of asking, which was who the two other people were going to be.

"I do not know them," said the fourth bearer.

"You will be told on the night, or you will not be told at all, and both of those are worse for you than being told in advance," said the signalman, "**and I am not going to argue for it and I am going to lose, and I would like it entered now that I argued for it and lost.**"

Nobody argued. The two people in the room with him are to be named to him at the moment, and the finding is the one the tenth hand said out loud in a counting room in Alder Reach on the evening of the twenty-seventh and that is nine words long.

**TWO PEOPLE WHO KNOW IN ADVANCE WILL AGREE WITH YOU.**

"He said that was correct," said the man of thirty-one, "and it is, and I would like it entered that I argued for the wrong thing on purpose because I had not thought of the right one, and the right one is in the book and it is not mine."

---

**And the twenty-eighth of this month is where the night got named, and it was not named by the man who was going to do it, and the finding is that a reader who says he will read only in an emergency has published the exact minute of the emergency and cannot take it back.**

The clerk of the ward market, who keeps the line book at the east end in the hours the counter is shut and who is not the market, asked him in four seconds on the twenty-seventh, in a counting room, with about thirty people in it, whether he intended to read the iron.

"I do not know," he said.

"**Then say it in that order in the book, because a man who does not know is a man anybody can ask, and a man who says he will not is a man who has already answered, and the second one of those is a thing this district has been trying to get out of people for nine weeks and you have just given it away for nothing.**"

He asked for that to be entered and it was entered in a clerk's hand and not in his own.

On the twenty-eighth, in about four minutes, in the same counting room, he gave the other answer, and it is the longest thing he has said in nine weeks and it is entered in his own words.

"I will read it once, and only the iron, and only if the movement cannot be made without it, and by movement I mean the throwing of the points of that loop. **And I want the hour written down and I want the minute written down, and I want them on a page anybody can read.** And the reason is that a man who says he will only use an instrument in an emergency has told this district where the emergency is, and there is no way to say that and then not have it be true. **I would rather everybody knew the exact moment this line pays for a reading than have it be a surprise at three in the morning with nine vehicles on a bank.**"

**The hour is the eleventh of this month at night. The minute is the minute the points of the loop are thrown.** The page went into the public lot book on the evening of the twenty-eighth and the reader at the ward market counter read it out on the morning of the twenty-ninth, and about fifty people stood at that counter, and a woman of about sixty with a stick asked one question about it, and the question was whether a page that says when is the same as a page that says why.

"It is not," said the tenth hand, "**and this one says when, and the why is on a different page, and the why is that a bracket is the only part of this that is inside a locked frame, and nobody in this district knows what the iron says until somebody stands in front of it and reads it, and the reading may be wrong, and nine vehicles will already be on the loop.**"

Nobody wrote anything under that heading, and **the minute says nobody wrote anything under it, and that is the first empty space anybody has left in this district's books on purpose.**

---

And the party went up the bank on the afternoon of the twenty-eighth and was on that shoulder at about seven in the evening with about twenty people who had not been asked to be there and had come anyway, and the last thing that happened on this bank before the twenty-ninth was a woman of about thirty-eight with a hook and a sacking sheet walking the length of the platform in the dark with a lamp she lit herself, twice, and finding it both times, and saying so out loud on the shoulder to about thirty people in the rain.

"That is not a rehearsal and I am not going to call it one, and the reason is that the fourth one was on the ground yesterday and this is one woman with a lamp and a hook," she said, "**and the only thing it found out is that a person can find thirty feet of board at two in the morning, and I would like that written down before somebody comes up here in September and puts four coppers in that column and tells me a platform is a thing you get off a train at.**"

The boy who keeps the index is seventeen, is paid four coppers a quarter, is not Adrian Vale's apprentice and has said so in a yard in front of more than one person. He wrote the figure on the twenty-eighth in a hand that is not anybody else's, **and the train on that siding had stood a hundred and thirty-seven days.**
