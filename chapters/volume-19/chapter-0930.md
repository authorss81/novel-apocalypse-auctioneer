# Chapter 0930: The TWO HUNDRED AND FOURTEENTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Last Morning Of This Stretch And A Woman Of About Fifty-Two Said A Reason Out Loud About A Thing That Is Not The Page And She Was Not Thanked And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud And Wrote One Line Carrying The Morning And The Page At The Back Carries Forty-Four Lines And A Yard That Has Learned To Say A Thing Out Loud Has Not Said The One Thing Anybody Came Down That Bank For And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and sixty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and twenty-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and forty-four days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and fifty-eight days. Eight hundred and nineteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and thirty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and thirty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eleven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and forty-seven days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and eighty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and seventy-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and one reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back of that lot book took one line for it this morning and the line carries the morning and not the reason, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before the two hundred and eighth morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and forty-four mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and forty-three. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and ninety-four mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and fourteen, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and thirteenth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries forty-four lines and it did not carry a forty-fifth this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and fifty-fifth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixty-ninth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and sixty-eight of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and fifty-ninth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the seventieth morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and twenty-nine days, the page at the back took one line this morning and the morning was read out loud before it was written, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said a figure out loud before the clerk's hand had moved, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

The boards went up after the light found the eleven feet and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank at the hour they come down. At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on the stone afterwards and left it there a while. At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said a figure out loud, and he said it before the clerk's hand had moved.

"Two hundred and sixty-one," he said, at the near end of the first table.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet said that after him out of step with each other, and about four of them have said since that this is the last morning of ten and that about four of them said nobody in that yard has been told there was a ten.

The turner had his wheel going with a piece of ash in it. The man who digs loam was at the north end on the same strip he has been on all week. The man of about thirty-two put his table up about four feet along from the second table and squared it with the back of his heel. The man of about thirty-four stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table and said nothing out loud at all this morning.

At about half past ten the woman of about fifty-two came down that bank for the sixth time and stood at about four feet off the near end of the first table and looked at the form under the clean page, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that she has asked one thing out loud in that yard in six times down that bank.

And then she said a reason out loud, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the first table, in about nineteen people's hearing, and she said it once, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a thing said out loud in that yard is going on the page at the back of that book this afternoon whatever it is about, and that nobody in that yard had said that before she said it.

"My mother is buried not far up that bank," she said. "And I get as far as the top of it and no further, and I have come down here six times and I have not got any further than the top of it one time."

And then she went back up that bank, and nobody in that yard said one word back to her, and nobody thanked her, and nobody asked her one second thing about her mother or about the bank or about the six times, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman came down a bank six times to say something that has nothing to do with a board or a book or a figure, and that a page at the back of a lot book is going to carry this morning and is not going to carry one word of it.

The man of about twenty-nine who drives the cart came through the gap in the bank at about half past one with a load of stone on it and went through that yard and out the far side of it without stopping, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a cart which stopped in a yard four mornings ago and said the only thing it had went through this morning without stopping, and that about four of them have not said whether that is because there was nothing left to say or because there was.

The woman who keeps a scale weighed a bar of iron out of a basket and wrote the figure on the edge of a board of her own with a bit of chalk, and she said nothing to anybody. And about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along, which is what he does. The man of thirty-eight was at the top of that bank and came down the slope and went along the side of that yard and out of it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixty-ninth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and sixty-eight of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and fifty-ninth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and then said one word on his own that nobody had said anything to him about, and he said nothing else, and nobody in that yard asked him one question this morning, and nothing that was said in that yard this afternoon carries what the word was.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and third time running, and nobody asked him anything. And then the clerk read the morning out loud, and she read it before she wrote anything, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, to about nineteen people at the foot of that bank, and she read it once.

"The two hundred and fourteenth morning," she said. "One reason was said out loud in this yard this morning and it is being written on the page at the back of that book, and the reason is not going on it, and that is the whole of it."

And she wrote one line on the page at the back of that lot book, in her own hand, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a clerk write one line for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and that page carries forty-four lines and not one of the forty-four is in the lot book and not one of them says what a woman said about her mother.

Nobody thanked the woman who said the reason and nobody thanked the clerk who wrote the line and nobody thanked the man who was asked nothing this morning. At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up, and the boards went down the way they go down, and the second table stood at the foot of that bank where it stands and the near rail was on the near side of it.
