# Chapter 0928: The TWO HUNDRED AND TWELFTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month Two Reasons Were Said Out Loud In One Morning Inside A Quarter Of An Hour And The Man Who Digs Loam And The Man Of About Thirty-Four Were Not Standing Together When They Said Them And They Did Not Know About Each Other And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud Once And Wrote Two Lines And Both Lines Carry The Morning And Neither Of Them Carries A Reason And The Page At The Back Carries Forty-Three Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and sixty-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and twenty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and forty-two days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and fifty-six days. Eight hundred and seventeen days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and thirty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and thirty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and forty-five days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and eighty-five marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and seventy-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and two reasons were said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back of that lot book took two lines for it this morning and the lines carry the morning and not the reason, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before the two hundred and eighth morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and forty-two mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and forty-one. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and ninety-two mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and twelve, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eleventh morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries forty-three lines and it did not carry a forty-fourth this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and fifty-third of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixty-seventh night of that run, having slept on five hundred and sixty-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and fifty-seventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the sixty-eighth morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and twenty-seven days, the page at the back took two lines this morning and the morning was read out loud before it was written, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said no figure, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

The boards went up after the light found the eleven feet and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank at the hour they come down. At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on the stone afterwards and left it there a while.

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said no figure, and said so out loud, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

---

The morning before this one a man said a thing behind the shafts of his own cart and went out of the yard and did not come back up the lane. And this morning about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a yard which has had a name in it and a reason off a digger and a reason off a cartman has begun to expect the next one, and that about four of them have said expecting one is not the same as getting one and that about four of them have not said which of the two a yard can help.

---

At about eleven the man of about thirty-four said a reason out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table where he stands, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people heard it, and he said it without stopping, and he kept his eyes on the boards while he said it.

"There is one window in this district I can see from that spot," he said. "It is my own and I look at it about nine times a morning and nobody in this yard has ever once asked me why I stand here."

---

And nobody in that yard said one word back to him and nobody asked him where the window is, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who has been standing at about four feet off the near end of the second table for a long while has said out loud that he stands there for a window and that about four of them have not said whether they are glad to know it.

---

The turner had his wheel going and did not stop it. The woman who keeps a scale weighed a bar of iron and said nothing. The man of about thirty-two put his table up and did not look at anybody.

---

And then at about a quarter past eleven the man who digs loam stood up out of the strip at the north end and said a reason out loud from where he was standing, in the ordinary voice, loud enough to go about four hundred yards of air, and about nineteen people heard it, and he did not walk into the middle of that yard to say it and he did not know that anybody had said anything at all.

"I was put out of a trade at seventeen that wanted my hands looked at all day long," he said. "And this is the only work I have ever had where nobody in this yard has ever once seen my hands."

---

And then he went back down into the strip and went on digging it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that two reasons were said out loud in that yard this morning inside about a quarter of an hour and that the two men were about four hundred yards apart when they said them and that neither of them knew the other had said one.

---

And about four of them said a man who has watched reasons come out of that yard for two mornings has had one himself and said it looking at the boards and not at anybody, and that a man who digs loam has said his from the far end of the yard without coming any nearer, and that about four of them have not said whether two men saying a thing a quarter of an hour apart is one thing happening or two.

---

At about half past eleven the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said one thing out loud, in the ordinary voice, before he said his number, and about nineteen people heard it.

"Then a morning is not a morning," he said.

And nobody asked him what he meant by it and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who keeps a tally of mornings has said out loud that a morning has come apart, and that nobody in that yard asked him to say it again.

The clerk stood with the front of that book open and said nothing out loud to anybody through the whole of the middle of the day, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came and looked at the front of it and went away again. The man of thirty-eight was at the top of that bank with his hands showing and he came down the slope and went along the side of that yard and out of it, and nobody said one word to him.

---

And about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along, which is what he does.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixty-seventh night of that run, having slept on five hundred and sixty-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and fifty-seventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near the man at the foot of that wall.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and first time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

And then the clerk read the morning out loud, once, and she read it before she wrote anything, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, to about nineteen people at the foot of that bank.

"The two hundred and twelfth morning," she said. "Two reasons were said out loud in this yard this morning and both of them are being written on the page at the back of that book, and neither reason is going on it, and that is the whole of it."

---

And she wrote two lines on the page at the back of that lot book, in her own hand, one under the other, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a clerk write two lines for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and that page carries forty-three lines and not one of the forty-three is in the lot book and both of the two of this morning carry the morning and nothing else.

---

Nobody thanked either of the two men and nobody thanked the clerk, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a page that has taken a line for a morning has taken two for a morning before this one, and that nobody in that yard knew it could and that about four of them have not said whether it can again. And about four of them have said that two lines went on that page this morning and that neither of them says anything at all.

---

At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up, and the boards went down the way they go down.

---

And about four of them have said that a yard which reads a morning out loud and then writes nothing about what was said in it has done that twice in one morning and once in two mornings, and that about four of them have not said what it is for.

The page at the back of that lot book carries forty-three lines and it did not carry a forty-fourth this morning and not one of the forty-three is in the lot book, and three of the first thirty-three were lines nobody in this district heard read. Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is seven and it did not go back this morning. This district has made fourteen things and the fourteen did not move. Five is what this district does not have and the fifth of the five is unpaid on every morning of this stretch, and a line on the back of that book is not a payment of it. The lane count is thirty and it did not move, and a morning that goes like the others is not a page coming down that lane, and the measure of this stretch does not go up that lane at all. The heading over the one name in that column still says whoever and the name does not satisfy the rule and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the space on the form that came down that lane is a different space on a different piece of paper and it is empty as well, and nobody in that yard has filled either one of them. The record has stood one hundred and twenty-seven days and nobody in that yard asked what it is one hundred and twenty-seven days of. Nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything this morning and two reasons were said out loud in that yard this morning and two lines were written on that page carrying the morning and not the reason and not the name and not the question and not the answer, and a man of thirty-eight has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three and has not been told he is a good man, and this morning went like the others, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a figure which took two in one morning is not a figure which takes one a morning, and that about four of them said nobody in that yard knew this morning that a morning like this one was possible, and that about four of them have not said whether it is possible again.
