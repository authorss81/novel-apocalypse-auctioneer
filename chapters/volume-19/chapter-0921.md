# Chapter 0921: The TWO HUNDRED AND FIFTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Yard Asked The Man Of Fifty-Six A Second Time In Twenty-Two Mornings And The Page At The Back Took A Line And The Line Carries The Morning And Not The Question And Not The Answer And Nobody Thanked The Man Who Was Asked And Nobody Thanked The Man Who Asked And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and sixty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and nineteen. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and thirty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and forty-nine days. Eight hundred and ten days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and twenty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and twenty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and ninety-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and thirty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and seventy-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and sixty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and the page at the back did not move and the clerk wrote the morning on it in her own hand, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before the two hundred and eighth morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and thirty-five mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and thirty-four. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and eighty-five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fourth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries thirty-eight lines and it did not carry a thirty-ninth this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and forty-sixth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and somebody asked him a question out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard that as well.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixtieth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and fifty-nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and fiftieth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the sixty-first morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and twenty days, the page at the back took one line this morning and the morning was read out loud before it was written, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said a figure out loud before the clerk's hand had moved, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

The boards went up after the light found the eleven feet, and then the light came over the top of that bank and across the yard to the low wall, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank at the hour they come down. At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on the stone afterwards and left it there a while.

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said a figure out loud, and he said it before the clerk's hand had moved.

"Two hundred and twenty-eight," he said, at the near end of the first table.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet said that after him out of step with each other, and about four of them have said since that a boy of seventeen said a figure out loud on the first morning of ten, and that nobody in that yard has asked him once what a figure he says out loud is a figure of, and that nobody asked him this morning either.

---

The yard got on with what it does. The form was on the boards under the clean page with the space on it empty, and the lot book was turned round with five lines in it and a column with one name in it and the heading over that column still saying whoever. The man of about thirty-two put his table up where he puts it and stood behind it with his hands on the edge.

---

The turner had his wheel going with a piece of ash in it and the shavings coming off it onto the boards in a heap he keeps kicking back into one place. The man who digs loam was at the north end with his barrow and was shovelling into the same place he shovelled into yesterday. The man of about thirty-two looked at the boards once and then at the ground and did not look at them again.

---

By about half past ten there was a thing going round that yard which had not been going round it before, and it was not a sentence anybody said out loud. It was that a page had taken a line on the one hundred and eighty-third morning and that since that morning not one person in that yard had asked the clerk a single thing about it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that two people will stand about four feet apart and look at each other and then look at the boards when a page has done something.

---

At about eleven the man of about thirty-four stopped where he stands at about four feet off the near end of the second table and asked the man of fifty-six a question out loud, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people heard it.

"Will you be at that wall tomorrow morning," he said.

And he was given the answer he gives at about four every afternoon, in the same words and at the same speed, flat, and about nineteen people heard it, and about four of them said the second part after the first out of step with each other, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet went quiet while he said it.

---

And then, later on, somebody at about four feet off the near end of the second table said out loud whether a line on a page came with a reason attached to it, and nobody took it back and nobody answered it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the sentence went out into that yard and stayed there and got worked on for the rest of the morning.

And the man of fifty-six was given a second thing that morning and he gave the answer he gives at about four, in the same words and at the same speed, and about nineteen people heard that one as well.

And about four of them have said since that a man can be asked one thing and given an answer and then asked another thing and given the same answer, and that about four of them have not said whether the second answer was about the same thing as the first one.

---

The woman who keeps a scale weighed a sack of something at about one and the needle came to rest a long way short of the mark where the lead weights begin, and she said nothing to anybody. And about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and sixtieth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and fifty-nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and fiftieth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near the man at the foot of that wall, and the ground they were standing on has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and neither of them has ever mentioned it.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the ninety-fourth time running, and nobody asked him anything, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard a man say the same four words for the ninety-fourth time and not one of them looked away.

---

And then the clerk stood up off the second table and read the morning out loud, and she read it before she wrote anything, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, to about nineteen people at the foot of that bank.

"The two hundred and fifth morning," she said. "Somebody asked out loud this morning and it is being written on the page at the back of that book, and the question is not going on it and the answer is not going on it, and that is the whole of it and I am not going to say anything else about it before I have done it."

And she wrote one line on the page at the back of that lot book, in her own hand, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a clerk write one line for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and that page carries thirty-eight lines and not one of the thirty-eight is in the lot book.

Nobody thanked the man who was asked and nobody thanked the man who asked, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked to see whether anybody was going to, and that nobody was.

---

The clerk shut that book after she had written and stood with her hand flat on the cover of it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that she has not looked at the page since she wrote on it, and that about four of them have said that a person who has just put a line on a page and then shut the book over it is a person who has finished with whatever it was.

---

At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in this yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up, and the boards went down the way they go down.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a line went on that page at about four this morning for a question that nobody thanked anybody for, and that page has taken a line twice now in forty mornings and that the two of them are twenty-two mornings apart, and that about four of them have not said why they know that number without counting it.

The page at the back of that lot book carries thirty-eight lines and it did not carry a thirty-ninth this morning and not one of the thirty-eight is in the lot book, and three of the first thirty-three were lines nobody in this district heard read. Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is seven and it did not go back this morning. This district has made fourteen things and the fourteen did not move. Five is what this district does not have and the fifth of the five is unpaid on every morning of this stretch, and a line on the back of that book is not a payment of it. A sheet at a gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and by how much that figure is out has never been done by anybody in this basin. The lane count is thirty and it did not move, and a morning that goes like the others is not a page coming down that lane, and the measure of this stretch does not go up that lane at all. The toll is ninety-eight and it is spent and it may not be raised or lowered or paid twice. The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five. The bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot and it will not be. The heading over the one name in that column still says whoever and the name does not satisfy the rule and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the space on the form that came down that lane is a different space on a different piece of paper and it is empty as well, and nobody in that yard has filled either one of them. The record has stood one hundred and twenty days and nobody in that yard asked what it is one hundred and twenty days of. Nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything this morning and the morning was written on that page in a clerk's own hand and not one line was taken for it, and a man of thirty-eight has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three and has not been told he is a good man, and this morning went into that yard the way a morning goes when somebody has asked somebody something in it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a page that has taken a line twice in forty mornings is a page nobody has asked to take anything else.
