# Chapter 595: A Man Who Mends Fencing Asked A Man Who Digs Loam A Question In The Plainest Words Anybody In That Yard Could Find And Was Not Thanked For It

There was no wind in that yard on the second of the fifth month and the boards of that second table were warm by about nine, and about four people in that yard had come up that bank before eight and had gone to the far end of that table and stood there, and about nine of them had come up it after a man who mends fencing, which had not happened on any morning of a month, and by ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen. Three pages were lying along the end of that second table and the copy at the near end of them has now been on that wood for ninety-three mornings, and the boards under the near end of that copy were worn pale in a way they had not been in about a month.

At about a quarter to eight a clerk of nineteen years entered, at the near end of that first table, that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered three hundred and thirty-four days as the age of that figure, and entered that the number of pages on the end of that second table is three and did not move, and entered that the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and entered that a man came up that bank before about four other men, and entered that she is not entering why, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them without looking at the wall, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that this is the three hundred and twentieth of those mornings, and that about four of them have said that a man who has read four numbers off that wall for three hundred and twenty mornings walked past it at about ten past eight this morning without stopping, which he has never not done.

"**The board carries four hundred and ninety-three, the train on that siding has stood eight hundred and nine days, nobody has entered anything for five hundred and twenty-three, and four hundred and eighty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was standing about nine feet further into that yard than he usually stands, with his binder shut under his arm, and had said nothing. The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing came across that yard at about ten with a rail under his arm, set it down against the low side of that second table without a word, went to the far end of it and stopped about four feet off a place where a man usually is, and that man was not in that yard yet, and about nine people saw him do it and did not follow him, and he said one thing out loud, in about twenty-six seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what he said and got sixty-five and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I am going to stand here and I am not going to touch anything, and I have said in this yard three times that I would not be thanked for saying a thing and this morning I am saying a thing and I am saying it before he has got here, and that is on purpose and about four of you can work out why.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and that she is not entering why he said it before another man had come up a bank, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Then the man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up that bank at about ten, and about four people in that yard had been waiting about half an hour for him to come up it and had not said so to anybody, and about four people in that yard turned round when he reached the top of it, and he came to the far end of that second table and stopped, and about nine feet off him a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was standing with his left hand in its cloth and his right hand hanging at his side and not on anything at all, and the boards under his boot had a pale worn patch in them the copy had, and the yard was quiet for about four seconds, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud, in about twenty-one seconds, to a man about nine feet away from him, and the boy counted what he said and got fifty-six and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I am going to say it in the plainest words I have got and I am saying it to you and not to the yard, and I am not going to call you by anything, and I would like it entered that I asked one question in this yard in a month, and here it is.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man asked that it be entered that he asked one question in that yard in a month, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

And then he said the other thing, and about nine people in that yard heard both halves of it.

"**If a man comes up that lane with a page in his hand, and he is not from this parish, and he wants to know whether that page came out of this yard, may he come up that bank and ask you about it. That is the whole of the question and there is nothing else in it and I am not going to dress it up.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered, and the ink was dry on that line before she had finished writing it, that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered the person it was put to, and entered that a question was said out loud in that yard in the open in front of about nineteen people at about ten, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Nobody in that yard said one word for about a minute and a half, and about nine people at the near end of that first table have said since that the longest silence anybody in that yard has stood in since a man read two third lines out loud was about a minute and a half, and that the man who asked it looked at the boards the whole time and that is the third time he has done that.

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam said nothing at all for about nine seconds after the man had finished and then he said one thing out loud, in about fifteen seconds, to a man about nine feet away from him, and the boy counted what he said and got thirty-six and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Ask me that again this afternoon. I am not saying no and I am not going to say it on a morning when nobody has told me what it is that I would be agreeing to.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he was not asked a second question, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

About four people in that yard said one thing out loud at about the same moment, in about three seconds, and the boy counted what was said and got seven and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**He did it. He went and asked.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that about four people said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a clerk heard it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Then the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud, in about thirty-one seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what he said and got eighty-two and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I am not to be thanked. I said in this yard that I should not be and about four of you have just gone against that inside about nine seconds of me asking him, and I would like it entered that I asked one man one question after standing nine feet off him for a month, and I would like it entered that I am not the man who decided anything, and I would like it entered that this morning is his.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man said he is not to be thanked, and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The clerk of nineteen years said one thing out loud at about half past eleven, in about thirty-two seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what she said and got eighty-three and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I am entering a question, and I am entering a man who has said he will answer it this afternoon, and I am not entering an answer and nobody is to write one down, and I am saying out loud that a man in this yard is not a thing this district can be thanked for putting on a page, and about four of you have been waiting a month to find out what happens in that yard and nothing has happened yet.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a clerk said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that she is not entering an answer, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was still at the far end of that second table at about twelve and then he went down that bank, and nobody in that yard said one word to him as he went, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a man went down that bank and entered that nobody in that yard said one word to him, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about half past one the road keeper came up that lane, read the four figures off that wall as he went by, and was not asked about the eleven miles, and about four people at that wall have said since that a man asked a man a question in that yard this morning and the road keeper was not here for it and went on up the lane the way he goes. The cart came up at about three with the near wheel still dragging and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and went on up that bank and set the shafts down at the top of it, and nobody in that yard asked him anything and he said nothing to anybody. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given, and about four people near that wall have said since that about nineteen people in that yard spent a morning on one question and the man on those stones has not been asked one in two hundred and thirty nights. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his two hundred and thirty-fourth night of that run, that he has slept on two hundred and thirty-third of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

Two hundred and forty-three days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. A hundred and ninety-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man, and the fourth line says that he is not named here. Two hundred and three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Two hundred and seventy-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is three hundred and seventy-six days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is two hundred and twelve days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched it.

A hundred and fifty-two marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are a hundred and thirty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and one mark went on the underside of those boards before about ten. The reading of that lot is the fourth of the five, the fourth of them is a person, the fifth of them is a remedy, and it is unpaid, and a question asked of a man did not advance it, and a clerk entered the reason, which is that a fourth thing asked for is not a fifth thing said. Nothing was read out loud in that yard today, one question was said out loud in it, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and it has been named on every one of these mornings and it is empty on every one of them, and about four people in that yard have said since that a man said a thing out loud in that yard this morning and that column is still empty, and the reason is that a man who is asked a question is not a man who reads anything.

Nobody in that yard went up that bank after the buckets, and the buckets went down that bank empty at about half past five and were carried back up it full, and the woman of about fifty-eight read three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and about four people at the foot of that bank have said since that a man who mends fencing asked a man who digs loam a question in that yard this morning and the woman who has read those three lines every evening for about four months was not in that yard when it was asked, and about four of them have said she has not been in that yard when anything has happened in it for about a month. The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall for the last time that day, got all four of them, and about nine people near that wall have said since that a man walked past that wall this morning without stopping and got all four of them right at about four this afternoon as he has every day, and that about four of them have said that a man who has not stopped at that wall since the sixth of the sixth month asked a man a question nine feet from a table this morning, and that about four of them have said the two of those facts have nothing to do with each other and that this district has not worked out what it is supposed to mean.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing asked one man one question in the yard of Lot Seventeen on the second of the fifth month at about ten, in front of about nineteen people, and the man he asked it to said he would answer it that afternoon and went down that bank at about twelve, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a question was said out loud in that yard and entered that no answer was entered, and the number of pages on the end of that second table is three, and a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was at the far end of that table at about ten with his right hand hanging at his side and not on the boards and not on the stone and not on any of the three pages.
