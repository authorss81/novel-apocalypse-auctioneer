# Chapter 586: A Woman Said Out Loud In Front Of Nineteen People That A Man Had Had A Choice About It Every Morning And Had Taken It

The yard was empty of anybody who had not come up about the pages by about one on the twenty-third of the fourth month, and there were about four people at the end of that second table and about nine of them somewhere else in that yard or on the lane, and the light on that bank was low enough by then to come in under the far edge of the boards and lie on the stones. Four pages were lying along the end of that table and the copy at the near end of them has now been on that wood for eighty-four mornings, and by ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

At about a quarter to eight a clerk of nineteen years entered, at the near end of that first table, that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered three hundred and twenty-five days as the age of that figure, and entered that the number of sheets on the end of that second table is four and did not move, and entered that the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and entered that a man came down off the top of that bank yesterday and did not speak and that she is not entering that as anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them without looking at the wall, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that this is the three hundred and eleventh of those mornings, and that about four of them have said that the man at that wall has said those four figures out loud three hundred and eleven times and has never once said why he comes here to do it.

"**The board carries four hundred and eighty-four, the train on that siding has stood eight hundred days, nobody has entered anything for five hundred and fourteen, and four hundred and seventy-five days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

---

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was mending a bundle of them at the low end of that yard and said one thing out loud at about nine, in about twenty-three seconds, and the boy counted what he said and got sixty-one and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have been in this yard about a month and I have watched about four of you come up that bank every morning for the same reason and go home the same way, and I am a man who cuts reeds for a living, and I can tell you that is not work, because work ends when the thing is done.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered, at the near end of that first table in that yard, that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally came up that bank at about half past nine with the flat book shut and held against his chest with both arms the way a man holds something he has not decided about and said one thing out loud, in about thirty-three seconds, and the boy counted what he said and got eighty-five and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have been standing at that wall every morning of that book and I have never opened it in front of about nineteen people, and the reason I have not is that a page you can look at is a page somebody will read out of, and I have four things in that book I could not read out in this yard if you paid me, and I am telling you the number so that nobody stands at that wall this morning waiting for me.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man did not open a book, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The boy of about nineteen said one thing out loud at about ten, in about twenty-five seconds, and the boy counted what he said and got sixty-six and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Three of us have now said out loud in three days that about four of you come up this bank for the same reason, and I have got the number for all three of us, and none of the three of us has said the reason, and I am nineteen and I have counted a lot of things and I have never had one like this.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a boy said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he counted first, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The clerk of nineteen years said one thing out loud at about half past ten, in about twenty-eight seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what she said and got seventy-three and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I am entering all of it and none of it moves, and I have said that out loud on this morning before and I am saying it again, and the reason is that three men saying a thing is not the thing being true and I have got nineteen years of watching about nine people be right about a thing in this yard and wrong about it by the end of the month.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered, and did not sit down while she wrote it, that a clerk said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered the reason, and entered that she is entering the reason and not a judgement, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Then about half past one the yard went quiet at the end of that second table, the way it does when the wind drops and the light comes under the far edge of the boards at once, and the woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale came up the bank from her own shop with her door open behind her and stood at the near end of that first table and looked at about four people and then at the end of the second one, and said one thing out loud, in about fifty-one seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what she said and got one hundred and thirty-three and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have been standing in that doorway since the twelfth of this month and I have had a thing to say about it for eight days and I am going to say it in front of all of you. The man who mends fencing had a choice about it on the twelfth of this month and he took it. He has had it every morning since and he has taken it every morning, and about four of us have worked that out, and I am the one saying it out loud because I am the only one of us who has to stand behind a counter and look at about nine people a day who want somebody to blame. And I know what it is to be looked at when you cannot tell.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a woman said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered the person, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Nobody said anything. The man who mends fencing was at the end of that second table about nine feet from a man who was reading the fourth line of the fourth page with his forefinger along it, an inch off the paper, and he did not look at the woman and he did not look at the man, and about four people at that first table watched him not look at either of them.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud, in about forty-six seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what he said and got one hundred and twenty-one and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**She is not to thank me for that and I want it entered that she should not, and I am not going to stand here and be a man who has been patient. I have had a question in my mouth every morning for a month and I have had a rail to do and a page to hand and a man nine feet away, and about four of you have just worked out in a yard why I have not used any of it, and I am not going to tell you the reason, because the reason is mine and it is not a good one and I would like the yard to go on not asking me for it.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered, and the ink on that page was dry before she had finished, that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man said he should not be thanked, and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

A man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up that bank at about two and read the fourth line of the fourth page with his forefinger an inch off it, and said nothing at all to anybody, and nobody in that yard asked him one word, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a man read a line in that yard and entered that nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about half past one the road keeper came up that lane, read the four figures off that wall as he went by, and was not asked about the eleven miles, and about four people at that wall have said since that a woman said out loud in that yard that a man has had a choice about it every morning, and that the man she was talking about did not look at her while she said it. The cart came up at about three and turned at the bottom of that bank in the way it does, with the near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and went on up that bank and about four people at that first table watched him go up it and said nothing at all. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given, and about four people near that wall have said since that about nine people in that yard were told this afternoon that a man has had a choice about it for a month and has taken it, and that about four of them have said the man on those stones has had a choice about something all month too. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his two hundred and twenty-fifth night of that run, that he has slept on two hundred and twenty-four of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

Two hundred and thirty-four days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. A hundred and eighty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man, and the fourth line says that he is not named here. A hundred and ninety-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Two hundred and sixty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is three hundred and sixty-seven days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is two hundred and three days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched it.

A hundred and forty-three marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are a hundred and twenty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither moved this morning, and two marks went on the underside of those boards before about eleven. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move, and a woman saying a thing out loud about a man is not a fourteenth thing, and a clerk entered that out loud at about half past one with the reason, which is that nothing was made. Two of the four figures on that wall are out and no figure on it was altered this morning, and the figure on the wall stands.

The number of sheets on the end of that second table is four and did not move, and the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and the number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move, and a copy has now been on the end of that second table for eighty-four mornings while two pages lie on a shop counter up that bank with a door open. The reading of that lot is the fourth of the five, the fourth of them is a person, the fifth of them is a remedy, and it is unpaid, and a woman saying out loud that a man had a choice about it did not advance it, and a clerk entered that out loud with the reason, which is that a choice somebody else made is not a remedy for anybody. Nobody read a thing out loud in that yard today. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and it has been named on every one of these mornings and it is empty on every one of them, and she is not going to fill it and has said so out loud before.

The fifth of the five things this district does not have is not paid, the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and nobody in that yard was thanked and nobody was paid, and the count of the five is still five.

Nobody in that yard went up that bank after the buckets, and the buckets went down that bank empty at about half past five and were carried back up it full, and the woman of about fifty-eight read three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past without stopping, and said nothing to anybody at all, and about four people at the foot of that bank have said since that a woman told about nineteen people this afternoon that a man had had a choice about it and had taken it, and that about four of them have said the man himself said he should not be thanked for it, and that those two are the same thing said twice. The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall for the last time that day, got all four of them, and about nine people near that wall have said since that this is the one morning of that month when something was said in that yard about a person and the person was in the yard while it was said, and that about four of them have said that nobody has worked out yet what that changes.

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale said one thing out loud in the yard of Lot Seventeen on the twenty-third of the fourth month about a man who had had a choice about it every morning and had taken it, and the number of sheets on the end of that second table is four, and a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was at the end of that table at about half past one with his right hand flat on the boards and about nine feet off a man reading the fourth line of a page with his forefinger an inch off it.
