# Chapter 1047: The Three Hundred And Thirty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Boards Going Up

Nobody has ever taken that sheet down and measured it against anything, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-six days is the age of that figure and there is no more to be said about it than that.

That board carries nine hundred and forty-five. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-one days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy-five days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty-six days lie between the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open, and it was not run, and it is not open and it is not a lot. Six hundred and fifty days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this ground stands. Six hundred and fifty-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty-four days is how far past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and twenty-eight days back. Six hundred and four marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and ninety marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted and there is no list for it anywhere in this district. Four documents are not owned here. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen have been made in this district, five were never got, and the fifth of those five is still owed.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before the page at the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line was written on that page for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because before that morning it could not hold one, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing has gone out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines all day. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-one mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and eleven mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-one mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirtieth morning and it is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and seventy-second of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-sixth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-five of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and seventy-sixth afternoon running and one word is the whole of that afternoon. And that is the one hundred and eighty-seventh morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer is given to that stone twice a morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twentieth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards and nothing has been entered in it. Ruled across the first of its pages are five lines, and the column beside them carries one name under a heading that still says whoever. Nobody has ever been asked to put anything in the second space on that form, including by naming it. That lane is still at thirty pages and delivered nothing this morning. That record has stood two hundred and forty-six days and nobody has asked what it is for. That ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and about nine people are standing on it.

That is the shape of the place and nobody in this district has ever walked either figure: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that page have passed through this district and nobody in it heard them read.
---

Ten to eight, at those boards, with the light still flat on the top of the bank.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and there is no figure after it this morning."

The clerk opened that book against the rail and shut it again.

"You have said that eight mornings running."

"I have said it every morning for two years and about three weeks."

"And how does a person know there is no figure after it," said the clerk, "when the one that could come has never once come."

The boy stood about four feet off the rail and turned the pencil over in his fingers.

"I do not know," he said. "I just say it out loud and then nobody has to wonder."
---

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six was at that wall with his hand up and got all four off it before the light was on the stone and said his four words to it, and got nothing back, and the boy at the top of the slope heard the sound a man talking to a wall makes at that distance, which is the sound of somebody not being answered, and he has heard it on a good many mornings and has never once gone down and asked about it.

He did the nine spaces between those boards twice before the sun was on them and got nine both times, and then he did them again about eleven and got nine, and he could not make one thing of that, and he had never made one thing of it.

There are nine spaces between the boards at those boards. He has counted them since he came. He could tell you how wide each one is and which one of them is the widest and he could tell you which one the sun comes over first, and he has never once been asked for any of it and he has never once been able to work out who it is for.

---

At about ten he came down the slope with the cap off his pencil and stopped about nine feet off the near end of the first table, which is where he has been standing for a week, and he did not come any closer.

The man of thirty-eight went along his own top and round the end of it and up the slope and back down and in at the corner he always comes in at.

The boy had a question. He had had it since the morning before and it was not a hard one and it was about a figure and it was about nothing at all, and he stood there with the cap off his pencil for about four minutes and put it back on and went up the slope.

Nobody in that yard asked him what he had come down for, and nobody has ever asked him that on any morning of that run, and it is a thing he has long since stopped finding strange, because a person who counts out loud in a yard gets asked nothing and that is the whole of what goes with it.
---

The woman of about fifty-two came down that bank about a quarter to eleven with a frame under her arm and a bag in her hand and stopped about four feet off him where he was sitting on the boards.

"You are counting those."

"They are nine."

"I have watched you count them for a run of mornings and I have never once seen you get a different number."

"Nine," said Orren Pike, "because there are nine."

"That is what I have watched," she said. "You get the same one every time and you keep on at it, and I have stood at the top of that bank thinking about a person who does that and I could not make up my mind whether it was a good habit or a way of not finding out."

"Both, probably," said the boy.

She laughed at that and it went about four feet and stopped.

"You will not tell anybody at the top of that bank that you said that."

"No."

"Good," said the woman of about fifty-two. "I have got four people up there and none of us says the whole of a thing and I would like it kept that the boy at these boards is the only one who says the half of them."
---

The turner was banking his wheel about eleven when the boy came past.

"Boards early today."

"They are not early. It is not half past four."

"They went up early in the day I am speaking of," said the turner, "which is not the same thing, and one of those two is a figure and one of them is an opinion, and I have watched about two hundred mornings of men confuse the two and I have never once been able to make anybody let go of it."

"You do not have to make anybody let go of anything."

"No," said the turner. "That is what makes it a job."
---

The man of about thirty-two was at his own table about two with the cloth over his arm.

"Your table is out of square."

"Since the spring."

"Have you laid anything under it."

"No."

"You have a piece of pallet lying on top of those boards four feet along from you and you have not laid that in either."

"That is not my corner," said the man of about thirty-two.

"It is four feet off your corner."

"It is not my corner," said the man of about thirty-two, and went along his own top, and the boy went up the slope with the pencil in his pocket and did not ask him any more about it.
---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices said it back after him out of step with one another, and it went off the top of that bank and came back down and was gone.

---

At about one the man of about thirty-four came down the slope with his cloth and stopped at the near end of that second table and put his hand flat on his own top at about four feet along from where the boy was standing.

"That corner has been out of square for three winters," he said.

"It has."

"You have looked at it about nine hundred times."

"More than that," said the boy.

"And you have said nothing about it to me, and I want to go on that way, and there is a thing I could say about it that I have not got to the end of and I am not going to get to the end of it this morning either."

"Then why did you stop."

"Because I have had a piece of pallet in my pocket for a run of mornings and I have not laid it in anywhere," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have nobody to hand it to, and you are the only person in that yard who has not asked me one question about it, and I have noticed that about you, and I am telling you that I have noticed it, and that is as far as I go."

The boy did not say anything at all to that, and the man of about thirty-four went along his own boards with the cloth, and about four feet along from the end of that second table there was a foot and a bit of pallet lying flat on top of them where it had been put down, and it was still there at the end of the day.

The boy was on the slope when it went and he heard all of it and he did not write it down, which is what he has always done with a number he cannot put on a page.
---

The turner came back through the gap about half past three with his hand off the frame and found the boy still at the boards.

"You have been at those boards about six hours."

"I have."

"You counted the spaces in them twice and you have said one number out loud and about four people heard it."

"Nine and thirty," said the boy, "and one of them is a count and one of them is a lane, and I have not worked out yet which of the two I would rather was the one somebody wrote down."

The turner went up the slope and did not tell him, because there is nothing in that yard that tells anybody anything, and because the boy had not asked him and there was no question in the air.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it one after another and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"Two hundred and twenty mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and not one of the four of them has ever needed me, and that is the only thing I have ever wanted out of a number."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and twentieth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk read that morning out loud at those boards with the pen in the holder and gave the whole of the account of it before she had the hand near any part of it.

"The three hundred and thirty-first morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and thirty-first morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.
---

The boards went up off the cart face down at about half past four and the light was still on the eleven feet when they did.

About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard.

Orren Pike was at the near end of the boards when it was done and he said the count out loud one more time before he went up the slope, which is the ordinary way that a morning ends in that yard.

"Thirty," he said, "and nothing came down that lane."

Then he went up the bank and counted the nine spaces on the way up, and got nine, and there was nothing behind the count this morning that he could put a second number to, and he had said so out loud at ten to eight before anybody's hand had moved on anything.

The page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines and did not move. The lane count was thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three lines out of the first thirty-three on that back page have gone through this district and nobody in it heard them read. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
