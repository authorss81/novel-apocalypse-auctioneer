# Chapter 1017: The Three Hundred And First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Table Of The Man Of About Thirty-Two Reached Three Hundred Mornings At The Foot Of That Bank And At About Eleven He Heard The Man Of Thirty-Eight Say One Figure Of His Own Out Loud Once And Nobody Else Said One That Morning And Nobody Answered Him

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-six days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and fifteen. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty-one days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-five days. Nine hundred and six days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and is not open. Six hundred and twenty days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this district is. Six hundred and twenty-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and ninety-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-four days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-eight days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-four marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and sixty marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing at all. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen things is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nobody in that yard put a reason into the air this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-one mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty-one mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and one mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the three hundredth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and forty-second of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-sixth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-five of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-seventh morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninetieth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines. The record has stood two hundred and sixteen days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-sixth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

He has kept that count in his head since the morning his own table went up at the foot of that bank and he has never once said it out loud to anybody in this yard.

Nobody has asked him for it. That is a thing he has got used to in the same way a man gets used to a rail he cannot see the bottom of.

---

He came down that slope at about half past eight because his own table goes up before the light gets on it, and he carried it up the last ten feet on his own shoulder because there is nobody in that yard at half past eight to help with a table.

Then he put the cloth on the top of it and went along the boards with the cloth, the whole length and back, the way he has gone along the top of his own table every morning since he put it up.

---

Three hundred.

He had the figure before he was halfway down that slope and he had it because the count is a thing he enters every morning and there is nothing in the entering of it that anybody could call arithmetic. It is a morning, and then it is a day later, and at some point along there the number he has been carrying stops being a number with a bit left over on the end and comes round onto something that has nothing on the end of it.

He got the cloth off the top of his own table and folded it over his arm and stood at the near end of it.

There is nothing whatever in this yard that happens when a count comes round like that. Nothing said, nothing written, nobody looking up. A wheel turns at the near end of that ground and a man of fifty-six is at his wall in the dark and about nineteen people are on their way down that hill.

---

He has thought about saying it. He has thought about saying the number out loud in that yard about as long as he has been keeping it, and every time he has got as far as having it in his mouth he has found that he did not know what the end of the sentence would be.

That is the whole of it. There is a number and there is no sentence, and a number on its own in about four hundred yards of air is a thing four people will say back at you out of step with one another and then it is gone.

There is a second thing he has thought about and has not done. A count of that kind is a figure, and there is a page at the back of that lot book with a clerk's hand on it, and one of these mornings a figure might go down on it the way a reason goes down on it.

He has no idea whether that is so. Nobody in that yard has ever said a word to him about what is on that page or what goes on it, and he is not a man who goes and looks.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down off that bank with his hands showing and worked out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back in at the same corner.

Just after nine three men walked the second table a foot down that slope with the rail riding on the near side of it, and the man who works at that end of it stood with his heel on a wedge until they were past and then took his heel off it. He held the boot on the wedge all the way round and did not say anything, and he does not say anything on any morning.

---

At about eleven a man's voice came out of that yard at about four feet off the near end of the first table.

It said one figure. It said it once, in the ordinary voice, the way a man says a thing he means and is not asking anybody anything about, and about nineteen people's hearing in that yard had it.

The man of about thirty-two had the cloth over his arm and he was standing at the near end of his own table about nine feet off him and he heard every part of it.

---

Nothing came back at it.

Nobody answered him. Nobody thanked him. Nobody asked him one word, and nobody at the top of eleven feet called down about it, and the wheel went on turning through the whole of it, and the man at the far end of the second table had his head over his bench and did not raise it.

He stood where he was and the cloth stayed over his arm.

---

The whole of that yard has about nineteen people in it and there are two people in it who can hear a figure at about four feet, and he is one of them, and the other one was about four feet off the near end of that second table with her hands on a scale.

Neither of them said anything. Neither of them went anywhere. And in about four hundred yards of air between the boards and the top of that bank, one figure that a man had said out loud went along with the wind and was not on anything and was not in anybody's hands by the time it had finished being said.

---

That is the whole of what happened at about eleven o'clock on this morning in that yard. One figure went out of it. There was nothing else in that yard to go out after it or before it, because the man who keeps a tally did not put his number into the air at half past one, and the woman at the scale about four feet off the near end of the second table worked all day without saying one out loud, and no reason was said by anybody at any hour.

The number the man of about thirty-two was carrying in his own head went out of his yard that morning and nobody said it, and it did not go out of the yard, and it is not in that lot book and it is not on the page at the back of it and it is not on a wall and it has not gone down that lane.

---

About half past three the sun came over the top of the low wall and lay down along the stones and a man standing in that strip of it said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones and got one word back.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall with his hand up on the stone and got all four of the figures off it, and then laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"I have not been able to make anything of these four and I have had a long run of mornings to do it in," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and ninetieth time running and nobody asked him anything about any part of it.

---

Just after four the clerk came along the boards and gave the account of that morning out loud before she had the pen near any of it.

"The three hundred and first morning," she said. "There was no reason said out loud in this yard this morning, so no line goes at the back of that book, and the morning goes down in my own hand on its own with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and first morning under the last line and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book and carried fifty-nine lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said that afternoon by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in.

He put the cloth over his shoulder and went up that bank and did not say a number on the way up it or on the way down it or at any point of that day.

The light was still on the top of that bank when he got to it and there were about nine people standing on it not going home yet, and none of them said anything to him about the count or about anything else.

---

The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at the foot of that bank for the three hundredth morning and nobody in that yard asked him what it was the three hundredth of. At about eleven the man of thirty-eight said one figure of his own out loud, once, and nobody else said one that morning, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him and nobody asked him anything. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at fifty-nine lines. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing.
