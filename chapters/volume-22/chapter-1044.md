# Chapter 1044: The Three Hundred And Twenty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And His Hand Flat On The Stone

There is no instrument in this basin that has ever been pointed at that sheet and there is not going to be one, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-three days is the age of that figure.

That board carries nine hundred and forty-two and it is nobody's board. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fifty-eight days and nothing has come off it in that time. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy-two days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open, and it was not run, and it is not a lot. Six hundred and forty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and fifty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty-one days past a printing it did not make is where that body has got to.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and twenty-five days back. Six hundred and one marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and eighty-seven marks in chalk are along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted and there is no list for it. Four documents are not owned here. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. This district has made fourteen things and did not get five of them, and the fifth of those five is still unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before there was a page at the back of that lot book for one to go on, and no line was written there for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing has gone out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, and that page stood at sixty-two lines from the boards going up to the light coming off them. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifty-eight mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and eight mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-eight mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and twenty-seventh morning and is not the second table and is not the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and sixty-ninth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-two of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and seventy-third afternoon running and says one word and goes. That is the one hundred and eighty-fourth morning running with nobody having put a question to him. That flat answer is given to that stone twice a morning and this morning it was the two hundred and seventeenth time running.

That lot book is open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across the first of its pages and the column on the second of them has one name in it under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody is ever asked to put anything in it. That lane count is thirty and not one page came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and forty-three days and nobody has asked what it is for. About nine people are standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and that is not the same figure as anything else in this block.

Neither of those distances has ever been walked or measured by anybody here: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not being raised here. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page have gone through this district without one person here hearing them read.
---

A quarter to eight, at that wall, and the light not over it yet.

He has done this before the sun is on the stone since before the middle of the winter, and he does it with his fingers because the light is not what he is reading by.

There are four figures cut into that stone a foot apart and about as deep as a thumbnail will go, and he has read all four off that wall on the seven hundred and sixty-ninth morning of that run, and he is going to read all four off it again at about four this afternoon, and both times he is going to get all four.

That is the job. There is no other part of it and there has never been any other part of it and nobody has ever come out of that yard and said a word to him about it.

He read the four from the top down, the way he has read them every morning of that run, and he got all four, and he put his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and said his four words to it.

About nineteen people heard those four words, because about nineteen people are in that yard at a quarter to eight, and nobody has ever asked him where they come from and nobody has ever asked him what they are for.

---

The boy was at those boards at ten to eight and had said his number out loud, and came down the slope at about ten and came the whole way along the stone until he was about four feet off that wall, and stopped there.

The man of fifty-six did not look round and did not stop.

"You read them before the sun's on them."

"I read them before the sun's on them."

"You always do that."

"Always."

"Why."

The man of fifty-six put his hand down off the stone and turned round, which he does perhaps once in four hundred times when somebody at that wall says a whole word to him.

"Because there is a light coming over the top of that wall at about eight," he said, "and it comes at an angle, and by the time it is on that stone the first one is gone in the shadow of the lip of it. I read them before the light and read them again when the light is straight on, and I get the same four both times, and I have never once been asked whether I get the same four."

The boy opened his mouth and shut it again.

"You have never been asked," said Orren Pike.

"Not by anybody," said the man of fifty-six, "and that includes you this morning, because you have not asked me and you stood there and waited."

"I was going to."

"I know you were," said the man of fifty-six. "You do that too."
---

He went back to the stone and read the four again on his own, standing well back from it, with his eyes shut for about half of it, which is a thing he has never told anybody either.

Then he came down the strip of stone and stopped where the man of sixty-one was sitting on the stones with his hands on his knees.

A man standing in that strip said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one about four feet off him on those stones and got one word back and neither of them said anything else about it.

About four feet further on, at the foot of the wall, a man of about sixty-four stood with his hands behind him looking at the four cut figures the way a man looks at a thing he has been looking at since before the rail went in.

Nobody put a question to him. Nobody had put a question to him on the one hundred and eighty-fourth morning running of that, and there was nobody in that yard with anything to say to a man of fifty-six either, and about half past nine a man of thirty-eight came down off that bank with his hands showing and went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back down and in at the corner he always comes in at, and worked that table from there until about four, and said nothing out loud at any point of the day, and nobody in that yard asked him one word about anything.
---

He came down the strip of stone about nine with his hand shut and did not stop anywhere, and the day went on the way that yard's days go, which is that about four people at the top of eleven feet stand and look down at it and about fourteen people at the foot of it work and nobody says to anybody one thing that is worth the saying.

The wheel went at the speed it goes at. A cart came through the gap and went round by the second table and did not stop. A man went up that bank with a barrow and came back down it empty. The boards at the far end of that ground went up one at a time off the cart, face down, and the light stayed on the eleven feet while they did it, which is not always the case.

And about four feet off the near end of the first table a man of thirty-eight worked a table from about half past nine until about four and said nothing out loud at any point of the day, and nobody in that yard asked him anything out loud at any point of the day, and there is not one person in this district who could tell you what it is like to stand four hundred yards off a man who says nothing.

---

The afternoon was long and the light came round.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and the day carried on the way that yard's days carry on after a number.

The boy came down again about half past two and sat down on the stones on the other side of the man of sixty-one from the man of fifty-six and put his elbows on his knees and did not say anything for about four minutes.

"You read them the same way every morning," the boy said at last.

"I read them the same way every morning."

"What would happen if you read them the other way round."

The man of fifty-six thought about that for long enough that the boy thought he was not going to answer.

"Nothing," he said. "That is what I have been turning over all afternoon. Nothing would happen. I would get the same four in a different order and there would be no way on that wall to tell me which order was the right one, and I would have gone two hundred and seventeen mornings without ever once knowing whether I was reading that stone or remembering it."
---

At about four he had his hand up on that wall.

He started at the bottom.

He had never once done it. The lowest figure first, then the one above it, then the second from the top, then the top one, all four in about as long as it takes him to say his four words, and he got all four of them, and the light was straight on that stone and there was nothing in it to be got wrong.

Then he put his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Two hundred and seventeen mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I read them off you this afternoon in the order I have never once used, and I got the same four, and I cannot tell you which of us has been doing the reading all this time."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and seventeenth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk read that morning out loud at the boards with the pen in the holder and gave the whole of the account of it before she had the hand near any part of it.

"The three hundred and twenty-eighth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and twenty-eighth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.
---

At about half past four the light went off those boards. About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard.

A man of fifty-six came down off that stone and went up the bank with his hands shut. About four hundred yards off him, a man of thirty-eight put a cloth down on the corner of his own table and stood a moment with his hand on it, and went up the bank, and the light was still on the top of it when he got there. Neither of them said one word to the other, and neither of them said one word to anybody, and nobody in that yard said one word to either of them.

That morning the man of thirty-eight was at the near end of the first table from about half past nine until about four and said nothing out loud at all, and nobody asked him anything out loud. The page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines and did not move. The lane count was thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have gone through this district unread. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
