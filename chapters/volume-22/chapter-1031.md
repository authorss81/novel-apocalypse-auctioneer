# Chapter 1031: The Three Hundred And Fifteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Corner Not Square

Nobody in this basin has ever gone at how much of it is out, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and seventy days is the age of that figure, and it has been on that sheet every morning of that run.

The board carries nine hundred and twenty-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and forty-five days and has not shifted a yard. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and fifty-nine days. Nine hundred and twenty days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, it was not run, and it is not open now. Six hundred and thirty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this yard has got. Six hundred and thirty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and forty-eight days past a printing it did not make. The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and twelve days back and no night has been named.

Five hundred and eighty-eight marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and seventy-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. Seven is what a clerk came to and it is on nothing whatever.

This district is four documents short and always has been. There are four places in this district that have ever had a reader for those three lines. Four conditions in this place have nothing at the end of them. Fourteen is the figure for what has been made here, and five for what has not, and the fifth of the five is unpaid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nobody said a reason out loud in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-one lines and carried sixty-one lines when the covers came over it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it. The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-five mornings.

The near rail has stood on the near side of it two hundred and ninety-five mornings. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifteen mornings, on the word of the man who keeps the tally, and he said that number out before it was entered anywhere. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and fourteenth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail, and those two are a different thing from this one. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fifty-sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and seventieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and sixty-nine of them. A man of sixty-one was on those stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and sixtieth afternoon running. And this is the one hundred and seventy-first mornings running that nobody has asked him one word. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the two hundred and fourth time running. That lot book lies open along those boards where it lies every morning. There are five lines on that book and a column on the second of them with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has ever been asked to fill it.

The page at the back of that lot book carries sixty-one lines. The lane count is thirty and nothing has come down that lane to move it. The record has stood two hundred and thirty days. About nine people are standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert, and that is the whole of the distances anybody in that yard works to.

That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and nobody in this yard will settle it twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have never been read by anybody in this district.
---

He was at his own corner at about half past eight and he had the cloth over his shoulder, and the wind came over the top of that bank at about eleven and took the loose edge of it and put it down again about four times before noon.

That corner has been out of square for three winters. He has watched it go out, and he has watched it come back in, and he has watched it go out again, and he has never once put a finger on it, and there is a reason for that which is not a reason at all, which is that he would have to say something out loud to get anything under it.
---

The man of about thirty-two came along with his own cloth and stopped at the end of his table and looked down the length of it.

"Your boards are cupping," he said.

"They have been cupping since the wet."

"It is the wrong way up for the wet you are getting." He put his thumb flat on the top and rocked it a quarter of an inch. "That is not a wet thing. That is a dry thing that has dried."

"Everything out here is the wrong way up for the wet."

"Aye," the man of about thirty-two said. "Everything out here is the wrong way up for the wet." He went along his own top with the cloth and did not say anything else, because there is not one further thing to say about a table to a man who has been going along the same table for three years.
---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down off that bank with his hands showing, went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back down, and came in at the corner he always comes in at.

He was about nine feet off. He had a cloth in his hand and he did not have anything in his mouth.

The man of about thirty-four stood with his own cloth over his shoulder and listened to that round go by, and there was a sentence in him that had been in him since yesterday afternoon, and it had got no further all night than it had got by the middle of the afternoon.

He could not say it. Not because of him — there was nothing standing in the way of him saying a thing out loud in that yard — and not because the man of thirty-eight had done anything to be careful of, and not because anybody at the top of eleven feet had asked him not to. He could not say it because he had got as far as the front of it about nine times since yesterday and about nine times the front of it had turned out to be a question, and a question is the one shape of sentence that cannot be put down in that yard and walked away from.
---

He wanted a straight edge at about a quarter to eleven and it took him until twenty past to ask for one.

The man of about thirty-two was along his own top with the cloth and he stopped when the other one came up.

"Have you got a straight edge."

"I have a straight edge."

"Then lay it along that corner for me."

The man of about thirty-two stood where he was.

"That is your table," he said.

"It is my table."

"Aye. And a straight edge laid on your table is a thing that has been laid on your table, and there is a man four feet off me who would see that and would have an opinion about it, and he has not said anything to you about that corner in three winters and I am not going to be the one who starts it."

"You could stand where he cannot see you do it."

"He can see everything on that ground. That is the whole of what he does."
---

The turner came past at about eleven with his hand still warm off the frame and stopped, because a man standing on one knee in the grit with a hand under a table is a thing you stop at.

"That is the corner," the turner said.

"That is the corner."

"I can hear it. It knocks when you take your weight off it."

"You can hear it from there?"

"I can hear it from the wheel. A table that knocks is the loudest thing in this yard and it is loudest on the upstroke." He put his thumb on his own hip where he had leaned. "That is the second worst corner on this ground."

"What is the worst one."

"The second table. About nine feet along from where she sets up." He went on up the slope. "I have never told anybody that either."
---

He worked his own top from about ten.

He came to the corner at about eleven and got down on one knee in front of it and put the flat of his hand on the boards where they meet the frame, and the frame went under his hand about a sixteenth of an inch, which is not much, and it is the first time in three winters that it has gone at all.

He sat back on his heels.

That is the thing about the corner. He had it in his head for three winters as a thing that was set wrong, because a thing that is set wrong stays wrong and a thing that has moved has moved, and those are different and he had never once gone and found out which one he had.

He put the heel of his hand under the frame and lifted while he looked down the length of it with his eye near the boards.

The boards were not level.
---

They had gone down at that end about a half-inch over the last four feet, and they had gone down on a slope, and the frame had stayed where the frame was, and that is all a corner out of square is when you get right down to it.

A thing that is set wrong you can take to pieces.

A thing that has sunk you cannot take to pieces at all without taking up the whole run of boards, and that run goes from that corner to the other end of that table and under another man's feet for about six hours a day.
---

He sat on his heels in the grit for a while with the cloth over his shoulder and the sun coming over the top of that bank onto the boards he was not working.

Here is what he had wanted from that morning, and it was a small thing and he had wanted it before the light was on them.

He had wanted to say one sentence out loud to the man of thirty-eight. It would not have had a question in it. He had had it shaped four or five different ways since yesterday afternoon and every one of them had ended with a word that made it a question, and the fifth one had been a flat statement and it had not been plain enough to stand on.

What he had wanted was not to be thanked and not to be agreed with. He had wanted one sentence to be in the air of that yard that came off a person who had heard it, so that the man of thirty-eight would have known that one other pair of ears in that place had it.
---

The man of about thirty-two came past at about half past twelve on his way to the far end with his own cloth.

"You have been on that corner about an hour and a half."

"I have."

"Has it told you anything."

"It has told me the boards have gone down." He got his weight off it and stood up. "That is not the same as being told, but it is more than I had at eight this morning."

The man of about thirty-two stood with the cloth in both hands and looked at the corner of somebody else's table.

"Three years I have looked at the end of my own," he said. "I know where every board on it is soft and I could not tell you which way the grain runs in half of them."

"You could if you got down on your knees."

"I could if I got down on my knees and if there were nobody watching, and there is about one of those two about nine days in ten." He went on along his own boards. "You are a quicker man than me and I am not saying that to be pleasant about it."

At about half past twelve he got up off his knees and put his weight on that corner to feel it go, and it went about as much as a floorboard goes and came back.

He worked the rest of his top.
---

She came down that slope at half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and put a bar on her own pan, and he could hear that pan want to follow the wind and then stop wanting to, the way it does, four or five times an afternoon.

Not one figure of hers came out of that yard that day. He heard all of them go onto her own account and come off her own account and not one of them came out into the air.

At half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air, and four voices took it back after him out of step with one another, and it went off the top of that bank and up the hill.

That goes every morning. He had heard it every morning of his working life in that place and he could have said the number himself, and he never had, and there is a reason for that too and it is not a reason.
---

About a quarter to two the man of thirty-eight was at the near end of his own table with the cloth in his hand.

The man of about thirty-four had a sentence. He had had it since about one o'clock and he had it now, shaped, plain enough to say out loud, and about four feet and a half of that yard was between the two of them and nobody else was near enough to hear either one of them.

He said the other one.

"You have got a low place in your top," he said. "About four feet along. It has been taking the water since the wet and it has never dried out."

The man of thirty-eight looked down his own table about four feet along, and he put his hand flat on the boards there, and he said nothing at all.

That was all. The man of thirty-eight said nothing else at all that day. He went on along his own top with the cloth and the man of about thirty-four went back to his own corner and stood over it.
---

Nothing of his own went out of that yard that afternoon.

That is the whole of the shape of that day and he went back to his own corner with the cloth and carried on down his own boards.

He has been three years at that table and about four of them in that yard and he has said one reason out loud in it, once, and got nothing back, and he has had the sentence about the man of thirty-eight in his mouth since yesterday afternoon and he has spent this morning not getting it out, and he put a different sentence in the air at a quarter to two about a low place in a table top.

The sentence about the table was plain and it was about the table and it was said in the ordinary voice about four feet off a man who had said a figure out loud the day before and about nine feet off a woman who had not said anything at all for a week.

The other one is still in him. It has not gone anywhere and it is not going to go anywhere on its own and he has not got a way of getting it out that does not go through a question.
---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have not said what he said it for.

They said it up there on the bank in the light, four feet from the boards and out of the wind, among themselves, and never in front of him and never in front of the man of thirty-eight.

One of them was the woman of about fifty-two, who has a frame and a bag and a form with a blank space on it, and one of them was a man who comes down that lane four days a week and does not stop, and about four of those four people have not said what a figure of his own is about, and about four of them have not asked him, and about four of them have not said what anybody ought to do about it.

That is the arrangement at the top of that bank. Nobody made it and nobody is going to hand it over.
---

A little after half past three the sun came over the top of that low wall and laid a strip of it down along the cold stones at the foot of it, and a man standing in that strip said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the stones, and got one word back, and neither of them said anything else about it.
---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall with his hand already up on the stone and got all four of them off it one after another, and his hand came down and lay flat over the lowest one and stayed there.

"Four figures cut in a stone and not one of them will tell a man anything," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard it, that was the two hundred and fourth time running, and nobody has asked him one question about any of it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards and gave that morning out loud, and the book was open against the rail and the pen was nowhere near it.

"The three hundred and fifteenth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand on its own with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it and it went out loud before any of it was written."

She wrote the three hundred and fifteenth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty-one lines when the covers went over it.
---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in it.

He put the cloth over his shoulder and went up that bank behind a man with a barrow, and about four feet off the near end of the first table a man in a coat was taking a cloth off the corner of his own table and putting it away.

Nothing was said to him on the three hundred and fifteenth morning and nothing was said to that other man either, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line and stands at sixty-one lines. The count at those boards is thirty. Not one of the first thirty-three lines on that back page has ever been read here. The fifth of the five is five and nobody has paid for it. Seven months is counted and it is on nothing at all. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven.
