# Chapter 1034: The Three Hundred And Eighteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Second Figure Went Out Hours After The First One

No instrument in this valley has ever been pointed at that sheet, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and seventy-three days is the age of that figure, and nobody here has ever said what it is the age of.

That board has carried nine hundred and thirty-two since before the rail went in. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and forty-eight days without a wagon moving off it. Nine hundred and sixty-two days is how long that entry has stood empty. Nine hundred and twenty-three days lie between the second of January and this morning.

It stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was never run and is not open. Six hundred and thirty-seven days is how far behind that second line the figures in this yard have got. The rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and forty-two days. Seven hundred and twelve days is how far it is from the first day of the eighth month to this morning. Six hundred and fifty-one days past a printing it did not make is a body four hundred miles off. The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and fifteen days behind this morning.

Five hundred and ninety-one marks have been taken off that board with a knife. Five hundred and seventy-seven marks in chalk are on the edge of that second table. The count of months came to seven and there is no paper it is on.

Four documents have never come into this district. Four places in this district have ever had a reader for those three lines. This district works under four conditions and not one of them is finished. There are fourteen things this district has made and five it never got, and the fifth of those five is unpaid. On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and that was before the page at the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

There was no reason said out loud in that yard this morning. The page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines all day. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it. The second table has been at the foot of that bank for three hundred and forty-eight mornings.

There has been a near rail on the near side of it for two hundred and ninety-eight mornings. There has been a man at the foot of that bank for three hundred and eighteen mornings. Nobody enters it but the man who keeps the tally, and he says it out before he writes it down. The man of about thirty-two's own table has stood there for the three hundred and seventeenth morning. That table is its own thing and does not go in a sentence with the second table or with that rail. The four figures on that wall have been read off by a man of fifty-six on the seven hundred and fifty-ninth mornings, and about nineteen people heard what he said to that stone at four o'clock. That man of about sixty-four was at the foot of the low wall on the six hundred and seventy-third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and seventy-two of the nights going. About four feet off him a man of sixty-one was on the cold stones for the two hundred and sixty-third afternoon running, and nobody has ever asked him for anything. And he is asked nothing, which is the one hundred and seventy-fourth mornings running of it. The flat answer is the two hundred and seventh time running and it is said to a stone. The lot book is spread along those boards and left open there all day. Five lines are ruled across the first of its pages and a column on the second of them has one name in it, and the heading over it says whoever. The second of those spaces is empty, and nobody has been asked to write in it, including by naming it.

Sixty-two lines are on the back of that lot book. The lane count is thirty and no page has come down that lane in this run of mornings. It has stood on that record two hundred and thirty-three days and it has never once been asked what it counts. About nine people are on that ground, which has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert, and neither has changed.

The toll of ninety-eight has been paid and spent and it stays paid. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have never been read to anybody here.

---

He came down off that bank at about half past nine with his hands showing, and he went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back down, and he came in at the corner he always comes in at.

His coat is over the back of the stool at that end. It has been over the back of that stool for as long as the stool has been at that end, and the stool is there because there was a job at that end once that wanted a man sitting down for, and the job is not there any more.

---

The man of about thirty-four came along his own boards with his cloth and stopped where he stops.

"That low place in your top," he said. "I have been thinking about it since I said it."

"Aye."

"I have got a piece of lead wire in my pocket and I have been carrying it round three days deciding where to lay it in."

The man of thirty-eight went along his own top about four feet along and did not look at the place.

"Do not lay it in," he said.

"Aye." The man of about thirty-four stood there for a moment with the cloth over his hand. "That is what I have been deciding. I did not want to have said it out loud for three days, because if I say it out loud then it is a thing and not a thing."

"It is not a thing," the man of thirty-eight said. "It is a table."

Then he went round the end of his own table and up the slope and back down.

---

At about eleven he said one figure of his own out loud.

It came out of him at about four feet off the near end of his own table, once, in the ordinary voice, at the speed a man uses for a thing he means, and it went out over about four hundred yards of air and off the face of that bank.

About nine people heard it.

Nothing at all came back out of that yard at him. Nobody thanked him. Nobody asked him anything, and nobody asked him what the figure was about, and nobody at the top of eleven feet called down, and nobody at those boards turned round on the rail, and the woman at the scale about four feet off the near end of the second table went on with her own pan.

Then that yard carried on with the middle of its morning.

---

There is a thing that happens to a man who has worked a table for nine years and has never once been asked what he is doing.

He gets very good at the part of the day that is in front of him. That is the whole of the trade and there is nobody to teach it to you and nobody to notice that you have it.

At about half past nine that morning he did the north end of his own table, which is the end that catches the sun, and he did it the way he has done it about nine hundred mornings, and at about ten he did the middle, and at about a quarter to eleven he came round the end of it to the near corner.

He has never in nine years stood at that corner with a figure in his mouth before this morning and he had no idea that there was a difference in the way a hand goes along a table top depending on what is in the man doing the hand.

There was a difference. He found it out at about eleven.

The rest of that morning was the longest part of that day.

That is not a thing he would have said to anybody and it is not a thing anybody in that yard could have seen. He worked his own top. He went along it and round the end of it and up the slope and back down four or five times and did the north end and did the near end, and every time he came in at that corner he was aware of the whole of that ground the way a person is aware of a door they have to go through.

He had put one figure into that air at about eleven and there was nothing whatever in that air to follow it.

---

The man of about thirty-four came along at about ten with his cloth over his shoulder and stopped at his own end and looked down the boards.

"You are doing that round of yours faster this morning."

"I am doing it the same. It is the same length."

"You have done it four times since I got here and the light has not moved off that bank."

"It has moved a foot," the man of thirty-eight said. "You cannot see a foot in an hour."

The man of about thirty-four stood there with the cloth in his hands.

"You could tell me how many you have done. You have not got to count them out loud. You have only got to say the number."

"No," the man of thirty-eight said, and went round the end of his own table.

He did not say it unkindly and he did not say it quickly. That is the whole of what he gave the other man and it was enough and neither of them said anything else.

She came down that bank at about half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table with her basket on her arm and put a bar on her own pan.

The wind had been on that pan since about eleven and it had not dropped and it had not risen. He had heard it on every one of the bars that morning.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it.

It went up the bank and about four people said it back after him out of step with one another, and it came down again, and that is the number that has gone out of that yard at half past one for longer than he has been standing at his own table.

---

And then a second figure went out.

It came out of her, about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice, and it went out over that yard and off the face of that bank, and it did not come back with four voices on it.

One figure had gone out of that yard at about eleven and a second figure went out of it after that, and the second one came about two and a half hours after the first one, and about nine people were standing in that yard when both of them went.

---

He was at the corner he comes in at with the cloth in his hand.

He did not stop.

He went along his own top and round the end of it and up the slope and back down and in at the same corner, and the whole of that round makes no sound at all and there was nothing in it that anybody could hear from four feet away.

Nobody at the foot of that bank put the two of them together. Nobody said the two were the same thing. Nobody said the two were different things. Nobody asked either of them what it was about.

---

She came down that slope about four feet off him and put the bar on her own pan and let it want to follow the wind until it came still.

She did not look over at his table and she did not look at anything. She had the same face she has had every afternoon of that run of nine years, and he has looked at that face about two hundred times a day for about two and a half hours and about nine hundred times in nine years.

There was a thing in him about four hundred yards long and it had been there since about eleven.

---

The man of about thirty-four came along about half past two and stopped at his own end and worked his own boards and did not say anything for about a minute.

"There was a thing at four o'clock yesterday," he said, to the boards.

"There was."

"I have been standing up that bank all morning with about four people who will not say what they think about it."

"Then they will not say it."

"Aye." He went along his own top. "That is what I have got instead of an answer, and I did not know that was what I was getting until this morning."

He put the cloth down on the corner of his own table at about two and picked it up again at about half past two, and that is the whole of what he did with his hands for the rest of that afternoon.

What he went back over afterwards was the two and a half hours and not the two figures.

He has been at that table nine years. In nine years there has never once been a morning when he put anything into that air, and there has never once been a morning when he wanted to know whether anything would come back off it, and this morning there had been about two and a half hours in the middle of the day in which he found out.

There is no way of putting that back to how he was at about half past nine.

---

A little after half past three the sun came over the top of that low wall and laid a strip of it down along the stones at the foot of it, and a man standing in that strip said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the stones, and got one word back, and neither of them said anything else.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four of them off it one after another, and his hand came down and lay flat over the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Two hundred and seven mornings, and the only one who has ever heard me is that wall," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and seventh time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

---

At about ten past four the clerk was at those boards with that book open against the rail, and the whole of that morning went out into that yard before the pen was anywhere near it.

"The three hundred and eighteenth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it and it went out loud before any mark was made on it."

She wrote the three hundred and eighteenth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and that page was carrying sixty-two lines when the covers came over it.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in it.

He put the cloth down on the corner of his own table and took his coat off the back of the stool and put it on, and then he picked the cloth up and folded it over his arm, which is the opposite of what he does.

Then he said the goodnight to nobody in particular and went up that bank behind a man with a barrow.

One figure went out into that yard at about eleven on the three hundred and eighteenth morning and a second one went out of it after that at about half past one. The page at the back of that lot book took no line for either. Nothing came back out of that yard at either of them, nobody thanked either of them and nobody asked anybody anything. That rail carries thirty pages and nothing came down that lane this morning. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have gone through this district without anybody hearing them read. What this district does not have is five, and the fifth of those five is unpaid. Seven months is counted by a clerk and it is written on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
