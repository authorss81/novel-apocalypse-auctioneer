# Chapter 1025: The Three Hundred And Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Half Past One The Man Of Thirty-Eight Said One Figure Of His Own Out Loud, Once, After The Figure The Man Who Keeps A Tally Says Every Morning Of His Life, And About Nine People Heard Both

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and its size of error is uncomputed and unexplained and unpaid. Seven hundred and sixty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and twenty-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and fifty-three days. Nine hundred and fourteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot. Six hundred and twenty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this yard has got. Six hundred and thirty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood where it was said. Seven hundred and three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and forty-two days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and six days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and eighty-two marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and sixty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing whatever. Four places here can read those three lines. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four conditions have no end on it. Five is what this district does not have and fourteen is what it has made, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and that page was not yet a place a reason could go, and no line on it was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because it was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud by anybody in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-one lines and carried sixty-one lines when she shut it. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-nine mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty-nine mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and nine mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and eighth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fiftieth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and sixty-fourth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and sixty-three of them, and this is the one hundred and sixty-fifth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninety-eighth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries sixty-one lines. The record has stood two hundred and twenty-four days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fifty-fourth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight stands paid and spent and is not going to be raised or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

He has a count of his own in his head and he has never once put it out loud.

Three hundred and eight. That is what his own table has stood at the foot of that bank this morning, entered every morning the way a man enters a thing he has done every day of his life, and nobody has asked him for it and he has not said it and there is no name on it anywhere.

He has thought about saying it. Every time he has got as far as having the figure in his mouth and has found that he does not know what the end of the sentence would be.

---

That is what a count is when you are the only one carrying it.

Put a number out into that yard with nothing on either end of it and what happens is four voices take it up out of step with one another and then there is no number in the air at all. He has watched that happen every morning of the run of his being at that yard. He has got a figure and he has got no sentence, and those are the two halves of it and there is no third thing he can add.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it.

It went up the bank. Four voices picked it up out of step with one another and came back down again. He has heard that number go out of that yard more mornings than he has had any business hearing it, and it has never once been in a book, and it has never once been in anybody's hands by the time it has finished being said.

That was the first thing of that afternoon and then the yard carried on.

---

And then a figure of his own went out of that yard.

One figure of a man's own came out of that ground at about nine feet off him, from the near end of the first table, and it was said once, in the ordinary voice, the way a man says a thing he means and is not asking anybody anything about.

About nine people heard it.

He heard all of it. He had the cloth over his arm and he was standing at the near end of his own table and there was about nine feet of that yard between him and where it came out of, and what came out of it was one number.

---

Nobody said the two numbers were about the same thing.

Nobody said they were about different things.

That is everything he gets to say about the two of them, and he was aware of that standing there. There is a number that goes out of that yard at half past one every morning and four voices take it, and then there is another number that comes out of the same air a moment later, and about nine people heard both of them, and not one person in that yard opened their mouth about the pair of them.

He has been three years here and there is not one morning he can put his hand on where anybody did that.

---

That is the thing about that yard and it is the whole of what he has learned standing at a table in it.

Anything can go out into that air. A number goes out at half past one and four people carry it up the bank. A reason goes out and about half an hour later a pen goes along a page about four hundred yards off. A figure goes out once from a man at a table and about nine people hear it and the yard carries on with the middle of its afternoon.

What never happens is anybody saying that two of those are a pair.

---

He went back to work and he did not say his own figure.

There is a thing about that yard in the middle of the afternoon that a man who stands at a table can watch happen.

The sun comes over the top of that bank at about half past three and lays itself down along the ground, and where it stops is about four feet short of the second table, which is the same four feet that everything in that yard keeps itself to. So the whole of every afternoon nobody at either table ever stands in any sun at all, and the strip of light lies there about four feet away from them going along towards the low wall and stops against the stones.

He has watched that light travel along that strip for as long as he has had a table at the foot of that bank, and there is nobody to say anything to about it, and he has never once moved his table a foot into it.

He had it in his mouth. Three hundred and eight, his own table's own count, the thing he has been carrying since the morning that table went up at the foot of that bank. It was there at half past one and it was there after the other number had gone off that yard, and there was a stretch of that afternoon in which he could have put it into the same air as the other two and he did not, and there is nothing in him about that now either way.

---

The sun cleared the top of that low wall at about half past three and lay itself down along the stones at the foot of it, and a man who had been standing in the shade since about nine said good afternoon to the man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones, and got one word back, and that was the whole of it.

---

He was at his own wall a few minutes before four with his palm up against the stone, reading the four off it the way he has read them since before the middle of the winter came on, and at about four o'clock he had all four of them and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying.

"I have said this out loud every morning of that run and nobody in this basin has come to find out what I am for," he said to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and ninety-eighth time running and nobody asked him anything about any part of it.

---

The clerk came along the boards at about ten past four with the book open against the rail, and said the account of that morning out loud before the pen was near any part of it.

"The three hundred and ninth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my own hand with nothing in it, and that is the account of it and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and ninth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty-one lines when she shut the book and carried sixty-one lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said that afternoon by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of that bank stayed where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

---

At about half past one on the three hundred and ninth morning the man of thirty-eight said one figure of his own out loud, once, at the near end of the first table, after the figure the man who keeps a tally says there every morning, and about nine people heard both of them, and nobody in that yard said the two numbers were about the same thing and nobody said they were about different things. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at sixty-one lines. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven.
