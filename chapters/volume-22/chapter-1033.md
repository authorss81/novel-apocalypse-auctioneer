# Chapter 1033: The Three Hundred And Seventeenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Figure Out Of That Yard

Whoever put that number up there never wrote down how far out it was, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and seventy-two days is the age of that figure.

Nine hundred and thirty-one is on the board at the top of that bank. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and forty-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and sixty-one days. Nine hundred and twenty-two days separate the second of January from the boards this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, nothing ran it, and it is not open now. The writing here is six hundred and thirty-six days behind the figure on the second line of that book. That yard has gone on the rule it said out loud for six hundred and forty-one days. The first day of the eighth month was seven hundred and eleven days back. A body four hundred miles off is sitting six hundred and fifty days past a printing it did not make. The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and fourteen days back, and no night was ever named.

Five hundred and ninety marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and seventy-six marks in chalk run along the edge of that second table. Seven months is what the counting came to and it is written on nothing.

This district is four documents short. Those three lines can be read in four places in this district. Four conditions stand here with nothing closing them. Fourteen things have been made here, five are missing, and the fifth of the five stands unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning. The page at the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, and no line on it was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning, because before that morning it was not a place a reason could go at all, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so that page stood at sixty-two lines at ten to eight and at sixty-two lines at half past four. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

At the foot of that bank the second table has stood three hundred and forty-seven mornings, and the rail on the near side of it two hundred and ninety-seven mornings. A man has stood there for three hundred and seventeen mornings, and that is the same figure as this morning, and it goes in after the man who keeps the tally has said it out loud. His own table has stood at that end of the ground for the three hundred and sixteenth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail, and nobody thanks anybody for it.

A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fifty-eighth of those mornings and has got all four of them both times every morning of that run. At the foot of that low wall there was a man of about sixty-four on the six hundred and seventy-second night of that run, having slept on six hundred and seventy-one of the nights of it. On those stones, about four feet off him, there was a man of sixty-one for the two hundred and sixty-second afternoon running, and one word is the whole of what he says in a day. And that is the one hundred and seventy-third mornings running that nobody has asked him one word. The flat answer of the man of fifty-six is the two hundred and sixth time running.

That book of lots is lying open along those boards with five lines on it and one column, and that column holds a single name under a heading which still reads whoever. A second space on that form has nothing in it and nobody has ever been asked to put something there. The lane count is thirty and it is not moved by a decision. That record has stood two hundred and thirty-two days. Ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning has about nine people standing on it. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert, and that is the whole of the distances here. That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not going to be paid twice. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have never been read in this district.

He got his own table up at about half past eight, before the light gets on it, with the last ten feet of it on his shoulder coming down that slope, and he put the cloth along the top of it and went to the end of it and back.

Three hundred and sixteen mornings is how long his own table has stood at the foot of that bank. He has it in his head the way he has the price of nine things in his head, and he has entered it in the morning every morning for three years, and it is the only count in that yard that he has never once put out loud.
---

The man of about thirty-four was on his knees at the far corner of his own table when the light came on it.

"Yesterday," he said, without getting up, "I found out what is wrong with my corner."

"You did not tell me what is wrong with your corner."

"I did not tell anybody what is wrong with my corner."

"Aye." The man of about thirty-two put his cloth over his shoulder. "You would not."

"The boards have gone down at that end. Four feet of them, about a half inch on a slope."

"Then it is not your corner."

"It is not my corner," the man of about thirty-four said. "It is mine and it is not the corner. I have been calling it my corner for three winters because that is where I stand."
---

They worked about four feet apart for the next two hours and said about nine things to each other, all of them about wood.

"Take the end off that one and turn it round," the man of about thirty-four said at about ten. "The grain will go the other way and it will take the water the other way with it."

"And it will not sit square to the frame."

"It will sit as square as the other one sits, and the other one sits."

"It will sit where the frame is, and the frame is where it is because somebody nailed it there and the nail is not coming out." The man of about thirty-two went along his own top with the cloth. "You can turn the ends of a table as much as you like and the middle stays where it was nailed, and then you have got a table that is wrong in a new way instead of a table that is wrong in the old one."

"That is the way of it."

"It is not much of a morning to a man."
---

He put the cloth down on the corner of his own table at about ten and went to the far end of it and looked along the whole length of it without touching anything.

The far end of that table is not square either.

He has known that for three years. What he had never done about it was anything, and what he had never done about the count was say it out loud, and this morning he found out that those two not-dos had been sitting on top of each other the whole time.

A man who says a number in a yard like this one has to be able to finish the sentence that follows it. If he cannot finish the sentence, the number is the first half of a question, and a question is the one shape of sentence that does not get put down in that yard and walked away from.

At about half past twelve she came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and he could hear that pan want to follow the wind and then stop wanting to, the way it does.

He had heard that sound from that table every working day for three years. It is a small sound and it is about four feet off the boards and it comes to the ear as nothing at all.
---

He noticed it at about a quarter to two and he noticed it the way a person notices that a cart has not come.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally had put his number up into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one and about four people had said it back after him out of step with one another, and it had gone off the top of that bank and up the hill, and that yard had carried on with the middle of its afternoon.

Then a figure of her own went out of it.

It came out of her about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice, at the speed a person uses for a thing she means and is not asking anybody anything about, and it went out over that ground and off the face of that bank.

She did not shout it. He has watched her for three years and she has never once shouted anything.

About nine feet off him, the man of thirty-eight was going along the top of his own table with the cloth in his hand and nothing came out of him.
---

Nothing came back out of that yard at her.

Nobody thanked her and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and nobody at the top of eleven feet called down about it, and nobody at those boards turned round on the rail.

That wheel went on turning at the near end of that ground without a break in it. The man at the far end of the second table kept his head over his bench. The man of about thirty-four went along his own top and did not break stride.

Then she lifted the bar off her own pan, put it back in the basket and had the next one out of it in one movement, and that yard carried on with the middle of its afternoon.
---

He did not go up that bank for about an hour after he had said it, which is not what he does.

He put the cloth over his shoulder and stood at the near end of his own table with his hand on the corner and looked down the length of his own boards.

Nobody had come and looked at that end. Nobody had put a hand on it or asked whether it stood up or asked him to show them. The cloth was still on the corner and the boards were still wrong at the far end, and what he had said had gone out into about four hundred yards of air and had not come back.

That was the whole of it, and it was a thing he had wanted for about three years, and it was over in about as long as it takes to say it.

The man of about thirty-two stood still with the cloth in both hands for the length of about three of his own breaths.

Here is a number he has been carrying in his head since the morning his own table went up at the foot of that bank, entered every morning for three years, and he has never once put it out loud because every sentence he has ever built around it has a number at the front of it and a number at the front of a sentence means that the rest of the sentence has to explain what the number is for.

A woman about nine feet off him had just put a number into that air and had walked straight on into the next bar.

That is the whole of what she did with it. There was no signal in her body that anybody could have read from the outside. He has watched her for three years.
---

The turner came past at about two with his hand still warm off the frame and stopped, the way he stops at about four places in that yard.

"You said something a minute ago."

"I did."

"I heard it."

"Then you have heard more of it than anybody else in this yard did, and I have been in this yard four years." He went along his own top. "It is a plain thing about a table and there is no figure in it."

"That is not a shape of sentence anybody has ever used in here."

"A figure with a sentence that finishes after it is not a figure," the turner said. "It is a man talking, and nobody on this ground has ever been handed anything for talking."

"And what do you get out of it."

"The same as anybody here." He went on up the slope. "That the day had a thing in it."

At about four he said a thing out loud.

He had it in his mouth for about an hour before that and he did not know for most of that hour that he was going to say it.

"The end of this table is wrong," he said, to the ground, in the ordinary voice, at the end of his own table. "And it is wrong because the middle of it was nailed, and you cannot get at the middle of it without taking the whole of it off. So it will be wrong until somebody who owns the middle of it decides."

Nobody answered him.

Nobody asked him which middle, and nobody asked him who owns it, and the man of about thirty-four about four feet off him went on with his cloth, and the sun came down off that bank onto the boards, and about nine people heard it and none of them said one word about it.

There was no figure in it. He had got that far by about half past two.
---

A little after half past three the sun came over the top of that low wall and laid a strip of it along the stones at the foot of it, and a man standing in that strip said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him, and got one word back.
---

At about four o'clock the hand of the man of fifty-six went up on that wall and came down with all four of the figures on it, and it lay flat afterwards over the lowest one.

"Two hundred and six mornings and the four of them have not changed and neither have I," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard it, and that was the two hundred and sixth time running, and nobody has asked him a question about any of it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards with the book open against the rail and gave that morning out loud before the pen was near any part of it.

"The three hundred and seventeenth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand with nothing in it, and that is the account of it and it went out loud before any of it was written."

She wrote the three hundred and seventeenth morning under the last line in her own small hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty-two lines when the covers went over it.
---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in.

He put the cloth over his shoulder and went up that bank behind a man with a barrow.

She said one figure of her own out loud at a quarter to two on the three hundred and seventeenth morning, and nothing came back out of that yard at her, nobody thanked her and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line and stands at sixty-two lines. The number at those boards is thirty and it has not moved. There are three lines out of the first thirty-three on that back page that nobody in this district has ever had read. Five is the fifth of the five and it has not been paid. Seven months is on nothing whatever. The sheet at that gatepost carries four hundred and eleven.
