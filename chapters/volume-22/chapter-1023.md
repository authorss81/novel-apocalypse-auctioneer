# Chapter 1023: The Three Hundred And Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Nobody Said A Reason Out Loud In That Yard And Nobody Said No, And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud At About Four And Wrote It On That Page In Her Own Hand Without A Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it has been on that sheet through every morning this district has run for seven hundred and sixty-two days. Seven hundred and sixty-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and twenty-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and fifty-one days. Nine hundred and twelve days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open, and it was never run, and nothing in this district has opened it since. Six hundred and twenty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this yard has got. Six hundred and thirty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood where it was said. Seven hundred and one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and forty days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and four days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and eighty marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and sixty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing whatever. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was long before the page at the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud by anybody in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty lines and carried sixty lines when she shut it. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-seven mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty-seven mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seven mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and sixth morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and forty-eighth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and sixty-second night of that run, having slept on six hundred and sixty-one of them, and this is the one hundred and sixty-third morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninety-sixth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries sixty lines. The record has stood two hundred and twenty-two days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fifty-second afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not going to be raised or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

He has a corner that is not square and he looks at it every morning and does not touch it.

That table gets carried a foot along its own boards most mornings and gets set down where it lands, and the near rail goes with it, and the corner at the near end has never once been right. He has watched that foot happen a great many times from about four feet off it and he has never once been handed an end of that table to hold, because the far end of it is the man with the stove and every person in that yard knows which end that is.

So he stands at that corner with the cloth over his hand and looks at it, and it is a thing he can look at for as long as the morning lasts.

---

A table that has been carried gets set down where it lands.

He said a thing about that once, out loud, on a morning not long back, at about ten to twelve, in the ordinary voice, and one line went on the page at the back of that lot book for it and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him.

He has not said anything out loud since then. He has gone over it since, not once in the way a man goes over a thing he wants to get right, but in the way a man goes over a thing that has come back from a long way off.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down off that bank with his hands showing, and went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back down, and came in at the same corner.

That round takes him the length of that yard and it takes him about as long as the light takes to come off the boards and on to them again. From the near end of the second table you can see him the whole way round and you cannot hear one bit of it.

---

He had the word by about eleven.

No is a short word and it does not take anything to be ready with. He had it the way a man has a thing ready, with nothing behind it and nothing in front of it, and it was in his mouth for the length of about four bars of his own work and it did not go anywhere.

---

That is the whole of what he can put in it. He has a word and there is no hour for it.

The yard gives an hour for a reason. That is what he has seen: a man stands about four feet off the near end of the second table at about ten to twelve and says why he does a thing, and it goes out into that yard and the page takes a line for it. He has seen it done once and had no part in it.

A no is not a reason and he knows that the way a man knows a thing by having it in his mouth and finding the yard has no place to put it. He is not going to say that a no would have gone on that page or that it would not have. He has not asked anybody and he is not going to.

---

What he did with the rest of that morning was work.

The corner gets looked at every morning of that run and it is never once got right, and there is a thing he has never told anybody about it, which is that he can tell it is out before he has got the cloth near it.

It is not a square corner and it has not been a square corner since the first morning he was at that end of that table. You do not see that by looking at it square or not square. You feel the cloth catch at the corner as it goes along, the same as any other day, and there is one place in that length of wood where the cloth does not want to go on.

He puts his hand flat on the wood at that place every morning to find out whether it has come right while he was not looking. It never has.

He went along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back down and in at the same corner, and he did it four more times before the light came off the boards. At about half past twelve she came down that bank with the basket on her arm, set up about four feet off him at the near end of the second table, and worked there all afternoon without saying one thing out loud at any point of it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it back after him out of step with one another, and that is all that went out into that yard this morning.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have not said what a figure of his own is about.

He is one of the four who stands at the top of that bank most evenings and he has not said a word about that. He has not said what it is about. He has not asked the man who said it, and he has not asked her either, and there is a no in his own mouth that he did not use, and none of those three things has anything to do with any of the others as far as he has ever been able to find out.

---

At about half past three the sun came over the top of the low wall and lay down along the stones, and a man standing in that strip of it said good afternoon to the man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones, and got one word back.

---

He got to his own wall a little before four and had his hand up on the stone when the light was still going along the top of that bank, and at about four o'clock all four of those figures were off it, and then his hand went flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Two a morning and all four of them every time and not one word out of anybody," he said to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and ninety-sixth time running and nobody asked him anything about any of it.

---

Just after four the clerk came along the boards to the near end of the second table with the book in her hand and stopped where she stops, and gave the account of that morning out loud before the pen was near any part of it.

"The three hundred and seventh morning," she said. "There was no reason said out loud in this yard this morning, so no line goes on the back of that book for it, and this morning goes down in my own hand on its own with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account and it went out loud before any of it was written."

She wrote the three hundred and seventh morning under the last line in her own small hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty lines when she shut the book and carried sixty lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He left the cloth where he leaves it, folded over the corner of his own table, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him to do that or anything else at that end of it, and he went up that bank with the rest of them.

---

Nobody said a reason out loud in that yard on the three hundred and seventh morning and nobody said no, and the clerk read the morning out loud at about four and wrote it in her own hand with no line in it. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at sixty lines. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and its size of error is uncomputed, unexplained and unpaid.
