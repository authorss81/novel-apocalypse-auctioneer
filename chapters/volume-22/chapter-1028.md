# Chapter 1028: The Three Hundred And Twelfth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And In The Afternoon The Man Of Thirty-Eight Went Up That Lane With Nothing In His Hand And Came Back Down It With Nothing In His Hand, And Nobody At The Top Of That Lane Offered Him Anything, And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and nothing in this district has ever been done with it. Seven hundred and sixty-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and twenty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and forty-two days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and fifty-six days. Nine hundred and seventeen days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not open and nothing in this district has ever opened it. Six hundred and thirty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this yard is. Six hundred and thirty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and forty-five days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and nine days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and eighty-five marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and seventy-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing. Four documents are not owned here. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Five is what this district does not have and fourteen is what it has made, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book could not take one on that morning or on any morning before the two hundred and eighth, and no line on it was written for a reason before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-one lines and carried sixty-one lines when she shut it. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-two mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and ninety-two mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twelve mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and eleventh morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fifty-third of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and sixty-seventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and sixty-six of them, and this is the one hundred and sixty-eighth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the two hundred and first time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries sixty-one lines. The record has stood two hundred and twenty-seven days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fifty-seventh afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised or paid a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

She lets him say it before her hand moves.

That is where a clerk stands, and she has stood at it for as long as she has been one. The boy is at that rail at ten to eight with his elbows on it and he says the number out loud in front of about four people who are waiting for it, and only after that does she open that book.

Thirty. There is no other figure coming this morning and there is no figure after it, and he says both of those parts out loud as well.

---

A clerk of fifteen years has a hand that does one thing and does it the same way every morning of those fifteen years.

That paper has a thin place in it about four inches in from the foot of the page, and it is thin because an old nib stood on it and was left standing in a year nobody in this district can name. She writes round it now. Every line she has put on that page for fifteen years has gone over that place and none of them has sat level with the lines above it.

She has not tried to move it and she is not going to.

---

The lane goes up off the top of that bank and she cannot see it from that rail.

What she can see of it is the last of it, about six feet of hard ground where it comes out at the top of that slope, between the end of the boards and a low wall with a gate in it that is not shut. There are about nine people at the top of eleven feet most of that afternoon and about four of them stand near that gate because it is where you can lean.

Thirty pages have come down that lane in somebody's own hand. That is a number the boy says out loud at ten to eight before anybody in that yard has moved a hand, and she has never seen a page come down it, and it has not been one since before she was carrying that book down that hill.

---

The morning went the way that yard goes.

Three men took the second table a foot further down that slope at nine, with the rail going along on the near side of it, and the man at that end kept his heel jammed down on a wedge until the last of them was level with him and then let the wedge drop by the leg. At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down off that bank with his hands showing and went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back down and in at the same corner, and the cloth went along the boards in his hand without making a sound.

About half past twelve she came down that bank with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she worked there all afternoon and said nothing out loud at any point of it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air, four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and she put nothing down about it.

---

At about half past two he put the cloth down on the corner of his own table.

She had the book open against the rail and she was not writing anything, because there was nothing to write, and she watched him come out from behind the near end of that table with his hands showing and go up the slope and up that bank and out along the six feet of hard ground and through the gate in that low wall.

He did not carry anything. She has watched that table from about nine feet off for a long run of mornings and there is not one thing on it that belongs to anybody else, and he went up that lane with his hands empty and nothing in them.

---

She did not go up to see. There is nobody in that yard who goes up that lane, and a clerk who has a page in her hand does not leave the rail to stand about at a gate, and he is a man who works at that table and she is a woman who writes at that rail and neither of those things has ever had anything to do with the other one.

So she stayed where she was and wrote nothing down and watched the ground at the top of that slope, which is a piece of ground with nothing on it about nine feet by four.

---

He was gone a while.

She counted a little while, and then she stopped counting, and then she went back to the page.

There was nothing to write on it. She had said that morning out loud at about ten past four before the pen went near anything, and she had said it again every morning of fifteen years, and there is no second thing she has ever said out loud at that rail on a morning when nothing had gone into the air in that yard at all.

So the pen stayed in the holder and the book lay open against the rail with the covers at the wrong angle for the wind, and she shut it and opened it again and shut it, and about four feet off her a woman went on working at a scale with the cloth off it.

That is all of it that can be counted on. The sun was off the top of that bank by the time he came back down it, so it was the better part of an hour, and he came back the way he went up, which is on his own feet and at a walking pace, and he came in at the gate and down that slope and about four feet along the boards and back to the near end of his own table.

His hands were empty when he came through that gate and his hands were empty when he got back to that corner.

Nobody at the top of that lane offered him anything. Nobody came down that hill with him. There is no one in this district who has anything to offer anybody at the top of that lane and there has never been one.

---

She has a form of words for one thing and not for the other.

That is what she noticed, standing at that rail with the book open and the pen in the holder. She has said a thing out loud before the pen goes near that page every morning for fifteen years, and it comes out of her in the same shape whatever the morning was, and there is no shape in her at all for a man going up a lane with his hands empty and coming back down it with his hands empty.

So she said nothing, and the pen stayed in the holder, and the page stayed where it was.

---

He was at his own wall at about four o'clock with his hand up on the stone, and all four of those figures came off it under his palm, and then his hand went down and lay flat on the stone under the lowest one for as long as he had it left to do.

"Everything I have said out loud in that yard has gone into that book and I have never once seen it," he said to that stone.

About nineteen people heard it, that was the two hundred and first time, and nobody has asked him about any part of it since the first of them.

---

She came along the boards to the near end of the second table at about ten past four and gave the account of that morning out loud before she had the pen near any part of it.

"The three hundred and twelfth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my own hand on its own with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and twelfth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty-one lines when she shut the book and carried sixty-one lines after.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He put his coat off the back of the stool and went up that bank with the rest of them.

---

On the three hundred and twelfth morning the man of thirty-eight went up that lane in the afternoon with nothing in his hand and came back down it with nothing in his hand, and nobody at the top of that lane offered him anything, and the lane count did not move, because a person going up that lane is not a page and a person not asking is not a page either. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at sixty-one lines. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing at all. That gatepost still carries four hundred and eleven, and its size of error is uncomputed, unexplained and unpaid.
