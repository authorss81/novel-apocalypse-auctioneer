# Chapter 1012: The Two Hundred And Ninety-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of About Forty-Eight Who Keeps A Tally Said His Number Into About Four Hundred Yards Of Air At About Half Past One And About Four People Repeated It Out Of Step With One Another And The Man Of Thirty-Eight Was At The Near End Of The First Table Saying Nothing

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-one days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and ten. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty days. Nine hundred and one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open, and it was never run, and nothing in this district has opened it since. Six hundred and fifteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book the work in this yard has got. Six hundred and twenty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood there in the open. Six hundred and ninety days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twenty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-three days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and sixty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and fifty-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

A clerk has counted seven months and it is on nothing at all. Four documents are not owned here. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen things is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud in that yard this morning by anybody, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and it stood at fifty-nine when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-six mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy-six mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-six mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-fifth morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirty-seventh of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-first night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-second morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-fifth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines. The record has stood two hundred and eleven days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-first afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not going to be raised or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

The north end of that ground is about four hundred yards from the boards and about the same from the near end of the second table, and it is the only part of that yard where you can stand with your back to the whole of it.

He digs loam. There is a strip of it along the top of that bank that has to come out and go somewhere, and where it goes is his business and the business has not changed in longer than he has been at it.

The loam comes up in slabs about the size of a door and about the thickness of his own hand, and it has to come up whole or it does not come up at all. It goes into a barrow and the barrow goes up the hard ground to the top of that bank and gets tipped, and the barrow comes back down empty, and that is eleven goes for a morning's work and he has done it about four hundred mornings out of a lifetime he has not counted.

There is a root under that strip that has been under it since before he came to it. He has got as far as two feet off it about three times and it has got as far as him every time.

---

He sets the spade against the root and goes down on it with the weight of his own arm and not with his leg, because a leg puts the shoulder out of it.

The root does not go. He works at the side of it and gets a barrow-load in three goes and then the fourth go he stands a minute with his hand on the spade and looks at about four hundred yards of air.

---

From the north end, that yard is a shape.

The wheel is a sound and not a thing. The two tables are a low grey line with a rail along it that catches the light when the light gets to it. The bank is a slope with about nine people standing along the top of it who are not going to do anything until the light goes off the boards.

And the man of thirty-eight is a small figure that goes out along a table, round the end of it, up a slope and back down, and comes in at the same corner.

You can watch him do that whole round from the north end and you cannot hear one bit of it. Nine years of that, going round the same table at about half past nine and about ten and about eleven and about noon and about one and about two and about three, and not one sound coming off any of it.

There is a man at the end of the second table who has a stove going and you can hear that from here. You can hear the second table go a foot along its own boards at nine o'clock. You can hear the spade of your own right hand at this end. Nine years, and that one figure is the only one in this ground that arrives at your ears as nothing at all.

---

About half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it.

He heard it late. It always comes to this end late. The far end of about four hundred yards of air takes the front off a sound and hands you the back of it, so what arrives at the north end is not the number so much as the fact that a number has been put out down there.

He waited the count he has always waited, which is the time it takes a man to decide he is going to say it, and he said it.

Three other voices had already been going by the time his own was out. He has never once managed to land on the same instant as anybody. It is not a thing he has ever tried to fix and it is not a thing anybody at that yard has ever said a word about, and every morning for longer than he can count backwards it has come out of him four hundred yards behind everybody else.

---

There is a thing he does about it and nobody has asked him to.

He waits until the number has been said and has begun to come back off the bank, and he counts the space between the man's voice and the first of the voices that answers it, and then he says his.

It is not a good way of saying a number. If a man has something to say at this end of a yard it is better to say it before. He knows that. He has never once done it before.

---

The barrow went up the hard ground and came back down empty and went up again, and between about one and about three he did four of them, and in between the barrow goes the spade goes along the top of the strip and the root is there.

---

About half past twelve she came down that bank and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and put a bar on her own pan.

From the north end you cannot see a figure on a pan. What you see is the shape of a woman standing still and then not standing still, and then standing still again while the wind is doing something to the pans of that scale.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his wall with his four figures and he laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"A man can get all four of these every morning of his life and be no nearer at the end of it," he said out loud to that stone.

It came to the north end as four words with the wind off them. About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and eighty-fifth time running and nobody asked him anything about it.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a figure has gone into that yard on more mornings than a line has gone down on that page.

It is a strange thing to be four hundred yards off and still hear it, because you do not hear the words, you hear that four people are saying one sentence to each other at the same time, which from the north end is a change in the sound of the bank and nothing else.

---

The last barrow went up at about half past three and he put the spade in the ground where he had been working and stood with his hand on the handle of it.

From there you can see the whole of that yard at once, and it is not a large thing to see. It is a hard piece of ground and a bank with eleven feet of it and a low wall at the bottom and about nineteen people on it who have all been doing the same things all day.

And there is one figure in the middle of it going round a table with a cloth in its hand, and from the north end, at four hundred yards, he has never once heard it.

---

The sun came over the top of the low wall a little after half past three and lay down along the stones.

Two men said good afternoon to one another at the foot of that wall, one standing in the strip of it and one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones, and from the north end what came across about four hundred yards of air was one word and then another word, and neither of them carried.

---

Just after four the clerk came along the boards and gave the account of that morning out loud before the pen was near any part of it.

"The two hundred and ninety-sixth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my hand and there is no line in it, and I have given that account out loud before I have made a mark on the page."

She wrote the two hundred and ninety-sixth morning under the last line and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book and carried fifty-nine lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He put the spade in the ground and put his coat over his arm and went up that bank at the back of everybody else.

At the top of it he stopped and looked back at about four hundred yards of air with the loam all down and his barrow empty and the wheel standing still at the near end of that ground.

There had been one number in that yard at half past one and he had been the last of the four to say it, and there had been no line on the back of that book at all, and neither of those two things had any connection with the other as far as he knew and he had never once gone to look.

---

One figure of a man's own went into that yard at about half past one on the two hundred and ninety-sixth morning and about four people said it after him out of step with one another, and no reason went out loud, so the page at the back of that lot book carried fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut it. The man of thirty-eight was at the near end of the first table that day and said nothing out loud at any point of it and nobody asked him anything. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing.
