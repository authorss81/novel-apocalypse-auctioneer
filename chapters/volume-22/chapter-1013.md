# Chapter 1013: The Two Hundred And Ninety-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Two O'clock The Man Of Thirty-Eight Said One Figure Of His Own Out Loud Once In The Ordinary Voice At About Four Feet Off The Near End Of The First Table And No Figure Of Hers Went Out At All That Morning And Nothing Else Went Out Either And Nobody Answered Him And Nobody Thanked Him And Nobody Asked Him Anything And The Page Took No Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and eleven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-one days. Nine hundred and two days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was left open four hundred and fifty days and was never run and is not open. Six hundred and sixteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this yard has got. Six hundred and twenty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood since that morning. Six hundred and ninety-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-four days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and fifty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven months is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing. Four documents this district does not own. Four places can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud by anybody in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and the morning went into the clerk's own hand without a line. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-seven mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy-seven mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-seven mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-sixth morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirty-eighth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-second night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-one of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-third morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-sixth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and twelve days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-second afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and will not be raised again or paid a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She noticed it at half past one and she noticed it the way a person notices that a cart has not come.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally puts a number into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one. It has come at half past one for as long as she has been standing at that scale, and it goes up the bank and about four people say it after him out of step with one another and it comes back down again, and then that yard carries on.

At half past one on this morning the yard carried on.

She stood with her hand on the rim of her own scale and listened past half past one, and the wheel went round at the near end of that ground at the speed it goes at, and the second table went along its boards about four feet off her, and the light on the top of that pan moved a little because the wind was still on it.

Nothing had gone out.

---

She has come down that slope nine years. There is no morning of that run she has come down it without the basket on her arm and the cloth off her own scale and the chalk in the pocket of her apron, and there is no morning of that run on which anybody at the top of it has said good morning to her.

The first year she used to think that was about her. It was not about her. There is nothing about her at all in the fact that nobody at the top of that bank says good morning, the same way there is nothing about her in the fact that the ground is hard.

She has not said a figure out loud on the way down or on the way up since about the fourth year of it.

---

She had put three bars on that pan before half past one and had said none of them out loud, and that was not new, and it was not a thing she had arranged. Some mornings her mouth stays shut from the top of that bank down to the bottom of it. This was one of the quiet ones and she had not known it until the number did not come.

Then she wrote the figure down on her own account and put the bar back on the basket and went to the next thing.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down that slope with his hands showing and went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the bank and back down and in at the same corner.

She has watched that round go nine years of mornings. There is nothing in it. A cloth along a board and a man's feet and a corner and back. If you were standing at the top of that bank with a thing in your ears you would not know it had happened.

---

At about two o'clock she put a bar on her own pan and let the wind have the first of it and then let it come still, and the figure was the figure it always is for that thing.

And a man's voice came out of the ground about four feet off her, in about nineteen people's hearing.

It was not loud. It was not soft either. It was the voice a man uses for a thing he means, and it said one figure, once, out loud, and it came out of him at about four feet off the near end of the first table.

She did not move. The bar was still on the pan and her hand was still on the rim of the scale, and she heard the whole of it, and the figure itself she has not got a hold on and has never been able to put anywhere.

---

The wheel went on turning through all of it.

The man at the far end of the second table had his head down over something on his bench. The man of about thirty-two went along the top of his own table with his cloth and carried on past her without breaking stride. A man at the near end of that second table stood with his boot on a wedge and lifted it again and went back to his bench.

Nobody answered him.

Nobody thanked him. Nobody said one word of anything back to him, and nobody asked him anything, and nobody went over to where he was, and the yard went on with the middle of its afternoon exactly as it had been doing.

---

She took the bar off her own pan and put it in the basket and got the next one out.

That is the whole of what she did. She did not go over. She did not stand there any longer than the next bar takes. She had been at that scale nine years and nobody in this basin had ever asked her what she weighs things for, and she had never once in nine years thought of going four feet to the left to ask a man a question about a number he had said out loud in the open air.

---

She weighed four more things before the light went off that bank.

Four more, in the ordinary way, with the wind taking the first of the pan every time and her letting it have the first of it. She wrote each one down on her own account and put the bar back in the basket and took the next one out, and her hands did the whole of it without her being in any of it.

And what was in her, for the length of about four bars, was this:

That a yard with one voice in it is a thing she had got a whole set of habits for. She knew what to do with a number that came out into the air and went up the bank and came back down again with four voices on it. She knew what to do with a number that came out and did not come back. She knew what to do with a silence, and what to do with a wheel, and what to do with a man putting a hand flat on a stone four hundred yards off.

There was nothing in her at all about what to do with that.

---

She has watched that round go for nine years. She knows the order of it. She knows he does the long side first and then the short side and then the slope, and she knows he comes in at the same corner every time, and she knows the cloth goes along the boards in his hand and makes nothing.

She has never once known him to be in a hurry and she has never once known him to be waiting for anything either.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have not said what a figure of his own is about.

Nobody at the top of that bank has said it to him and nobody has said it to her and it has not been said in front of the man of thirty-eight on any morning the turner has been standing at that wheel.

---

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards to about four feet off the near end of the second table and gave the account of that morning out loud before she had the pen near it.

"The two hundred and ninety-seventh morning," she said. "There was no reason said out loud in this yard this morning, so there is a line for nothing at the back of that book and the morning goes down in my own hand without a line, and that is the account of it and it went out loud before any of it was written."

She wrote the two hundred and ninety-seventh morning under the last line in her own small hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book and carried fifty-nine lines after.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six had his four figures off that wall and got all four of them and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"I have said this out loud more mornings than I have said anything else and I am no nearer knowing what these four are for," he said to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and eighty-sixth time running and nobody asked him anything about any part of it.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in.

She knotted the cloth over her own scale and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank with the rest of them and did not look back down at the near end of the first table.

---

One figure of his own went out into that yard at about two o'clock on the two hundred and ninety-seventh morning, once, in the ordinary voice, and no figure of hers went out at all that morning and nothing else went out either, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him and nobody asked him anything, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at fifty-nine lines before the clerk shut it and fifty-nine lines after. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and its size of error is uncomputed, unexplained and unpaid.
