# Chapter 1050: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Two Minutes Apart

That sheet has been on that gatepost since before the rail went in and nothing has ever been done to it, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-nine days is the age of that figure.

That board carries nine hundred and forty-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-four days without a wagon coming off it. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy-eight days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open, and it was not run, and it is not a lot and is not open. Six hundred and fifty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and fifty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty-seven days is how far past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and thirty-one days back. Six hundred and seven marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and ninety-three marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months a clerk has counted and there is no list for them anywhere in this district. Four documents are not owned here. Those three lines are readable in four places in this district and not in a fifth. Four conditions have no end on it. What has been made in this district is fourteen and what has never been got is five, and the fifth of those five stands unpaid this morning as it has stood every morning of this run of mornings.

Out loud in that yard, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, a reason was said, and there was no page at the back of that lot book for one to go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, and nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason because before that morning it had nowhere to hold one, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book. That page was at sixty-two lines and stayed at sixty-two lines. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-four mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and fourteen mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-four mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirty-third morning and it is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and seventy-fifth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-ninth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-eight of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and seventy-ninth afternoon running and one word is the whole of that afternoon. And that is the one hundred and ninetieth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer is given to that stone twice a morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-third time running.

Along those boards, face down on its spine against the wind, that lot book lies open with nothing entered in it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages. A column runs down the next of them and holds one name under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody in this yard has been asked about it. That lane carries thirty pages in the carrier's own hand and delivered nothing this morning. That record has stood two hundred and forty-nine days. Nobody has paid anything for that ground and nobody had to: it has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are on it.

He has never been asked for either of them and has never offered them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. There are three lines in the first thirty-three on that back page that nobody in this district has ever heard read.
---

He has been in that yard thirty-one years and he has a wheel at a speed it goes at, and the whole of what he is for is a wheel that does not stop.

He had the stove going by six and he had banked the wheel by six and a half, and he has not put a whole sentence out loud into that yard in thirty-one years, and there is one sentence in him that he has been carrying for a long time and has never once got to the end of.

Every one of those mornings he has got as far as the front of it and stopped. He has never told anybody that he gets as far as the front of it. It is the only thing in his life he has never told anybody and it is the only thing in his life that has never once been asked for, and he has not decided whether that is a good arrangement and he is not going to.

Ten to eight, at those boards, the boy saying his number out loud the way he says it every morning, and then saying out loud that there was no figure behind it this morning, and the clerk opening that book against the rail and shutting it again.

That is the first sound of that yard and he could set a wheel by it.

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six was at that wall with his hand up and got all four off it before the light came over the top and said his four words to it, and nothing came back off that stone, which is what it does.

At about half past nine there was cloth on two tables, and about four hundred yards of air with a stone at the bottom of it, and a woman four feet off the near end of the second table who was not there yet, and nothing at all in that yard at that time of the morning that was going to need a person to hear it.
---

She came down that bank at about half past twelve with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and put a bar on her own pan.

The man of thirty-eight was at about four feet off the near end of the first table with the cloth in his hand.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices said it back after him out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and was gone.

One figure of her own went out of that yard into the open air.

It came out of her at her own scale, once, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people heard it, and it went out over that ground and up that bank and nothing whatever came back out of that yard at it.

About a minute later a second figure went out of that yard.

It came out of the man of thirty-eight, at about four feet off the near end of the first table, once, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people heard that one too, and the two of them were about four feet apart.

The turner put his heel on the frame and stopped the wheel.

That is the only way that wheel has ever been stopped. The frame takes the heel and the wheel comes off it and the yard goes quiet at the near end of that ground, and there has not been a morning in thirty-one years when he stopped it for anything at all except the day being finished with.

He stood with his hand on the frame and looked at the boards.

About nineteen people were in that yard and none of them said one word about either of the figures, and none of them thanked either person for either of them, and nobody went over to where either of them was.

Nobody asked that woman what she weighs things for.

Nobody asked that man anything at all.

He stood there for about as long as it takes the sound of a wheel to go out of a yard and come back in, and then he took his heel off the frame and let the wheel take up its speed again, and it took it up at exactly the speed it had been going at, and he did not stop it again that day.

---

A man of about thirty-two came along his own boards about two with the cloth over his arm and stopped where he stops.

"You stopped it."

"I did."

"The wheel has been at that speed every morning I have been at that table."

"It has."

"You have not stopped it once in three years, not where I could see it."

"Not once."

"Not this morning either, not for long."

"Not for long," said the turner.

He went up the slope with the cloth and did not say anything else about it, and the turner went on with what he was doing, and neither of them ever went back over it with anybody.

---

Orren Pike came down off the slope about half past two with his hands in his pockets and stood at the near end of the frame and looked at the wheel going round.

"It is at the same speed."

"It is."

"It has been at that speed all morning."

"It goes at that speed."

The boy stood there with his chin on the frame and did not ask what it was for, and he has never once asked that in two years and about three weeks, and nobody in that yard has ever told him there is a question there.

"There is a thing I could say about a wheel," the turner said.

The boy waited for it.

"No," said the turner.

"That is all right," said Orren Pike. "I have got nine spaces to count and I am late for them."

---

The man of about thirty-four came through the gap about three with a cloth over his arm, and there was a foot and a bit of pallet still lying on top of the boards at the far end of his own table where he had put it.

"You have not laid that in anywhere."

"No."

"That corner has been out of square for three winters and you squared it with your heel and then it went out again."

"It went out again," said the man of about thirty-four.

"You have had that piece in your pocket for a run of mornings and you have put it down on top of the boards where you can see it."

"That is where I can see it," said the man of about thirty-four. "You are going to want to know what it is for. I am not going to be able to tell you and that is not a thing I am doing to you."
---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four of them off it one after another and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Two hundred and twenty-three mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got all four of you off you twice today and not one person in this yard knows what they are, and neither do I, and that is where we have got to."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and twenty-third time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk was at those boards with that book open against the rail and the pen in the holder and gave the whole of the account of that morning out loud before she had the hand near any part of it.

"The three hundred and thirty-fourth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."

Then she wrote the three hundred and thirty-fourth morning under the last line on that back page in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

That went over the worn place in that paper, about four inches in from the foot of the page, about the width of two fingers, and it sat a little lower and a little further right than the lines above it, and she left it where it was, and she has never tried to correct for it in the whole of the fifteen years she has been opening that book.
---

At about half past four the light came off those boards.

About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and the turner banked the wheel and put his hand on the frame to stop it, which is what he does every morning of that run and did this morning as well.

The boards came down off the cart face down and went up the bank.

She knotted the cloth over her scale and put the basket on her arm and stood at about four feet off the near end of that second table, and about nineteen people went up that bank without her.

He went up with the last of them and stood at the top of it and looked back down at that yard and there was a woman standing at the foot of it by a table and a man standing four feet off the end of another one with a cloth over his arm, and neither of them went up.

---

One figure went out into that yard at about half past one on the three hundred and thirty-fourth morning at one person's own scale, in about nineteen people's hearing, and about a minute later a second figure went out of that yard at about four feet off the near end of that first table, in the ordinary voice, in about nineteen people's hearing.

Nothing came back out of that yard at either of them. Nobody thanked either of them. Nobody asked that woman what she weighs things for and nobody asked that man anything at all.

The page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines when the covers came over it and did not move and did not carry a sixty-third line.

That lane carries thirty pages and nothing came down it. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have gone through this district without anybody hearing them read. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted by a clerk and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost, and it will still be on that gatepost.
