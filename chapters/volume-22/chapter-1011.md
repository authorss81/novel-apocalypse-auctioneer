# Chapter 1011: The Two Hundred And Ninety-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of Thirty-Eight Was At About Four Feet Off The Near End Of The First Table From About Half Past Nine Until About Four And Said Nothing Out Loud At All And Nobody In That Yard Asked Him Anything Out Loud At Any Point Of That Day And The Page At The Back Of That Lot Book Took No Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirty-nine days. Nine hundred days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was never run and it is not open now. Six hundred and fourteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book the writing in this district has got. Six hundred and nineteen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood where it was said. Six hundred and eighty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twenty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-two days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and sixty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and fifty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing whatever. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four conditions have no end on it. This district has made fourteen things and does not have five, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud by anybody in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand without a line. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-five mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy-five mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-five mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-fourth morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirty-sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fiftieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and forty-nine of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-first morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-fourth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and ten days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fortieth afternoon running. About nine people stood on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and nothing in this district is going to raise it or pay it twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

There is a wheel at the near end of that ground and it goes at one speed, and the man at it has been at that wheel long enough to have stopped noticing the sound of it, which is the whole of what a wheel is for.

He is about forty-one. He has a stove at the far end of the second table and a fire in it by eight, and the far end of that table is the one part of this yard where a man can stand with his back to everything and still be in the middle of it.

The stove takes about four bits of wood to lay and about two matches to catch, and he has got it so that he can light it off the ground on one knee and get his hands back on the bar before the flame is properly in it. That is the whole of his cleverness and he has never needed any more than that.

At the far end of that table there is one hook with a cloth on it and a place on the wood where a man can put a cup down without the grain of it being in the way. He has had that end of that table longer than anybody has been able to say.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them before anybody in that yard had moved a hand.

"Thirty," he said, "and there's nothing else coming this morning."

The turner heard him and did not turn round. That boy's voice has gone across that ground every morning for as long as the turner can remember, and the number has never once changed, and neither has the way the boy says it, and that is a thing you can get used to the way you get used to a wheel.

---

The man of fifty-six was at his wall before there was light on it.

From the far end of the second table you cannot see a man standing flat against a wall four hundred yards off. You see the shape of him, and the shape of him was there in the grey, and then the light came over the top of that bank and the shape was a man at a wall with his hand up on it, and then he took his hand down and came away from it.

He does that twice a morning. Everybody in this yard knows it and nobody has ever asked him what the four figures on that wall are, and he has never once offered to say.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-two came along with his cloth and set his own table up where he sets it up, and put one hand flat on the top of it and took the hand away again.

Then the man of thirty-eight came down the slope.

He came down it the way he has come down it every morning, with his hands showing, and he went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the bank and back down and in at the same corner, and the cloth went along the boards in his hand without any noise at all.

That is the thing about him. Every other thing in this yard makes a noise of some kind when it is being done. The wheel. The spade at the north end. The second table going a foot further along its boards just after nine. The stove door. The man of fifty-six's boot on the gravel when he comes off that wall. Nine years of mornings, and the man of thirty-eight does a full round of that table and the yard hears nothing at all.

---

Just after nine the second table was carried a foot further along.

Three men took it and one of them walked backwards with his hand under the far rail, and a man at the end of that table stood with his boot on a wedge until they were past, and then he took the boot off and put the wedge down by the leg.

The turner has watched that a foot happen a great many times and he has never once been asked to hold anything at either end of it, because the far end of that table is his and everybody knows it. So he stands at his own bench with his shoulder against the wood and lets them get on with it.

The rail goes a foot with the table. It has gone a foot with the table every time that table has been moved, and it has never once been moved on its own.

---

The turner's own wheel went on turning at that.

He put his shoulder into the bar he had and let it run and watched the piece at the far end of it come round to where he could get at it, and he did not hurry it, and it did not hurry him.

That wheel has been at that yard longer than the tables have. It is not on the near rail and it is not carried a foot along at nine o'clock. It is the one fixed thing in this ground with a thing that turns in it.

---

About half past twelve she came down that bank with the basket on her arm and the cloth off her own scale, and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and put a bar on the pan.

The pan wanted to go. The wind came over the top of that bank about eleven and it had not let up since, and a pan wants to follow the wind, and she stood there and let it want to go until it came still.

Then she read the figure off it and wrote it down on her own account and went to the basket for the next thing.

She did not say that one out loud, and she has said a figure out loud on more mornings than she has said one quietly, and this morning was one of the quiet ones, and there was nothing in her head about it either way.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the ordinary voice.

It went up the bank and four voices picked it up out of step with one another and came back down, and the turner heard the four of them arrive at slightly different times, which is the part of it he has never got used to. Four people saying the same number and none of them hearing it at the same moment as anybody else.

He has never once gone into that number. He has never once seen the man write it down. It goes out and it comes back and that is the whole transaction, and it has happened every morning of the run of that wheel.

---

The wind came up over that bank about one and got under the cloth on her own scale and made it stand out away from her hand, and she put her thumb on it and went on.

The afternoon of that day went on the way afternoons go on in that yard, which is to say that about nineteen people did about nine things and none of them were in a hurry and all of them were the nine things they had been doing the day before.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that more figures have gone out into that yard than lines have gone on that page.

That is the nearest anybody comes to saying anything about it, and it is said about four feet from the boards, and nobody has ever said it in front of the people it is about.

---

She worked the afternoon out at that scale and the light came over the top of the low wall a little after half past three and lay down along the stones at the foot of it.

The man standing in that strip of it had been standing in it since about nine and had not moved out of it and had not gone up the bank to get his coat.

"Good afternoon," he said to the man on the cold stones about four feet off him.

"Afternoon," said the man on the stones, and that was the whole of what either of them said to the other, and the light went on travelling down the length of that strip.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was back at his own wall and had his four figures off it and got all four of them, and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"You could stand at this wall all day and not come away knowing what these four are for," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and eighty-fourth time running and nobody asked him anything about any part of it.

---

The turner took his bar off the wheel and set it on the stand and put his hand flat on the wood of it to be sure it was cool, which it was.

Then he stood at the far end of that table and watched the last of that afternoon come off the boards, and the man of thirty-eight came in at his corner for the last time of the day and stopped, and looked at nothing in particular, and put his cloth down on the corner of his own table.

Nobody asked him anything that day. Nobody has ever asked him anything at any point of any day the turner has been standing at that wheel.

---

Just after four the clerk came along the boards and read that morning out loud before she had the pen near any part of it.

"The two hundred and ninety-fifth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that belongs on the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my hand with no line in it, and that is the account of it and it went out loud before I wrote it."

She wrote the two hundred and ninety-fifth morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in and stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

She knotted the cloth over her scale and put the basket on her arm, and the chalk went into the pocket of her apron, and she went up that bank with about nineteen people in front of her.

The boards came down off the cart face down, the way they have come down at the end of that day for longer than the boards have been at that yard. They went over on their sides and the light went off all of them at once, and the two tables were standing there with nothing on them and the rail along the near side of the second one.

The turner pulled the fire out of that stove and stood in the cold to do it and then went up that bank too, and the wheel stood still at the near end of that ground for the night.

---

Nothing was said out loud in that yard on the two hundred and ninety-fifth morning by anybody that the page at the back of that lot book could take a line for, and the page stood at fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The man of thirty-eight worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table from about half past nine until about four and said nothing out loud at all, and nobody in that yard asked him anything out loud at any point of that day. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and nobody in this basin has done anything whatever with it.
