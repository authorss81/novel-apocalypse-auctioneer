# Chapter 1014: The Two Hundred And Ninety-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Woman Of About Fifty-Two Came Down That Bank With A Frame And A Bag And Asked Nothing And The Second Space On That Form Was Empty In Front Of Her And She Did Not Fill It And Nobody Asked Her To

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and twelve. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-two days. Nine hundred and three days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was never run and it is not open and there is no second opening of it. Six hundred and seventeen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book we are. Six hundred and twenty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and ninety-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-one days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-five days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-one marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and fifty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing whatever. Four documents this district does not own. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud in that yard by anybody this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-eight mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy-eight mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-eight mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-seventh morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirty-ninth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-two of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-fourth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-seventh time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines. The record has stood two hundred and thirteen days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-third afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

She had thought about the frame on the top of that bank for a long time before she carried it down.

It is four pieces of wood and nothing else. There is no glass in it and there is nothing in it to have glass in it. It is a thing you can carry under one arm and it does not weigh anything and it does not do anything.

Last time she came down that slope she had her hands empty on purpose, to find out what a yard does with a person who has brought nothing, and the answer had been that a yard does with that person exactly what it does with a post at that end of the ground.

So this time she brought something, to find out whether that made any difference at all.

There is a length of it you could use for anything. She has looked at it in three rooms and carried it up and down two staircases and put it down and picked it up again, and every one of those times there was somebody standing in the room looking at it, and every one of those times she had a sentence ready.

Down a slope with about nineteen people on it and a hard piece of ground at the bottom, she did not have a sentence ready at all.

---

She came down that bank about ten to one with the frame under her left arm and the bag in her right hand.

Nobody said good morning to her. Nobody said any other word to her either. A woman went past her at the top of the slope with a cloth over her own scale and did not turn her head, and the man at the boards looked at the ground between his own boots for about a second and then looked somewhere else.

She has been down that bank before now and it does not get different, and she had not expected it to get different, and she had come down anyway.

---

She stood the frame up on the hard ground in about four feet of sun about ten paces off the near end of that second table.

She squared it by eye. There is no straight on that ground to square anything by, so she put one edge of it parallel with the foot of the bank and left it there, and the bottom rail of it came out a hand's width out anyway.

Then she took a step to one side and looked at that yard through the middle of it.

The frame is about two feet by three and what is inside it is that yard: the hard ground, the bottom of the bank, the leg of the second table, about nine feet of a man's back going round the end of a table with a cloth in his hand.

It is the same yard. That is the whole of what she found out by standing a piece of wood in it. It is the same yard with four pieces of wood round the edge of it and there is nothing on the other side of the frame that was not there before she got there.

She looked at it for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and then she picked it up and leaned it against the leg of the second table where it would not fall over, and that is the whole of what she did with it.

A man of about thirty-two came past with his cloth and had to go round it. That is the whole of the notice that frame took in that yard all day.

---

The sun came across that hard ground from about half past two and by three it had the frame standing in it with the sun on the top rail of it and the bottom rail still in shade, and the wood on the top rail had gone the colour of butter and the wood on the bottom one had not gone any colour at all.

She stood there and watched a piece of wood change colour and thought that there was nothing whatever wrong with that either.

---

About one o'clock she took the form out of the bag.

It came out folded in four the way it had gone up that bank, and there was a stub of pencil in the fold of it that had been in the fold of it the last time as well.

She opened it out on her knee with the corner of it held down by the bag and looked at the second space on it.

The second space was empty.

She put the pencil back in the fold of the form and folded the form in four again and put it in the bag, and she did not ask anybody about it and nobody asked her about it.

---

She stood there with the bag at her feet until about two.

There was about four feet of sun left by then and the frame was standing in it with the leg of the second table in the middle of it, and she looked at that yard through it twice more and both times it was the same yard.

A yard is a yard whether you have brought something to it or not. She had come down that bank to find that out and she had found it out, and it took a morning, and it is a morning she would not have chosen and could not have done without.

---

That is the whole of what happened to that form at the foot of that bank on the two hundred and ninety-eighth morning. It came down that slope with her and it went back up it with her and the second space on it was empty in front of her for as long as the sun took to come off the top rail of that frame and it is empty now and nobody in this district has ever asked her to put anything in it.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it after him out of step with one another and it came back off the top of that bank.

She was the fifth thing that happened after that number and none of the five had anything to do with the other four.

---

About half past three the sun came over the top of the low wall and lay down along the stones at the foot of it, and a man standing in that strip of it said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones, and got one word back, and the sun went on coming down those stones.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six had his four figures off that wall and got all four of them and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"There has never once been a person in this yard who told me what these four are for," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and eighty-seventh time running, and nobody asked him anything about any of it.

---

Just after four the clerk came along the boards and read the morning out loud before she had the pen near any of it.

"The two hundred and ninety-eighth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes into my hand on its own with no line in it, and that is the whole of the account and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the two hundred and ninety-eighth morning under the last line and took no line for it.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four people out of about nineteen of them said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

She said the goodnight with the rest of them. Nobody said it back to her.

She took the frame up off the leg of that table, which took her both hands, and put it under her arm, and picked the bag up off the ground, and went up that bank with about nineteen people in front of her.

Going up it, about halfway, she stopped and put her weight on her back leg and looked down at that yard for as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

There were two tables on it and a rail along the near side of the second one and a wheel at the near end of that ground and a low wall with a strip of cold stone at the foot of it, and about nine people on the top of that bank above her waiting for her to go on up.

Then she went on up.

---

The woman of about fifty-two came down that bank with a frame and a bag and asked nothing, and the second space on that form was empty in front of her and she did not fill it and nobody asked her to. No reason went out loud in that yard on the two hundred and ninety-eighth morning, so the page at the back of that lot book took no line and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut it. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing.
