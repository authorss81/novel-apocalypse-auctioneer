# Chapter 1049: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Mallet Off A Shelf

Nobody has ever come at that sheet with anything at all, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-eight days is the age of that figure.

That board carries nine hundred and forty-seven and no hand in this district owns it. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-three days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy-seven days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty-eight days lie between the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days, was never run, is not a lot now, and is not open now either. Six hundred and fifty-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and fifty-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty-six days is how far past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and thirty days back. Six hundred and six marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and ninety-two marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

A clerk has counted seven months in this district and there is no list of anything for that figure to sit on. Four documents are not owned here. There are four places in this district that can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Made here is fourteen. Not got is five. And the fifth of that five is unpaid and nobody in this district has paid it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before there was a page at the back of that lot book for one to go on, and no line was written there for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because before that morning it could not hold one, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason went out loud in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines from the first board up to the last one off. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-three mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and thirteen mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-three mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirty-second morning and it is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and seventy-fourth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-eighth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-seven of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and seventy-eighth afternoon running and one word is the whole of that afternoon. And that is the one hundred and eighty-ninth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer is given to that stone twice a morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-second time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it. On that open book five lines are ruled on one page and a column runs down the next of them with a single name in it under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form has been empty for as long as that column has been there. Thirty pages have come down that lane and not one of them came down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and forty-eight days. Public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, that ground, and about nine people are on it.

Both of those are distances and neither of them is a figure anybody here has ever counted: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page are still not read to anybody here.
---

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six got all four off that wall with his hand up before the light was on it and said his four words to the stone, and nothing came back off that wall, and about four hundred yards off him a man at the end of a second table did not hear one word of it and did not look up.

He went to the wheelhouse at about ten in the morning.

It is about as far from the end of that second table as that table is long, and he had gone over that ground perhaps four hundred mornings in his head and had never once gone over it with his feet.

There is a shelf in behind the door in there, and on the shelf there is a mallet, and the door is not shut in that weather.

The turner was at the frame with his hand off it.

"You are not lost," said the turner.

The man at the end of the second table did not answer, and the turner did not wait for an answer, and went on with what he was doing, and that was the whole of what was said in that shed on that morning.

He took the mallet off the shelf and stood in the doorway of that shed with it in his hand for about as long as it takes a wheel to go round four times.

There was no sentence that had to go with it. He had been telling himself for a run of mornings that going to that shed meant putting something out loud into that yard first, and he had been wrong about that for a run of mornings, and it had taken him about that long to be wrong about it.

He carried it back down the boards in his right hand with the head down so that it did not catch anybody, which is the way a man carries a thing through a yard he has never once been asked anything in.
---

At the end of that second table the near leg of that bench was loose in its socket.

He took the bale tie out of his coat, where he keeps it, and folded a strip off the end of it and pulled the packed strip out of the socket with the tip of the mallet and drove the new one in with the face of it, four goes, and no more noise than that.

Then he put his weight on the bench and took it off again.

It did not knock.

He put his weight on it a second time to be certain, and it did not knock, and then he left it alone and did not touch it again that day, and he has never touched it again since.

Nobody in that yard would have heard a difference between that morning and the middle of the afternoon.
---

At about half past twelve she came down that bank and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table with her basket on her arm and put a bar on her own pan and let it want to follow the wind until it came still.

The man of thirty-eight was at about four feet off the near end of the first table with the cloth in his hand and he came past within four feet of her and of that bench twice before the light came off the boards, and made no sound at either pass, and that is a thing about that table and not about him.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and did not come back.

She said one figure of her own out loud that morning.

Once, at her own scale, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people heard it over that ground. It went out across the boards and up that bank, and nothing whatever came back out of that yard at her.

Nobody thanked her. Nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and nobody at the top of eleven feet called down about it, and nobody at those boards turned round on the rail, and nobody at the end of that second table said one word about it.

And about four feet off the near end of the first table, the man of thirty-eight did not move.

He had the cloth in his hand and he was coming back along his own top and he did not move, and he went on along that top to the north end and worked the north end, and he has not gone back over that half minute in any morning since.

---

At about one the woman of about fifty-two came down that bank with a frame under her arm and a bag in her hand, and went along the boards to where a man of about thirty-four had a foot and a bit of pallet lying flat on top of his own boards, and stood over it for a while.

"That has been on those boards a run of mornings," she said.

"It has."

"You put it down in front of yourself instead of under that corner and you have never laid it in anywhere."

"That is where I can see it," said the man of about thirty-four.

She looked at him for about three seconds and then went on up the bank without saying anything else, and she did not fill in the space on the form in the bag on the way, because she never does.

---

Orren Pike came down off the slope about one with his hands in his pockets and stopped at the near end of that second table and put his elbows on the top of it, which he does about four times a week.

He looked along that table for a while.

"Your bench is not knocking."

"No."

"It was not knocking the last time I was at it either."

"It went again," said the man at the end of the second table, "and it has not gone since."

The boy looked at the bench leg and then at the mallet standing against the side of that table with its head down, and then at the end of that table, and he did not ask where the mallet had come from, because he had been in this yard two years and about three weeks and he had a rule about a good deal of that ground.

"You have been at the end of this table a long time," he said.

"Fifteen years."

"Nobody has ever said anything to you at the end of it."

"Nobody has ever said anything to me at the end of it," said the man at the end of the second table, "and that is not a complaint. It is the only thing I have got that nobody in this place has ever thought about taking off me."

The boy thought about that for about as long as a wheel goes round nine times.

"I offered you this table to sit on," he said, "about four mornings back, and you said there was nowhere to sit at that rail."

"There is not."

"You have a bench."

"I have a bench," said the man at the end of the second table, "and a bench is not the same thing as a place, and I have worked that out over about fifteen years and I did not have to go and get a mallet to work it out."

He went back to his end of that table and the boy went up the slope, and none of that was about the figure that had gone out of that yard at about half past one.
---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall, and took the four off it one at a time in the order they come, and set his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and let it lie.

"Two hundred and twenty-two mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and the best thing anybody has ever said near me in that yard was said about a bench leg and not to me."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and twenty-second time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk was at those boards with that book open against the rail and the pen in the holder, and she gave the whole of the account of that morning out loud before she had the hand near any part of it.

"The three hundred and thirty-third morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and thirty-third morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.
---

At about half past four the light came off those boards. About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight, and nobody in that yard said one word about the mallet standing against the side of the second table on their way up the bank.

One figure of her own went out into that yard at about half past one on the three hundred and thirty-third morning, once, at her own scale, in about nineteen people's hearing, and nothing came back out of that yard at her and nobody thanked her and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and the man of thirty-eight was at the near end of that first table and did not move. That page took no line and stood at sixty-two lines. The lane count was thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three lines of the first thirty-third lines on that back page have never been read in this district. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
