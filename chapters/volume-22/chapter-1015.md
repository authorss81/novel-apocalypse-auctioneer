# Chapter 1015: The Two Hundred And Ninety-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of Fifty-Six Read The Four Off That Wall At About A Quarter To Eight And Again At About Four And Got All Four Both Times And Laid His Hand Flat On The Stone Under The Lowest One And About Nine People At The Top Of Eleven Feet Did Not Ask Him Anything About Any Of It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and thirteen. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-three days. Nine hundred and four days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and was not run and is not a lot and will not be one. Six hundred and eighteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this district is. Six hundred and twenty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and ninety-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-two days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-six days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-two marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and fifty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. Four documents this district does not own. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nobody said a reason out loud in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-nine mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy-nine mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-nine mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-eighth morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fortieth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-fourth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-three of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-fifth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-eighth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines. The record has stood two hundred and fourteen days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-fourth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not going to be paid a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He stands at about four feet off the near end of the second table with the cloth over his hand and looks at the corner of it.

That is where he stands. He has stood at that corner longer than the rail has been on the near side of that table and longer than most of what is in this yard has been standing anywhere, and the corner is not square, and he looks at it, and he does not touch it.

A table that has been carried gets set down where it lands. He has watched that table be carried a foot along more mornings than he could put in a line, and it has never once been set down straight, and it is that corner that has never been right.

He does not try to get it right. There is nobody in that yard who wants that corner got right, and a thing nobody wants done is a thing you can look at for as long as you like.

---

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six was at his own wall with his hand up on the stone.

There is no light on that wall at a quarter to eight. That is not a complaint, it is the arrangement. Four figures are cut into the stone of that wall at four heights, and the man of fifty-six can get all four of them off it in the dark with his hand, and he does, twice a morning, and he has done it for longer than anybody in that yard has been counting anything.

The man of about thirty-four was on his own route at the time and saw the shape of him against the grey from about sixty feet off and turned back to his own corner.

The four figures are cut into that stone and not painted. He has been close enough to that wall to know that much, because everybody has been close enough to that wall at some point, and what is on it is four figures and what they are is not a thing anybody in this district owns.

A quarter to eight in the dark with your hand on cold stone is not reading. It is finding out that they are where they were. He has done it since before the middle of the winter came on and he has got all four every morning of that run.

---

At about half past eight the man of about thirty-two came along with his cloth and set his own table up where he sets it up and put one hand flat on the top of it and took the hand away again.

The man of about thirty-four watched him do it from his own corner. He has watched that table go up in that spot every morning of a run that neither of them has ever put a start to, and the two of them have never once said a word to each other about a table.

---

The yard does what it does.

A wheel goes over at the near end of that ground at the speed it goes at and not one beat faster than it went yesterday.

Three men walk a table down that hard ground with a second one behind it a foot back and a rail riding along on the near side of that, and at the far end of it the man who works there waits with his heel jammed down on a wedge until the last of them is level with him. A man comes down off that bank at about half past nine showing the open palms of his hands and works his way out along the top of a table, round the far end, up the slope and back down to the same corner.

A woman comes down that bank at about half past twelve with a basket on her arm and sets up about four feet off the near end of the second table and works there all afternoon and says nothing out loud at any point of that day.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it after him out of step with one another.

Nobody at the near end of the second table said anything about it, and nobody at the top of eleven feet said anything about it either, and about half past one is not the hour that anybody in that yard has ever used to say something to somebody.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a figure has gone into that yard on more mornings than a line has gone on that page.

They say it to each other and not down the slope, and none of the four of them has ever said what the difference is, and the sentence has got shorter every year it has been said.

---

The light came over the top of the low wall a little after half past three and lay down along the stones at the foot of it, and the man standing in that strip of it had been standing in it since about nine.

"Good afternoon," he said to the man sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones.

"Afternoon," said the man of sixty-one, and neither of them said anything else.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was back at his own wall and had his hand up on the stone again and got all four of the figures off it, the second time that day.

He got all four. That is the thing about him. Twice a morning, in the dark at one end of the day and in the light at the other end of it, and both times all four, and not one morning out of the run of them that anybody can put a hand on where he has got three and had to go back for the fourth.

Then he laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there, and his hand covered about half of it.

"These four are on this wall and what they are for is not in this yard," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard those words for the hundred and eighty-eighth time running. Nobody asked him anything about it, and nobody has asked him anything about it since before the middle of the winter came on, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard the whole sentence and did not ask him one word of any of it.

---

The afternoon went on at that table. The sun came off the top of that bank and lay down along the ground in a long strip that stopped about four feet short of where the second table stood, so that the whole of that afternoon nobody at that table ever stood in any sun at all.

That is a thing about the yard and not about anybody in it. The ground at the foot of that bank is eleven feet below the top of it, and the sun clears the top of that bank at about half past three and is off that ground again by about ten past five, and the tables are where the tables are.

---

Just after four the clerk came along the boards and gave the account of that morning out loud before she had the pen near any part of it.

"The two hundred and ninety-ninth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my own hand with nothing in it, and that is the account and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the two hundred and ninety-ninth morning under the last line and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book and carried fifty-nine lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said that afternoon by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

The man of about thirty-four put his cloth over the corner of that table, which is not a thing anybody in that yard has ever asked him to do, and went up that bank with the rest of them.

The cloth stayed where he put it, on the corner, overnight, and it was still on the corner at ten to eight and it was still on the corner at half past nine, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him why it goes there or what it is for.

---

The man of fifty-six read the four figures off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four on the two hundred and ninety-ninth morning and got all four of them both times and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything about any of it. No reason went out loud in that yard that morning, so the page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at fifty-nine lines. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing.
