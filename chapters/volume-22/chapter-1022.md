# Chapter 1022: The Three Hundred And Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Woman Who Keeps A Scale Said One Figure Of Her Own Out Loud At About Half Past One About Four Feet Off The Near End Of The Second Table And The Man Of Thirty-Eight Was At The Near End Of The First Table And Said Nothing At All

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and its size of error is uncomputed and unexplained and unpaid. Seven hundred and sixty-one days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and twenty. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and fifty days. Nine hundred and eleven days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot and will not be one. Six hundred and twenty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book the writing here has got. Six hundred and thirty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and three days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-nine marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and sixty-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing at all. Four documents are not owned here. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen things is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, and no line was written on it for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because until that morning it was not a place a reason could go, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nobody said a reason out loud in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty lines and carried sixty lines when she shut it. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-six mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty-six mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and six mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and fifth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and forty-seventh of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and sixty-first night of that run, having slept on six hundred and sixty of them, and this is the one hundred and sixty-second morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninety-fifth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries sixty lines. The record has stood two hundred and twenty-one days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fifty-first afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

She has said a figure out loud on more mornings than she has said one quietly, and she has never once worked out which mornings are which.

There is no rule in her about it. A bar goes on the pan and the wind takes the first of the pan and she lets it have the first of it and lets it come still, and then either the figure is in her mouth or it is not, and there is nothing before it and nothing after it that would let her put them in order afterwards. She has gone at this nine years. She has never kept anything about it, and the not-keeping is not a decision either. It is how her mouth is.

---

She came down that slope at half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, basket on her arm, cloth off her own scale, and put a bar on the pan.

The wind had come up over the top of the bank about eleven and had not let up since. That pan wants to follow it and she stood there and let it want to go, the way she has let it want to go every morning of that run of nine years.

Then it came still and she read the figure off it and wrote it down on her own account and put the bar back in the basket and took the next one out.

---

Two more bars went on before the light changed.

That is all the difference there is between the mornings when a figure of hers goes out and the mornings when it does not. Two bars, or three, or the wind dropping for a moment, or the cloth going slack on the rail behind her. There is no signal in her body that she can read from the outside, and she has looked, and she has never found one.

She wrote the third figure on her own account and put the bar back in the basket.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it.

It went up the bank and four voices picked it up out of step with one another and came back down again, the way it has gone every morning of the run of her being in that yard, and then the yard carried on with the middle of its afternoon.

She had the fourth bar on the pan.

---

And she said the figure out loud.

That is the whole of what she did. She said one figure of her own, once, at about half past one, at her own scale, in the ordinary voice, into about nineteen people's hearing, and it went out of her the way that number goes out of the man at half past one every morning of his life.

It went up the bank. It did not come back down with four voices on it. It was out over that yard and then it was not in it any more.

---

About four feet off the near end of the first table the man of thirty-eight was in the middle of his round with the cloth in his hand, going out along the top of the table and round the end of it and up the slope and back down.

That round makes no sound. She has watched it go nine years of mornings and there is nothing in it to hear, and if you had something in your ears at the top of that bank you would not know it had happened.

She saw him at the corner. He came in at the corner he comes in at, and he did not stop, and he did not look over at her scale, and he said nothing at all.

---

Nobody said anything.

Nobody said why. Nobody thanked her for it and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and nobody asked the man of thirty-eight one word of anything, and the second table went along its own boards about four feet off her without a noise and the wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning at the speed it goes at.

The bar came off that pan and went into the basket and the next one was out of it before her hand had finished.

---

There is no habit she has for that.

That is what was in her for the length of about three bars. Not the figure, which was a figure she had weighed and put back on the basket with the rest of them, and not the man, who was a man going round a table. It is that a yard with one voice in it is a thing she has a whole set of ways of being in, and a number that comes out at half past one and goes up the bank and comes back with four voices on it is a thing she knows exactly what to do with while it is happening.

And a figure of her own going out into the same air is a thing she had no way of being in at all, and neither was there a way of being in the man not saying anything about it.

---

She weighed four more things before the light went off the top of that bank.

The wind took the first of every one of those pans and she let it have it. Each figure went onto her own account and each bar went back into the basket and the next one came out of it, and that is what her hands did for the rest of that afternoon without her being anywhere inside any part of it.

She has said figures out loud on mornings when nothing else went out into that yard at all. She has said them on mornings when the man at half past one had already been and gone. She has never once compared the two kinds of morning, and there is nothing in her now that wants to.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have not said what a figure of her own is about.

That has been said about four feet from the boards, and among themselves, and never in front of her and never in front of him.

---

About half past three the sun came over the top of the low wall and laid itself down along the stones at the foot of it, and a man standing in that strip of it said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones, and got one word back, and neither of them said anything else.

---

At about four o'clock he went up to his own wall with his hand already up on the stone and got all four of the figures off it one after another, and his hand came down afterwards and lay flat on the stone over the lowest one of them and stayed there.

"Since before the middle of the winter came on I have had my hand on that wall and there is not one figure of the four I cannot get anything out of," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard it that afternoon, and that was the hundred and ninety-fifth time, and not one of them has asked him a single question about any part of it.

---

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards to the near end of the second table with the book open against the rail and gave the account of that morning out loud before the pen was near any part of it.

"The three hundred and sixth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book. So the morning goes down in my own hand with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and I have said it before I made a mark."

She wrote the three hundred and sixth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty lines when she shut the book and carried sixty lines after.

---

The light went off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in.

She knotted the cloth over her scale and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank with about nineteen people in front of her, and about four feet off the near end of the first table a man put his cloth down on the corner of his own table and said the goodnight to nobody in particular.

---

At about half past one on the three hundred and sixth morning the woman who keeps a scale said one figure of her own out loud, once, at her own scale, and the man of thirty-eight was at the near end of the first table and said nothing, and nobody said why and nobody asked her what she weighs things for and nobody asked him anything. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at sixty lines. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven.
