# Chapter 1042: The Three Hundred And Twenty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Two Minutes Later

Nobody in this valley has ever gone near that sheet with a rule or a level or a man who wanted to be right about it, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-one days is the age of that figure.

Nine hundred and forty is carried by that board and no hand in this district has ever held it. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fifty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy days. Nine hundred and thirty-one days stand between the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open now and is not a lot. Six hundred and forty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this ground stands. Six hundred and fifty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and fifty-nine days is how far past a printing it did not make a body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights stands eight hundred and twenty-three days behind this morning. Five hundred and ninety-nine marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and eighty-five marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is no paper in it. Four documents have never come here. Four places here have ever had a reader for those three lines. Four conditions are worked under and not one of them ends. Fourteen things have been made and five were never got, and the fifth of those five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before there was a page at the back of that lot book for one to go on, and no line was written there for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines from ten to eight until the covers came over it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifty-six mornings. The near rail has been on the near side of it three hundred and six mornings and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-six mornings, put into the air by the man who keeps it before it goes anywhere near a book. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and twenty-fifth morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and sixty-seventh of those mornings and got all four. A man of about sixty-four stood at that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-first night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty of the nights of it. For the two hundred and seventy-first afternoon running a man of sixty-one was on those stones and says one word and goes. This is the one hundred and eighty-second morning running with nobody putting a question to him. That flat answer goes to the stone twice a morning and this morning it was the two hundred and fifteenth time running.

That lot book is spread open along those boards and no figure has gone into it this morning. Five lines are ruled on the first of its pages and the column beside them has one name in it under a heading that still says whoever, and the second of those spaces is empty and nobody has ever been asked to fill it, including by naming it. The lane count is thirty and that lane brought nothing down today. That record has stood two hundred and forty-one days and nobody has asked what it is for. About nine people are standing on that ground, which has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

Nobody in this place has ever measured either of those two distances: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

Nobody is going to touch the toll of ninety-eight, which is paid and spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have never once been read by anybody here.
---

He got down on one knee about half past eight, before anybody was on those boards, and put the heel of his hand on the corner he had squared the day before.

It had gone.

Not far. He got his eye down to the level of the boards and looked along the top from the far end, the way you look along a thing to see whether it is straight, and about the width of a thumbnail of the corner was out again where the ground had taken it overnight.

Three winters it had been out, and one push of a boot had brought it square, and by the time the light was properly on that bank it had begun to go back.

He sat back on his heels with the cloth over his arm and looked at it for a while, and nothing in him wanted to say anything to anybody about it, and there was not one person in that yard he wanted to say it to.
---

The piece of pallet was still in his coat.

He took it out. About a foot and a bit of it, an inch and a bit thick, two years out in the weather, one end sound and one end of it soft enough to take a thumbnail in. She had told him which end before she let go of it and he had said nothing at the time, because she had not asked him anything either.

He turned it over. He could lay it under that corner in about a minute and that corner would hold for as long as anything in that ground holds.

And laying it meant taking up four or five boards, and those boards go under the feet of a man who works a table four feet off the near end of the first table for six hours a day, and he would have to say so out loud to somebody, and he had said no reason out loud in that yard for longer than he cared to count.

So he got up and put the pallet down on the boards at the far end of his own table, flat, on top of them, where nobody puts a boot, and where it could be seen.

Then he went along his own top with the cloth.
---

The man of about thirty-two came along his own boards at about ten and stopped where he stops.

"Your corner was square when I came down."

"It was."

"It is not now."

"No."

"You squared it with your boot."

"I did."

The man of about thirty-two got his own hand down on his own top and pushed it about a sixteenth the other way, watching it.

"Nothing on this ground holds its own shape past the morning," he said. "Three years on this table and every line I take off it is a line I have to take off it again by the end of the day."

"It was square at eight."

"Then it will be square again this evening and it will go in the night," said the man of about thirty-two, "and neither one of us is going to tell that yard which of those two it was."

He straightened up and looked at the four feet of boards between his table and that one, and at the far end of it, where a foot and a bit of pallet was lying flat on top of the boards.

"You put that out where I can see it."

"Where I can see it."

"My table is the one that is out," said the man of about thirty-two. "Mine has been out since the spring and I have done nothing about it either, and I have not even got a piece of pallet in my coat. Do not go telling yourself this morning was a lesson. About half of this ground is out of square and the other half is out of square in a different direction."
---

He went along his own boards with the cloth and did not answer that, because there was not one word in it that wanted an answer, and because a man who answers a thing like that starts a thing like that.
---

The boy was at the boards at ten to eight and had said his number out loud, and came back down the slope about eleven with the pencil in his hand and the cap off.

"What is that on the end of your table."

"That is a piece of pallet."

"It has a bad end."

"It has."

"You have laid it on the top of your boards instead of under the corner."

"That is where it is."

The boy looked at it, and then at the corner four feet along from it, and then at the man, and did not say any of the four things that were in him, which were that a pallet on top of boards is not a repair and is not nothing, and that he could not work out which of the two it was meant to be.

"Then what is it for," said the boy.

"For having it where I can see it," said the man of about thirty-four.
---

The man of fifty-six was at that wall at about a quarter to eight and came away with all four off it in his hand and said his four words to the stone and got nothing back, which is what happens at that wall twice a morning.

At about half past twelve she came down that bank and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table with her basket on her arm and put a bar on her own pan and let it want to follow the wind until it came still.

The man of thirty-eight was at about four feet off the near end of the first table with the cloth in his hand, working his own way down the length of it, and he came past within four feet of her twice before the light moved off the boards, and made no sound at either pass.
---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off that bank and did not come back.

One figure of her own came out of that yard.

It came out of her at her own scale, once, in the ordinary voice, and it went out over the ground and up the face of that bank, and about nine people heard it, and none of them said anything.

About two minutes later a second figure went out of that yard.

It came out of the man of thirty-eight, at about four feet off the near end of the first table, once, in the ordinary voice, at the speed a man uses for a thing he means. The two of them were about four feet apart.

Nobody at the foot of that bank said one word about either. Nobody thanked either of them. Nobody asked either of them anything at all, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line for either.

Then that yard carried on with the middle of its afternoon, the way it always does.
---

The man of about thirty-four was at the far end of his own table when both of them went.

He had his hand on the cloth and the cloth was where he had left it, and he went on along his own top and round the end of it and up the slope and back down and in at the same corner, and the whole of that round makes no sound at all.

He did not stop and he did not look over and he did not tell anybody about it that afternoon or the next morning or any morning after that.

There is a great deal that a man hears in a yard and does not carry anywhere, and he is not the only one, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him what he carries.
---

A little after three the sun came over the top of that low wall and laid a strip of it along the stones at the foot of it, and a man standing in that strip said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the stones, and one word came back, and neither of them said anything else about it. About four feet off that, at the foot of the wall itself, a man of about sixty-four stood with his hands behind him and looked at the four figures cut into the stone and did not go over to where the two figures had gone out of that yard, and nobody put a question to him about any part of the afternoon.
---

The turner came through the gap about three with his hand off the frame.

"You put that pallet on the boards."

"I did."

"On the top."

"On the top."

He looked at it and then went up the slope, and the sentence he had in him about it stayed in him, because he had said one sentence out loud in that yard already this winter and had not put it past anybody.
---

At about four the man of fifty-six got his four off that wall again and laid his hand on the stone under the lowest one.

"Two hundred and fifteen mornings," he said to that stone, "and not one of them has ever told me what a figure is for, and I have never once asked."

About nineteen people heard that, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk read that morning out loud at the boards with the pen in the holder and the book open on the rail, and gave the account of it before the pen was near any part of it.

"The three hundred and twenty-sixth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the pen was near the page."

She wrote the three hundred and twenty-sixth morning under the last line and took no line for it, and that page was carrying sixty-two lines when the covers came over it.
---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight.

One figure went out into that yard at about half past one and a second figure went out about two minutes after it, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line for either. About nine people heard both of them. That rail carries thirty pages and nothing came down that lane today. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page are still unread in this district. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted and there is no list for it. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost and has been on it longer than anybody here has been asking.
