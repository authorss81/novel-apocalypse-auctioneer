# Chapter 1040: The Three Hundred And Twenty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Worn Place In The Paper

It has been up at that gatepost since before the rail went in, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and seventy-nine days is the age of that figure, and it is not going to be.

Nine hundred and thirty-eight is on a board that nobody in this district owns. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fifty-four days and there has never once been a figure given out loud about it. Nine hundred and sixty-eight days is how long nobody has entered anything, and the clerk is asked about none of it. Nine hundred and twenty-nine days have gone by between the second of January and this morning.

It stood open four hundred and fifty days, was not run, and is not open and is not a lot. This yard is six hundred and forty-three days behind the figure on the second line of that lot book. That yard said its rule out loud six hundred and forty-eight days ago and it is still standing. Seven hundred and eighteen days has it been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off sits six hundred and fifty-seven days past a printing that was never made. Eight hundred and twenty-one days ago was the ninth of the nine printed nights, and it was not named.

Five hundred and ninety-seven is what has been cut off that board. That second table has five hundred and eighty-three marks in chalk along its edge. Seven months stands counted and there is no list anywhere for it to stand on.

The district has four documents missing. Four places in this district have the reading of those three lines. This place keeps four conditions and there is no end on any of them. This district has made fourteen and lacks five, and the fifth of the five is still owed. The one hundred and eighty-second morning of that yard is the morning a reason was said out loud, and it was before the page at the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go. No line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning, because before that morning it could not hold one, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The page at the back of that lot book held sixty-two lines at ten to eight and sixty-two lines at half past four, because nobody said a reason out loud in that yard this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it. The second table has stood at the foot of that bank for three hundred and fifty-four mornings.

The near rail has stood on the near side of the second table for three hundred and four mornings. A man has been at that bank three hundred and twenty-four mornings and it is entered on the word of the man who keeps it, and it is entered on the word of the man who keeps it. His own table has been up at that end of that ground for the three hundred and twenty-third morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. Four figures have been read off that wall by a man of fifty-six on the seven hundred and sixty-fifth mornings and nobody in this district owns that wall. At that low wall a man of about sixty-four was standing on the six hundred and seventy-ninth night of that run having slept on six hundred and seventy-eight of them. For the two hundred and sixty-ninth afternoon running a man of sixty-one has sat on those stones and one word is all he says. And the one hundred and eightieth mornings have gone by with nobody asking that man anything at all. The flat answer goes on to the two hundred and thirteenth time running, and it is said to that stone twice a morning. That lot book is lying open on the boards, face down on its spine to keep the wind off it. Five lines are on it and the column beside them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form has never been filled and nobody has ever been asked about it.

The page at the back of that lot book carries sixty-two lines and not one of them is in the lot book itself. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane on that morning. That record has stood two hundred and thirty-nine days and it is not going anywhere and the clerk has never been asked what it is for. Nine people or thereabouts are on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert, and that is what is between a person and the top of the ground.

Nobody is going to touch that toll of ninety-eight, which is paid and spent. On that back page, three of the first thirty-three lines have never once been read by anybody in this district.
---

Ten to eight, and the boards, and the boy.

"Thirty," Orren Pike said, "and there is no other figure coming this morning."

"Thank you," she said.

He went off up the slope and she opened the book against the rail and shut it again, because she does that about forty times a day, and the pen stayed in the holder.
---

She has a form of words for two kinds of morning and she has had them since she was nineteen.

The first is the morning a reason goes out loud somewhere in that yard. Then she reads the morning, and she says where the reason came from and roughly when, and she says that one line goes on the back of that book for it, and that the line carries the morning and not the reason.

The second is the morning nothing goes out loud at all. Then she reads the morning, and she says that nothing has gone out loud in that yard that goes at the back of that book, and that this morning goes down in her own hand with nothing in it, and that that is the whole of the account of it.

Both of those go out loud before a mark of any kind is made on that page.

There is a third kind and she has never had words for it and she is not going to invent any at about four in the afternoon with nineteen people standing about nine feet off her.

She is thirty-four and she has been a clerk for fifteen years and she reads that morning out loud before she writes one word of it, and she has never once been asked why.

Nobody in this district has asked her why. It is not a thing anybody in this district would think to ask, and she has noticed that it is not a thing anybody would think to ask, and she has not decided what to make of that.
---

There are about nine people at the top of that bank and about four of them have said a sentence this winter, and about four of them have not said what a figure of a person's own is about, and about four of them have not asked.

She has heard it. Not the words — the words never carry past the first four feet of that bank — but the fact of it, which comes down the slope in about four voices at once about twice a week, and once, on one morning in this run, in about nine voices at once.

She did not go up and ask. There was nothing to ask with, and there is a second space on a form folded in four in a bag in that house and it has been blank every morning of that run, and she is a clerk and she writes down what goes on that page and not what goes on that bank.

That is the whole of it and she has never said it out loud to anybody either.

There is a worn place in that paper.

It is about four inches in from the foot of the page and about the width of two fingers and it has been there for the whole of the fifteen years she has been opening that book, and every line she has ever written across it has sat a little lower than the lines above it and a little further right, and she has never once tried to correct for it.

You can write a line over a worn place and you cannot make it sit level. She has tested that on other paper and it is not a matter of pressure. The paper has gone thin there and it takes the ink further down than it takes it anywhere else, and what a line does over that place is not run level but go slack.
---

The turner came along the boards about half past nine with his hand still warm off the frame and stopped about four feet off her.

"You have it open and shut three times this morning."

"I do that about forty times a day."

"Why."

"Because it is the same book forty times a day and it is the only habit I have got." She shut it again. "You have one habit. You have a wheel at the speed it goes at and you have the stove and you have the hand you put on the frame at the end of a day to stop it. If anybody took the wheel away from you this afternoon you would still put your hand on the frame at half past five."

"That is exactly what I would do."

"I know," said the clerk. "I have watched you do it for about four years and I have never once said so, and you have never once asked me what I do at that rail, and that is the arrangement and neither of us made it."

He went up the slope without saying anything else about it and she opened the book and shut it again.

At about half past nine she had the pen out for the first time that morning and did not use it.

She writes the morning at about ten past four. That is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement for fifteen years. She reads it out loud first and then she writes it and then she shuts the book, and between the reading and the writing there is that whole afternoon, which is the part of the day the pen belongs to.

She put the pen back in the holder.
---

A clerk of fifteen years knows what a yard sounds like at every hour of it.

At about half past six there is one stove door and a gate. At about eight there are boards going up and a wheel being banked. At about half past nine there is cloth. At about half past twelve there is a scale taking the wind.

At about half past one there is a number, and it goes up a bank and four voices take it out of step with one another and it comes back down, and after that the day has a hole in the middle of it about as wide as a man is tall.

She has heard that hole every working day of fifteen years and she has never once been in it.
---

There is one thing a clerk knows that nobody else in a yard knows, and it is the exact length of every hour in it.

She knows the hour by the sound and by the light and by who has stopped. She knows it at four in the morning in the winter when the boards are the only thing there is. She knows it at ten past four in the afternoon when a pen is about to go along a page.

Fifteen years of a yard does that to a person.

Nobody at the foot of that bank has ever noticed it, because there is no hour at which anything about it can be pointed at, and because asking her what she does with her day would be a question, and a question is the one shape of sentence that does not get put down in that yard and walked away from.

She has not been asked and she is not going to be and she has stopped waiting to be.

At about half past twelve she came along the boards and looked at that book lying open against the rail and she had the whole afternoon to think about one sentence and she did not get any further with it than she has got for some time.

The sentence is about four feet off the near end of the first table.

It has been in her since before the middle of the winter. It is short and it is a question and she has known for a good while that it is a question, and a question is the one shape of sentence that does not get put down in that yard and walked away from. There is nowhere in that yard for a person to put a question.

She has not put it down.

That is the whole of it. She is not going to put it down this morning either, and there is no part of her that thinks she might, and she has stopped being a person who expects to.
---

A clerk is a person who reads a morning out loud before she writes it, and there is one thing in that yard she has never had a form of words for.

She had it on the two hundred and fifteenth morning and she has had it since, and it is a question, and a question is the one shape of sentence that does not get put down in that yard and walked away from.

So she reads the morning, and she writes the morning, and she shuts the book on however many lines are on the page, and she goes home, and the question stays in her and has never once come out of her mouth in that yard.

She has not given up on it. She has simply stopped expecting it to come out on any particular morning, and there is a difference between those two things, and she knows exactly what the difference is because she has been a clerk for fifteen years and she has never once not known the difference between two things.

The man of about thirty-four came along the boards with his cloth at about one and stopped about four feet off her.

"Your corner is not square."

"It is not square and it is not going to be square and I have a piece of pallet that says so on the other end of it."

"A piece of pallet with one sound end is worth two hands."

"It is worth four feet and I am not a joiner."

"Aye," he said. "You said that on the bank and I have thought about it since."

Then he went along his own boards and she went back to the book.
---

At half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it back after him out of step with one another, and it came off that bank and went on up the hill.
---

At about half past three she stood at the boards with the pen in the holder and did the thing she does about four times a day, which is to open the book and shut it again and look at the back of the last page for about a second and a half.

That worn place is four inches in from the foot of it and about the width of two fingers, and there are a great many lines across it by now, and the more she looks at it the less she can tell herself why they go slack where the paper has worn thin. That is a thing about paper and she is a person who writes on it.

She shut the book and put the pen in her pocket and went along the boards to where she stops.
---

There is a form of words for a morning on which a reason was said out loud and a line went on the page for it, and there is a form of words for a morning on which nothing went out loud and no line went on the page, and she has both of them ready before she gets to the boards.

There is a third kind of morning and she has never had a form of words for it.

A figure goes out. Nobody answers it. Nobody thanks the person who said it. Nobody asks that person anything, or anything about it, or anything at all, and the page at the back of that lot book does not move, and at ten past four she stands at that rail with the pen in the holder and has got nothing at all to say about it.

That has happened. She has stood there with the pen in the holder and the book open against the rail and the light going off the boards and had nothing to read out.

The pen went in. The morning went down in her own hand with nothing in it. That is what she says.

She has never said the other thing, and there is nobody at the foot of that bank to say it to.
---

The boy was at the boards at ten to eight and said his number out loud before her hand had moved on anything, and then he stood there about four feet off her while she wrote nothing at all.

"You have not written it," he said.

"I have not."

"You said it out loud though."

"I said the morning out loud. That is different from writing it, and you know it is different, because you say your number out loud and nothing has ever come of it either."

The boy put his hands back in his pockets.

"That is the first thing anybody has said to me about the difference," he said.

"It is not advice," said the clerk. "I am a person who reads a morning out loud before she writes it and there is a reason for that which I have never given anybody and you are not getting it this morning."

"Fine," said Orren Pike, and went up the slope.

The man of thirty-eight was at the near end of his own table with the cloth over his arm and nothing came out of him that day, at about four feet off the near end of that table, from about half past nine until the light came off the boards, and not one person in that yard asked him anything at any point of it.
---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six had his hand up on the wall at the far end of that ground and came down with all four of the figures on it, and it lay flat over the lowest one afterwards.

She could hear it from the boards. That wall is a long way off and a man talking to it is not, and there is no part of that yard where she has not been able to hear that voice for fifteen years.

"Two hundred and thirteen mornings," said the man of fifty-six to that stone, "and I have got the same four off it and the four have got nothing to say to me."

About nineteen people heard that, and that was the two hundred and thirteenth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any of it.
---

At about ten past four she came along the boards with that book open against the rail and gave the whole of the account of that morning out loud, and the pen was near no part of it while she did that.

"The three hundred and twenty-fourth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."
---

She shut the book and put the pen back in the holder and stood at that rail for the length of about two of her own counts, and then she opened it again, which is a thing she does perhaps once in ten mornings.

She counted the lines on that back page without the pen near any part of it.

Sixty-two. That is not a sum and it is not a figure anybody in this district would call anything at all. It is a page in a book with lines on it, and there is no reason on earth for a woman to stand at a rail and look at them, and she has never told anybody in this place that she does.

The worn place in that paper is about four inches in from the foot of the page. Every line she has ever written across it sits a little lower and a little further right than the lines above it, and the last of them went across the worn place, and it did not sit level with the lines above it, and she left it where it was.

Then she wrote the three hundred and twenty-fourth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand.

It went over the worn place in that paper about four inches in from the foot of the page. It did not sit level with the lines above it and it sat a little lower and a little further right than the rest, and she left it where it was, the way she has left about two hundred and forty of them where they were.

The page carried sixty-two lines when the covers came over it and carried sixty-two lines before.
---

At about half past four the light left those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day there.

About four feet off the near end of the first table a man of thirty-eight put his cloth down on the corner of his own table and stood a moment with his hand on it, and went up that bank, and the light was still on the top of it when he got there, and about nine people at the top of that bank stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.
---

On the three hundred and twenty-fourth morning the man of thirty-eight was asked nothing and said nothing, and the page at the back of that lot book did not move and stands at sixty-two lines. That count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three lines out of the first thirty-three on that back page have never been read by anybody in this district. The fifth of the five is five and nothing has been paid for it. There are seven months counted and there is nothing whatever for them to be counted on. Four hundred and eleven is what the sheet at that gatepost carries and it was still carrying it when the boards came down.
