# Chapter 1048: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Pen In A Holder

That sheet has been on that gatepost since before the rail went in and nobody has ever been near it since, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-seven days is the age of that figure.

Nine hundred and forty-six is carried by that board. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-two days and no wagon has been taken off it. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy-six days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open and is not a lot. Six hundred and fifty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and fifty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty-five days is how far past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and twenty-nine days back. Six hundred and five marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and ninety-one marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months a clerk has counted in this district and there is nothing whatever for them to be counted on. Four documents are not owned here. Those three lines can be read in four places in this district. Four conditions have no end on it. There are fourteen things made here and five missing, and it is the fifth of the five that is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before the page at the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line was written on that page for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing has gone out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines from the first board up to the last one off. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-two mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and twelve mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-two mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirty-first morning and it is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and seventy-third of those mornings. That man was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-seventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-six of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and seventy-seventh afternoon running and one word is the whole of that afternoon. And that is the one hundred and eighty-eighth morning running with nobody having put a question to him. That flat answer is given to that stone twice a morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-first time running.

That lot book is open along those boards. There are five lines ruled across its first page, and on the second of them a column stands with one name in it under the heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty in front of everybody in this yard. The count against that lane is thirty, and that lane is as empty as it has been every morning of this run. That record has stood two hundred and forty-seven days. About nine people stand on that ground, which became public lot on the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man at the foot of that wall knows both of them and has never once said so: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Nobody in this district has ever had those three lines read out of the first thirty-three on that back page.
---

He was at the foot of that wall before half past seven, which was earlier than he had ever been, and the light had not come over the top of it.

He came down because there was something to see at about eight and had been coming down because there was something to see at about eight for a run of mornings now, and this morning he wanted the whole of it, from the first figure to the last, and the first figure came at a quarter to eight, so he wanted to be standing there before that.

He stood with his hands behind him and looked at the four figures cut into that stone about a foot apart and waited for the man to come and read them.

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six came along the strip of stone with his hand up and got all four of them off the wall before the light was on it, and said his four words to the stone, and nothing came back off the stone, which is what that wall does twice a morning and has done for longer than he has been standing at the foot of it.
---

The boy was at those boards at ten to eight and said his number out loud, and came down the slope at about ten and came along the whole length of that stone and stopped about four feet off him.

"I got down before you did," the boy said.

"You did not."

"I did. I was on that slope when the light was still on the boards."

The man of about sixty-four did not argue it, because the boy was wrong and because arguing it would have been a question, and he had not asked one this morning and he was not going to start.

"You have not asked me anything," he said.

"I have not."

"Two years you have been in this yard."

"Two years and about three weeks."

"Then you are the only person in it who has not," said the man of about sixty-four, "and I have been at the foot of this wall longer than you have been standing at those boards, and I have never been asked one thing, and I would not know what to do if I was."
---

A man of sixty-one came out and sat down on those stones about four feet off him with his hands on his knees, and said good afternoon to a man standing in the strip of light that had come over the top of that wall, and got one word back.

The boy sat down on the stones on the far side of the man of sixty-one and put his elbows on his knees.

"They were at a quarter to eight again."

"They were."

"You have watched that every morning since you started coming down early."

"Not every morning," said the man of about sixty-four. "I have watched it nine mornings and I have got four of them right in my head as they should be, and the other five I have watched the hand rather than the wall, and there is a difference between the two and I could not tell you what it is."

"Is it a good difference."

"I do not know what good is for," said the man of about sixty-four. "I used to read marks off a board for a living and a good mark was a mark you could swear to, and there is nothing on that stone you could swear to, and the hand goes up and comes down and gets all four, and that is the whole of what it does."

The boy did not ask him for more, because the boy had a rule about that man which he had never been given by anybody, and it was that the number of questions a person is asked is a thing you can see from four feet off.
---

He could not hear the boards.

That is the plain fact of where he stands. The boards are about four hundred yards off at the other end of that ground and the wind comes off that bank almost every afternoon of that run and takes whatever is said at them flat along the stones and out over the top of the yard.

He has read marks off boards for a long time. He read them off a tide board and then off a depth board on a stretch of water that has a different name now, and the whole of his trade was that a mark you could not see was a mark you did not swear to.

And he has worked out, without deciding to work it out, that he can tell how long a sentence is from the shape of a clerk's mouth at four hundred yards. A short one goes quick and does not open very far. A long one opens and stays open and comes down at the end in a way he can see from here.

He cannot tell a single word of it and he would not know what to do with one.
---

At about half past three the turner came through the gap and stopped about four feet off the wall with his hand off the frame.

"You are down earlier again."

"I am."

"Two mornings running you have been here before the light has got over the top of it."

"Three," said the man of about sixty-four. "This makes three, and it is about a week since the first one and the first one I did not tell anybody about and I did not tell you about this one either until you said it."

"I did not say it as a question."

"No," said the man of about sixty-four. "You do not do that. You have never once done that, and I have been watching you not do it for two years and about three weeks, and it is the only arrangement in this yard that has never once been broken by anybody, including you."

The turner went up the slope and said nothing else, which is what the turner does.
---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices said it back after him out of step with one another, and it went off that ground and came down the slope and passed over him and he could not have told you one word of it if he had been asked, which nobody was.
---

The afternoon went past the foot of that wall the way afternoons go past a wall. He did not walk. He has walked about four times in that yard in the whole of that run and he has never once walked anywhere in particular, and the not walking anywhere in particular is not a decision either. It is what a person does who came down to stand at the foot of a wall and has run out of things to stand there for.

At about three a man of about thirty-two came along that ground with his cloth over his arm and got as far as the wall and stopped and did not come the last four feet, which is where a man stops when he does not want to be four feet off a person who is standing still.

Then he went on up the slope to his own table and never said one word, and neither did the man at the foot of the wall, and that was about four hundred yards of quiet in the middle of that ground.

---

About four, from where he stood, a hand went up flat against that stone and the four figures came off it and were gone, and the hand came down over the place where the lowest one had been and stayed there.

"Two hundred and twenty-one mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and the best of the four is the one I have never once been asked to explain."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and twenty-first time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk read the morning out loud at those boards and gave the account of it out loud before she had the pen near any part of it.

"The three hundred and thirty-second morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."

Then she wrote the two hundred and thirty-second morning under the last line on that back page in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and shut the book, and that page was carrying sixty-two lines when the covers came over it.

Nobody in that yard heard one word of what she had said except the four people standing at the foot of the rail, and nobody has ever asked her to say it again and nobody has ever asked her why she says a morning out loud before she writes it.

He stood at the foot of that wall and did not move for about two minutes after that, and there was nobody within four feet of him to see that he had not moved.
---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and about four hundred yards off him the sound of it arrived as a shape in his ear and not as anything he could use.

The lane count was thirty and nothing came down that lane that day. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have gone through this district without anybody hearing them read. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted by a clerk and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
