# Chapter 1039: The Three Hundred And Twenty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And He Came Down To That Wall Before Half Past Seven

No hand in this district has ever touched that sheet, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and seventy-eight days is the age of that figure, and it was put up by somebody who has not come back.

There is nine hundred and thirty-seven on that board and it has been repainted twice over. One thousand two hundred and fifty-three days is how long that train has stood there, and a man comes and looks at it about twice a month. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and sixty-seven days and no name has gone into the column under the heading that still says whoever. Nine hundred and twenty-eight days have passed from the second of January to this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days, it was not run, and it is not going to be run. Six hundred and forty-two days is how far behind the figure on that second line the clerk's hand is. Six hundred and forty-seven days is how long since the rule in that yard was said out loud. The first day of the eighth month stands seven hundred and seventeen days back from these boards. Six hundred and fifty-six days past a printing it did not make is where that body is sitting. The ninth of the nine printed nights, eight hundred and twenty days back, has never been named.

The cuts in that board come to five hundred and ninety-six marks. Somebody has put five hundred and eighty-two chalk marks along the edge of that second table. The months were counted to seven and the count is on nothing whatever.

Four documents are wanted in this place and are not here. There are four places in this district where those three lines can be read. ---

He got down there before half past seven.

That is new. He has been coming to the foot of that wall at about nine every morning for longer than he can get a number out of his own head for, and the reason he came at about nine was that the light at about eight was not any good and there was nothing to see at about eight.

There is something to see at about eight. He had never once been here to find out.

---

The man of fifty-six was already at it.

He had his hand up on the stone and he got the first of them off the wall, and then the second, and then the third, and then the fourth, and he did not hurry any of them and he did not miss any of them, and between the second and the third he took his hand off the stone entirely and shook it out and put it back.

Then his hand came down and lay flat over the lowest one of the four.

---

The man of about sixty-four stood about nine feet off with his hands behind him and did not move for about as long as it takes to say one thing out loud to a stone.

He has been a man who read marks off a post for thirty-one years. A tide board, and then a depth board on a stretch of water that has a different name now, and the whole of that trade is going up to a thing and taking a number off it and saying the number out loud to whoever is standing there.

Nobody has ever asked him anything here. Not one word has gone down to him at the foot of that wall this winter, or the winter before, or out of that yard at all. He came and nobody said good morning to him and he said nothing about that and it has never once been a thing between him and anybody.

And he cannot ask, because asking would be a sentence with a person in the end of it, and he has watched that place a long time and he knows that a question does not get put down in that yard and walked away from.

---

The boy came down that slope about ten to eight, said his number out loud at those boards before anybody's hand had moved, and went back up the bank. He has done that every morning of that run and he does not say good morning to anybody.

At about eleven the boy came down again and stopped about four feet off him with his hands in his pockets.

"You were here before the light."

"I was."

"Before half past seven."

"A little."

The boy stood there. He is seventeen and he is the one person in that yard whose whole business is a figure, and he had come down that slope at eleven in the morning to ask a man of sixty-four a question and he could not get the end of it out.

"You come early now," the boy said at last.

"It is warmer on this side of the wall at that hour."

"That is not why."

"No," said the man of about sixty-four. "It is not."

The boy waited, the way boys wait when they have found out that they are not going to get what they came for.

"Nobody at those boards will tell me who fits that rail," he said at last.

The man of about sixty-four looked up the bank for a while and did not answer that, and he was not going to, and there is no version of this morning in which anybody asks him anything.

"It is a hard wall to read in that light," the boy said, instead, and went up the slope.

"That's all right," said Orren Pike, and went up the slope.

---

The boy was at those boards at ten to eight and said his number out loud before anybody's hand had moved, and then he came down the slope and stopped about four feet off the wall.

"You have been here every morning since before the light."

"Not since before the light."

"Twice this week."

"Twice this week," said the man of about sixty-four, "and not once in the whole of the time before that, and I would go back to nine if there were anything to go back to."

Orren Pike stood there about as long as a boy stands when he has been answered more honestly than he expected.

"Nobody has asked you anything, have they," he said.

"Nobody," said the man of about sixty-four. "Go and do your spaces."

The man of about thirty-two came along with his cloth at about nine and stopped about four feet off him.

"You are here early."

"I am."

"Before the light."

"Before the light," the man of about sixty-four said.

The man of about thirty-two stood there about as long as that conversation was going to be, and then he said, "It is a hard wall to read in that light, and that is the only useful thing anybody has said to me about it in three years," and went along his own boards with the cloth.

There was a man in that yard who had said a thing out loud twice in fifteen days and had got nothing back from either of them, and there was a man at the foot of that low wall who had said nothing out loud in longer than either of them could remember, and the two of them were about eleven feet from each other and neither of them knew the other one existed.

The man of about thirty-two came along with his cloth and stopped about four feet off him.

"You are here early."

"I am."

"Before the light."

"Before the light," the man of about sixty-four said.

The man of about thirty-two stood there about as long as a man stands when he has said a thing and is not going to be asked anything more about it, and then he said, "It is a hard wall to read in that light," which is the only useful thing anybody has said to him about it, and went along his own boards with the cloth.

---

What he wanted out of that morning, and it is a small thing and he wanted it before the light was on the boards, was to be in that yard at about eight in the morning when those four get read off that wall by the only man who has ever read them.

He had got that.

He had got it and it had taken him the whole of eleven minutes and he had seen all four of them read off and put a hand flat over the lowest one, and about half past twelve he began to understand that he was going to go home that evening with exactly what he came down there for.

---

A man of about sixty-four gets up at about six and is at that wall by about seven, and there are three hours of that day in which nothing whatever happens within nine feet of him.

He does not go up the bank in them. He has never gone up that bank in the morning and there is a whole morning of that yard he has never seen from above.

That is not a complaint. He is not a man who is waiting for something, because he has never once been asked a question in his life that he has not answered, and there has not been one since before the middle of the winter.

What he has instead is a wall with four figures cut into it and a man who reads them twice a day, and about three hours a day in which the only thing that happens is the light coming across four feet of stones.

---

A man came along about eleven with a barrow and stopped at the gap and looked down the slope and did not go through it.

"You could get a cart down there in another hour," he said, to nobody.

Nobody answered him and he went on up the bank, which is the ordinary way a thing that is said at the top of eleven feet behaves.

About nine people are up there in the afternoon and about four of them have said a sentence this winter, and about four of those four have not said what a figure of a person's own is about. A fifth one about a cart is not a sentence of that kind, and it went the way the other four went, and it will not be repeated at supper on any bank in this district.

At about half past twelve she came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and worked there all afternoon.

At half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put that number of his up into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people said it back after him out of step with one another, and it came off the top of that bank and up the hill.

Nothing else came out into that yard that afternoon, and about nine people in that yard could have told you so, and he was one of them and he is not a man who says so.

---

At about half past three the sun came over the top of that low wall and laid a strip of it down along the stones at the foot of it.

He said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on those cold stones.

"Aye," said the man of sixty-one.

That was the whole of the word and it was the whole of what was said between them, and neither of them said anything else, and neither of them has ever said anything else to the other.

---

Then the man of fifty-six came across to his own wall.

He came at about four, the way he comes, and he put his hand up on the stone and got the first of them off, and then the second, and then the third, and then the fourth. He got all four of them both times, the same four, in the same order, and there was nothing in his face at all while he did it.

Then his hand came down.

---

It stopped about an inch short of the stone.

The man of about sixty-four, standing about nine feet off in the strip of light that had come over the wall, saw it happen and did not understand it for about the length of time it takes to count to three.

He had watched that hand go down onto that stone for longer than he had been coming down here at nine. It went down flat and it went down over the lowest of those four figures and it lay there about as long as a man lets a thing lie, and every single time it touched stone.

This morning it stopped about an inch short of the stone and hung there in the air for the length of a breath, and then it went the rest of the way down and came flat on the stone with the sound a hand makes.

He put his hand there afterwards.

"Two hundred and twelve mornings of my hand on this stone and it has never once told me what it is for," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard it, and nobody asked him one question about any of it, and he has been asked nothing this morning or on any morning before it.

---

A man who used to read a board for a living does not stop doing it when there is nothing on the board.

That was thirty-one years of a tide board and then a depth board, and the whole of that trade is going up to a mark in the afternoon and taking a number off it out loud for whoever is standing there. He has been at the foot of that wall for a long time and about nine feet of him stands between that man and that habit every morning of it.

What he does instead is watch a hand.

It is not a good thing to watch and it is not a sad thing. It is nine feet of cold stone and a man putting a hand up on a wall twice a day and taking it down again, and a man of sixty-one who has sat at the other end of that strip for long enough to know what a hand does.

The man of about sixty-four stood in that strip of light for another four or five minutes.

Here is what he had. A man had read four figures off a wall and got all four of them, twice, the same four in the same order, and had put his hand flat over the lowest of them, and on this morning his hand had stopped about an inch short of the stone on the way down and then gone the rest of the way.

That is all. There is no more in it than that, and he had a trade of thirty-one years for reading what a mark is doing and it did not tell him anything.

A hand that stops an inch short is a shoulder. A shoulder that has got smaller over some years, or an arm that has got longer, or a man who has begun to reach that wall differently without any idea that he has, and there is no version of it that is a message and there is no version of it that is nothing.

He had a thing to say to somebody and there was nobody in that yard to say it to, and nobody at the top of eleven feet had said one word to him about it, and there had never once been a morning when anybody in that place said anything to him at all.

---

The turner came past the foot of that wall at about ten past four on his way to bank the stove and stopped for the length of a sentence.

"You were here before the light again yesterday."

"I was."

"You have been twice this week. You have not been in the morning in the whole time I have known you." He put his hand on the cold stone. "I am not asking you why. I am telling you that I have noticed, which is a different thing and the only one of the two I am allowed to do."

"Noticed for what."

"For nothing," said the turner. "I notice about nine things in that yard a day that nobody has asked me about. It is what a wheel is for."

Then he went up the bank and the man of about sixty-four stood at that wall for another quarter of an hour in the dark.

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards and gave that morning out loud before the pen was near any part of it, and wrote the three hundred and twenty-third morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.
