# Chapter 1026: The Three Hundred And Tenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Woman Of About Fifty-Two Stood In About Four Feet Of Sun With Nothing In Her Hands And Nobody Said One Word To Her And Nobody Said Good Morning To Her, And The Second Space On That Form Was Empty The Whole Of That Morning

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and there is not one thing on any page in this tree that says how wrong it is or by how much. Seven hundred and sixty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and twenty-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and forty days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and fifty-four days. Nine hundred and fifteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not open. Six hundred and twenty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book the work in this yard has got. Six hundred and thirty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and forty-three days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seven days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and eighty-three marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and sixty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing at all. Four documents are not owned here. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen things is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go until the two hundred and eighth morning, and no line on that page was written for a reason before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud in that yard this morning by anybody, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-one lines and carried sixty-one lines when she shut it. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and ninety mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and ten mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and ninth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fifty-first of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and sixty-fifth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and sixty-four of them, and this is the one hundred and sixty-sixth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninety-ninth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries sixty-one lines. The record has stood two hundred and twenty-five days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fifty-fifth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not going to be paid a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

Thirty, and there is no other figure coming this morning.

He says that second part out loud as well, because a number on its own at those boards leaves about four people standing about nine feet off him waiting for the rest of it, and he has found out the hard way that the rest of it has to be said.

Then he says there is no figure after it either, which is what he says on the mornings there is no figure.

---

There are nine spaces between the boards in front of him where he sits with his elbows on the rail.

There were nine of them when the water in that ditch had no light on it and there were nine of them when the light came over the top of that bank and got on the wall behind the boards, and it has been nine every morning of his being at that rail, which is the only number he owns.

That is his whole work at ten to eight. One number that does not move, said out loud to about four people who are waiting for it, in front of a gap in a rail.

---

His name is Orren Pike and he is seventeen and there are about nineteen people in that yard and he is the one who sits at the place where the counting gets done.

He has had some years to notice that those two arrangements have nothing to do with each other. A wheel turns at one end of that ground all day. Three men carry a table a foot along its own boards at nine. A woman comes down a slope at half past twelve with a basket on her arm and puts a bar on a scale and does that all afternoon. And the one thing he is put there for is a number that has not moved in longer than he has been saying it.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down that bank and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and worked there all afternoon.

He watched her set up from where he sits, which is the way he watches her set up, with his elbows on that rail and without looking away once.

---

At about one o'clock the woman of about fifty-two came down that slope and stopped about ten paces off the near end of the second table and put her bag down on the hard ground at her feet.

She had nothing in her hands. That is what he noticed first, because the morning before last she came down that hill with a frame under one arm and a bag in the other hand, and the morning before that she came down with her hands empty on purpose.

This morning she came down with a bag and she set the bag down and she stood there with her hands at her sides.

---

Nobody said one word to her.

There was a rail between him and her and about nine feet of hard ground, and he said nothing to her, and he has said nothing to her on every morning she has come down to that end of the second table, and so has every other person in that yard.

Nobody said good morning to her. The man of about thirty-two went past her with his cloth. The man at the near end of that second table had his boot on a wedge and lifted it and put it back. The wheel went on turning at the near end of that ground.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it back after him out of step with one another, and it came off the top of that bank.

He had a pencil in the pocket of his coat that morning and he did not take it out.

---

About four feet of sun came across that hard ground at about two and lay down along where she was standing, and she stood in it with her hands at her sides and the bag at her feet.

The sun came up over that bank and made a strip about the width of a table and put her in the middle of it. She did not move out of it while it was there and she did not go and get anything.

---

At about half past two she took the form out of the bag.

He could not read it at nine feet and he did not try. She opened it out on her knee with the corner held down by the bag and looked down at it for a while, and she put a stub of pencil back in the fold of it, and she folded the form in four and put it in the bag and stood up in the sun again with her hands at her sides.

That is the whole of what she did with it that morning.

---

There is a space on that form that has been empty every time he has seen anybody hold it.

He does not know what is on a form and he could not tell you what a form is for, and there is nobody in that yard he would ask, and it is not his business and it is not anybody's business at that rail.

He knows this much about it. A woman carried a thing down that slope and opened it in about four feet of sun and looked at it for as long as she wanted to, and then she folded it in four and put it back in the bag and stood there with nothing in her hands again, and about nineteen people watched her do that and not one of them said a word.

---

About half past three the light came over the top of the low wall and found the stones at the foot of it, and the man standing in that strip of it said good afternoon across about four feet to the man of sixty-one sitting on them, and the man of sixty-one gave him the one word back.

---

The light was off the top of that bank by four and he was still at that wall, because he always is, and at about four o'clock his hand went up onto the stone and came down with all four of those figures on it, and then it went flat under the lowest one and stayed there.

"There is a number on me that has never once been put down anywhere and I have said it out loud every morning of that run," he said to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and ninety-ninth time running and nobody asked him anything about any of it.

---

At about ten past four she was at the boards with the book in her hand, stopped where she stops, giving that morning out loud before any of it was written down.

"The three hundred and tenth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so the morning goes into my own hand with no line in it, and that is the whole of the account and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and tenth morning under the last line in her own small hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty-one lines when she shut the book and carried sixty-one lines after.

---

When the light went off those boards at about half past four, about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He took his elbows off that rail and wrote nothing down, and the second space on the form that the woman of about fifty-two carried down that slope in the morning was empty all morning and is empty now, and nobody in this district has asked her to put anything in it.

---

The woman of about fifty-two stood in about four feet of sun with nothing in her hands on the three hundred and tenth morning and nobody said one word to her and nobody said good morning to her, and the second space on that form was empty the whole of that morning. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at sixty-one lines. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and its size of error is uncomputed, unexplained and unpaid.
