# Chapter 1045: The Three Hundred And Twenty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And One Figure Of His Own

Nothing has ever been put on that sheet by anybody who knew what it ought to say, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-four days is the age of that figure and it has been the age of it for all of them.

That board carries nine hundred and forty-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fifty-nine days with a wagon on it that nobody has looked at. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy-three days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty-four days lie between the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid was open and it was not run and it is not open now and is not a lot. Six hundred and forty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this ground stands. Six hundred and fifty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty-two days is how far past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and twenty-six days back. Six hundred and two marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and eighty-eight marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months a clerk has counted in this district and there is no list for them anywhere. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what has been made here and five is what was never got, and the fifth of that five owes.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and that was before there was a page at the back of that lot book for a reason to go on, and no line was written there for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because before that morning it could not hold one, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, and the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines all day. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifty-nine mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and nine mornings and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-nine mornings, entered on the word of the man who keeps it and out in the air before it is anywhere else. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and twenty-eighth morning and it is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and seventieth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-fourth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-three of the nights of it. For the two hundred and seventy-fourth afternoon running a man of sixty-one sat on those stones and gives that afternoon one word. And this is the one hundred and eighty-fifth morning running with nobody having asked that man anything. The flat answer that man gives to that stone was given for the two hundred and eighteenth time running today.

That lot book lies open along those boards and nothing has gone into it. Five lines are ruled across the first of its pages and the column on the second of them has one name in it under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has ever been asked to fill it, including by naming it. Thirty is the lane count and that lane brought nothing down on that morning. That record has stood two hundred and forty-four days. There are about nine people on that ground, and it has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

The yard takes one of those and the hill takes the other: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and nobody is going near it. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have never once been read to anybody in this place.
---

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall about four hundred yards off and got all four of them off it before the light was on the stone, and said his four words to it, and nothing came back off that wall. The man of thirty-eight did not hear a word of it and has never once tried to hear it, and that is not a thing anybody in that yard has ever said out loud to anybody.

He came down off that bank at about half past nine with his hands showing, and instead of going out along the top of his own table from the north end the way he has gone along it for nine years, he came in at the corner he always comes in at and started at the near end.

The near end is the end that is in the shade until about eleven. He has started there perhaps twice in nine years and both times there had been a reason about the light.

He did the near end anyway.

---

The low place in his own top is about the width of his hand and about a sixteenth down and it sits about a foot along from the near corner, and he has known about it since before the middle of the winter, and a man of about thirty-four named it out loud in that yard and was told not to lay anything in it.

He went round it with the cloth on every pass this morning and he did not put anything in it and he did not take anything out of it, and he worked about a foot and a half of that top four separate ways because there are four ways to do a foot and a half of table and he knows all of them.

---

The man of about thirty-four came along his own boards about ten with his cloth and stopped about four feet off him.

"You have started at the near end."

"I have."

"It is dark at this end until eleven."

"It is," said the man of thirty-eight, and went along his own top and round the end of it and up the slope and back down and in at the same corner.

He had said nothing else. Nobody asked him anything else, and there was not one person in that yard who had anything to put in front of him that morning, which is how that yard works and how it has worked for as long as anybody in it can remember.
---

At about eleven the light came over the top of that low wall and up the face of that bank and found the boards, and the eleven feet between that wall and the top of the bank went from grey to the colour of a wet slate, and about nine people at the top of that bank stood in it and looked down at that yard for about a minute and a half and then went on standing there.

He got the sun on the north end about a quarter past eleven and worked it, and it was the same work it is every morning, and he can do the whole length of that table without looking at any part of it.

---

At about half past twelve she came down that bank and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table with her basket on her arm and put a bar on her own pan.

She stayed there the whole of that afternoon and said nothing out loud at any point of it, and the wind was on that pan from about one and it did not drop and it did not rise.

About four feet off the near end of the first table he came past her twice before the light moved off the boards and made no sound at either pass, the whole of that round makes no sound at all, and he did not stop and he did not look over.
---

He was at the corner he comes in at with the cloth in his hand, and he came in at it a minute after he had been in it.

A figure of his own came out of him at about a quarter to twelve.

Once. In the ordinary voice, at the speed a man uses for a thing he means.

It went out over that yard and off the face of that bank and about four hundred yards of air took it, and nothing whatever came back out of that yard at him.

Nobody thanked him. Nobody asked him what it was about, or what it was, or anything at all, and nobody at the top of eleven feet called down, and nobody at those boards turned round on the rail, and the woman at her own scale about four feet off the near end of the second table went on with her own pan.

Then that yard carried on with the rest of its morning and his own top went on the way his own top goes.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices said it back after him out of step with one another, and it went up that bank and came off it and went on up the hill.
---

The turner came along the boards about one with his hand off the frame and stopped about four feet off the near end of that table.

"You are doing that round of yours the other way round."

"I have started at the near end."

"Not the round. The round is the same. You are coming back to the corner with the cloth in the other hand."

The man of thirty-eight looked at his own hand and put the cloth back the way it goes.

"Thank you," he said, and went round the end of his own table.

The turner went up the slope and did not say anything else about it, and nobody asked the man of thirty-eight anything else either.
---

The man of about thirty-four came along his own boards about one and a half with the cloth and stopped about four feet off him.

"That pallet is still in your coat."

"It is."

"Four feet along from me that corner is out again."

"It is."

"And you have not laid it in."

"No."

"Then why are you telling me how it went last night."

The man of thirty-eight put the cloth down flat on his own top for about as long as it takes to say nothing, and picked it up again.

"Because you are the only man in that yard who has said a thing to me about a table without asking me a question about anything else," he said, and went round the end of his own table.

That is the whole of what was said. He did not put anything else in that air about it and the man of about thirty-four did not ask him anything, because there was nothing to ask him, and both of them understood that there was nothing to ask him, and that is the arrangement that two working men in that yard have without ever once making it.

---

The boy was down off the slope about two with the pencil out and the cap off and he did not come to the near end of that table. He stopped about nine feet off it, which is further than he came yesterday, and stood there for about two minutes and then went back up.

He has two years and about three weeks in that yard and he has never once been told he could not stand where he was standing, and he has got the measure of the ground well enough to know where the edge of it is.

The man of thirty-eight saw him do that and worked the middle of his own top for a while without looking up, and then, at about half past three, he did the low place in his own top with his bare hand instead of the cloth, once, and put the cloth back on the table and went round the end of it.
---

At about half past three a man of about fifty-two came down that bank with a frame under her arm and stopped about nine feet off the near end of that table, which is as close as she has ever come to it, and looked along the length of his own top the way a person looks along a thing they are not going to touch.

She did not ask him anything and he did not stop for her.

"You have started at that end this morning," she said.

"I have."

"Nine years on a table and you have started at the near end in the shade until eleven."

"It is the same table."

"It is," she said, "and you are the same man, and I have come down nine feet off you to say that I noticed which end you began at, and that is the whole of what I came down for."

He went along his own top and round the end of it without answering that, which she had not expected him to do.

About half a minute later she went back up the bank, and about half a minute after that a boy of seventeen came down it and stood about nine feet off where she had been and did not go any nearer.

Nobody at the foot of that bank has worked out why those two do that, and nobody in that yard has asked either of them, and a person who works it out from four feet off would not be seeing the same thing they are seeing.

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"Two hundred and eighteen mornings," he said to that stone. "And I have never once had anybody stand still while I read them."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and eighteenth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk was at those boards with that book open against the rail and the pen in the holder, and she gave the whole of that morning out loud before she put it near any part of it.

"The three hundred and twenty-ninth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and twenty-ninth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and that page was carrying sixty-two lines when the covers came over it.
---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard.

One figure of his own went out into that yard at about a quarter to twelve on the three hundred and twenty-ninth morning, once, in the ordinary voice, and nothing came back out of that yard at him, nobody thanked him and nobody asked him anything. The woman who keeps a scale was at her own scale about four feet off the near end of that table all day and said nothing out loud at any point of it. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at sixty-two lines. The lane count was thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have never been read in this district. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
