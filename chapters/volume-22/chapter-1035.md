# Chapter 1035: The Three Hundred And Nineteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Wheel Stopped

It has hung on that board through every season anybody here can remember, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and seventy-four days is the age of that figure.

Nine hundred and thirty-three is the figure on that board and nothing is entered on it. That siding has had the same thing standing on it for one thousand two hundred and forty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and sixty-three days. Nine hundred and twenty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, no lot came of it, and it is not open. The figure on the second line of that book is six hundred and thirty-eight days ahead of this yard. That rule was said out loud in that yard six hundred and forty-three days ago. It has been seven hundred and thirteen days since the first day of the eighth month. That body is four hundred miles off and six hundred and fifty-two days past a printing it did not make. The ninth of those nine printed nights is eight hundred and sixteen days back.

Five hundred and ninety-two marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and seventy-eight marks in chalk are on the edge of that second table. Seven months is what a clerk got and it goes nowhere.

No copy of four of those documents exists in this place. Four places can read those three lines and three of them are on this ground. Four conditions with no end on them are what this place goes by. Fourteen is what has been made and five is what was never had here, and the fifth of the five has not been paid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and it was before the page at the back of that lot book could take one.

Not one reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines and carried sixty-two lines when she shut the book. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has been standing at the foot of that bank for three hundred and forty-nine mornings, and the near rail has been on the near side of it for two hundred and ninety-nine mornings. A man has stood at the foot of that bank for three hundred and nineteen mornings and the entry follows him and the number goes up into the air first every single time. The man of about thirty-two has had his table up at that end of the ground for the three hundred and eighteenth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read the four off that stone on the seven hundred and sixtieth of the mornings he has stood there and nobody has ever asked him a question about any of it. A man of about sixty-four was at that low wall on the six hundred and seventy-fourth night of that run, and six hundred and seventy-three of those nights went by with him asleep on them. A man of sixty-one has been on those stones for the two hundred and sixty-fourth afternoon running and he says one word and nothing else. And that is the one hundred and seventy-fifth mornings running without one question from anybody. His flat answer is the two hundred and eighth time running.

The lot book is open where it is put every morning of that run. It carries five lines and a column holding one name, and the heading above that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form stays empty and nobody is ever asked about it. The lane count is thirty and it stands there in the morning whatever the day does. The record stands two hundred and thirty-four days. That ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and about nine people are standing on it. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and that wall stands about four miles off the culvert at the top of it. The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not going to be paid again. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have never been read to anybody here.
---

He lights his stove off the ground on one knee at about a quarter to eight and it takes him about as long as it has ever taken him, and that wheel goes at the speed it goes at through the whole of that morning without slowing once.
---

A wheel that turns at one speed all day is a thing you can hear the length of.

That is what he has got instead of a clock. He does not need to know the hour to know where in that day he is. He knows where he is by how the light has got under the frame and by which of the nine things in that yard has stopped making its noise, and by how the boards under him sound when a man walks out along the top of one.

He came at that yard thirty-one years ago on a cart with a different wheel and he has never once asked anybody here what a wheel is for.
---

The man of about thirty-two came along with his cloth at about half past eight and got his own table up before the light got on it, and stopped at the end of it.

"You are still on that frame."

"I am on the frame it came on."

"You could put a new one under it and nobody would say anything to you about it."

"You have been at that table three years and you have said that sentence to yourself about four hundred mornings," the turner said. "You have never said it out loud to me and I am not going to help you do it."

The man of about thirty-two stood there and then went along his own top with the cloth.
---

Here is the thing he had in him from about ten.

It is one sentence and it has been in him since the winter before last, and it is about two things that go into the same place every morning of that run and do not come out of it in the same shape, and he can see both of them from where he stands and has never had any business joining them.

A figure gets said out loud across that ground. A line gets written on a page at the back of a book lying open on those boards.

Both of them go into that yard. Neither of them comes back out of it. The figure is out loud for about a second and then there is no part of that ground holding it. The line goes into a book that is shut at half past four and opened again in the morning, and it is still in there, and nobody in this district has ever had a line of it read to them.

He had got as far as the front of that about four hundred times. What stopped him every time was the verb in the middle of it. Any verb he put there made a claim. There was no verb available to him that joined those two things without also saying they were the same kind of thing, and he did not know whether they were the same kind of thing and he had no way of finding out and he was not going to guess in the middle of a morning with about nineteen people standing in it.
---

At about half past eleven he stopped turning.

He stopped the wheel with his heel on the frame and that is the only way it has ever been stopped and that is the only reason he can do it without anybody looking up.

"A figure said across this yard and a line written on that page are two different things going into the same place," he said, in the ordinary voice, at the speed a man uses for a thing he means.

Then he took his heel off and the wheel went on at the speed it goes at.

That was the whole of it. He did not say what made them different and he did not say what made them go into the same place, and he put his hand back on the frame, and about nine people had heard it and about four of them had not looked up.
---

The man of about thirty-two came along about ten past twelve and stopped and looked at the wheel and then at him, which is a thing nobody else in that yard does.

"You stopped it."

"I did."

"I have three years at that table and I have never known you could."

"You have never needed to know it."

The man of about thirty-two stood there with the cloth in his hands.

"That is a different thing from not knowing," he said.

"Aye," said the turner. "It is. Somebody else stopped it this morning."

"Nobody else in this yard touches that wheel."

"No," said the turner, and put his hand back on the frame, and that was the end of that.

Nobody answered him.

Nobody asked him what he meant by different and nobody asked him what he meant by the same place, and the man of about thirty-four went on along his own top about four feet off him, and the woman at the scale about four feet off the near end of the second table worked her own pan, and the man at the end of the second table kept his head over his bench, and the light went on doing what the light does.
---

It is worth being plain about what a wheel is.

It is a wheel. It goes round because something turned it, and it goes round at the speed it goes at because that is the only speed anybody has ever asked of it, and it has been asked that by four men in thirty-one years and by nobody else.

A man who has said nothing out loud in a yard for thirty-one years does not have a great deal of practice at the moment of saying a thing. What he has is a wheel, and a wheel gives you a place to put your hands while the inside of your chest finishes getting ready.

He said one thing out loud in that yard for the first time in thirty-one years and about four hundred yards of air took it and the yard carried on with the middle of its morning.
---

The man of about thirty-four came along at about half past one and stood at the end of his own table and looked at the wheel for a while without saying anything.

"You stopped it."

"I stopped it."

"I have never seen anybody stop that wheel except at night." He put his cloth over his shoulder. "There is no reason to stop it in the middle of a morning."

"There is a thing I said this morning."

"Did I hear it."

"You did not and nobody did and that is what I am telling you." He put his hand back on the frame. "You are the only person in this yard who would have noticed, and you did not notice, and I would have liked it if you had noticed and I am not going to pretend otherwise."

The man of about thirty-four stood there about as long as a man stands when he has nothing to put in that, and then he said, "It is a hard wheel to stop and a hard wheel to start," and went along his own boards with the cloth.

What he found out afterwards, at about two, is that the wheel does not know.

He had expected — he did not know what he had expected, and that is nearly the same thing — he had expected to have to take the rest of that morning differently. He got his hand back on the frame and the wheel turned and the sun came off the top of that bank onto the boards at about the hour it comes onto the boards, and everything about his hands was the same as it had been at about eleven.

He stood there with his hand on that frame for about a minute trying to find the place where a thing you have said out loud once sits afterwards, and there was no place. It had not gone anywhere and it had not stopped anything, and there was not one person in that yard who altered what he was doing by a hair because of it.

The only thing that had moved was that it was out of him.
---

At half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it back after him out of step with one another, and it came off the top of that bank and up the hill.
---

The man of about sixty-one on the stones and the man of about sixty-four at the foot of that low wall are about four feet apart and have been for the length of that run of afternoons, and neither of them says more than one word to the other.

A man at a wheel nine feet off can hear a stone move.

He has heard the coat folded on the stones about once an hour all afternoon. He knows the sound of a folded coat being moved and picked at and moved again, and he knows the sound of a man putting his weight on one heel and taking it off, and he has never once been able to work out which of those two men makes more of the noise.

That is the whole of what he has instead of a conversation, and he has had it for about nine years, and it is more than a great many people get.

She weighed four more things before the light went off that bank and not one figure of hers went out into that yard that afternoon.
---

A little after half past three the sun came over the top of that low wall and put a strip of light down along the stones, and a man standing in that strip said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him, and one word came back.
---

At about four o'clock that man's hand was up on the wall and came down with all four of the figures on it, and it lay flat over the lowest one afterwards the way it lies every afternoon of that run.

"Two hundred and eight mornings and this stone has not asked me one question either," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard it, and that was the two hundred and eighth time running, and nobody has asked him anything about any of it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk stopped where she stops with the book open against the rail and gave that morning out loud, and the pen was still in the holder while she did it.

"The three hundred and nineteenth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand on its own, and that is the account of it and it went out loud before a single mark was made on it."

She wrote the three hundred and nineteenth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.
---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard.

He banked the stove off the ground on one knee and it took about as long as it takes, and he put his hand on the frame on the way past it, and the wheel was stopped, and he has stopped it by hand every night for thirty-one years.

The turner said one thing out loud on the three hundred and nineteenth morning, about a figure said across that yard and a line written on a page, and he did not join the two of them, and nobody answered him. That page stands at sixty-two lines and did not take a line. Thirty is what that rail carries. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have still never been read to anybody here. There are five things this place does not have and nobody has settled the fifth of them. Seven months sits on nothing at all. The figure on that sheet at the gatepost is four hundred and eleven.
