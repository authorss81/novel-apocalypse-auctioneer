# Chapter 1043: The Three Hundred And Twenty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Four Feet Apart

That sheet has been on that gatepost since before the rail went in and nothing has ever been done to it since, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty-two days is the age of that figure and it will be here when the cloth is off those boards.

Nine hundred and forty-one is what that board carries. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fifty-seven days without a sound off it. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventy-one days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty-two days lie between the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open, and it was never run, and it is not open and it is not a lot and nothing in this district has opened it since. Six hundred and forty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and fifty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has been standing. Seven hundred and twenty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty days past a printing it did not make is where a body four hundred miles off has got to.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and twenty-four days back. Six hundred marks have been cut off that board and there is no end on that figure either. Five hundred and eighty-six marks in chalk are along the edge of that second table.

Seven months a clerk has counted and there is nothing at all for them to be counted on. Four documents this district has never had hold of. There are four places in this district that have ever had a reader for those three lines. Four conditions are worked under in this place and no end is set on any of them. Fourteen things have been made and five were never got, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before the page at the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line was written on that page for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because before that morning it was no such place, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nothing has gone out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, and that page stood at sixty-two lines when the light went off those boards. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifty-seven mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and seven mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-seven mornings on the word of the man who keeps it, and that number goes into about four hundred yards of air before it goes anywhere else. The table of the man of about thirty-two has been up at that end of that ground for the three hundred and twenty-sixth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and sixty-eighth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at that low wall on the six hundred and eighty-second night of his run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-one of them. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and seventy-second afternoon running and gives that afternoon one word. And it is the one hundred and eighty-third morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone for the two hundred and sixteenth time running today.

That lot book has been open along those boards all morning with nothing entered in it. Five lines are ruled across its first page and the column on the second has one name in it under a heading which still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty in front of everybody in this yard and nobody has ever been asked to fill it, including by naming it. The lane count is thirty and no page has come down that lane. That record has stood two hundred and forty-two days. About nine people are on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. Both of those figures have never been measured by anybody in this district: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have never been read by anybody in this district.
---

Ten to eight, and the boards, and the boy.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and no figure behind it."

He stood about four feet off the rail in the cold and waited, which he does every morning, and the clerk opened that book against the rail and shut it again and did not look up.
---

The marks cut off that board are a thing she has looked at about four times a year for fifteen years.

They are not on the lot book. They are not on the page at the back of it. They are cut into a board at the far end of that ground, one mark at a time, by somebody who does not come at a time she is ever there, and every one of them is a day and none of them is a morning, and the last of them was cut about a week ago and was still bright in the grain when the light came over the top of that wall at about eight.

She has never counted them to the end.

That is not a decision. It is the way fifteen years of not being asked has left a person, and she knows it is that, and she has never done anything about it.

---

At about half past one two figures went out into that yard.

The first came off the woman who keeps a scale at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice. The second came off the man of thirty-eight at about four feet off the near end of the first table, once, in the ordinary voice, and it came out in the same minute as the first one and the two of them were about four feet apart.

About nine people heard both of them.

Neither figure brought one thing back out of that yard. Nobody thanked either of them. Nobody asked either of them anything, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line for either of them.

The clerk had the pen out of the holder at the time and did not use it.
---

She put the pen back and shut the book and stood at the rail and did the thing she does about four times a day, which is to open it and shut it again.

That afternoon went past her the way her afternoons go.

At about two the boy came back down the slope and stood about four feet off the rail with the cap off his pencil and did not put anything on anything.

"You read that morning out loud on the boards before your hand was near it."

"I read every morning out loud before my hand is near it."

"You have never read one out loud that had two figures in it."

"No," said the clerk. "That is the third kind of morning and I have not got a form of words for the third kind of morning, and I have been getting by for about a fortnight on the form of words for a morning when nothing went out at all, and this morning it did not be nothing."

"Then what did you read."

"I read the morning, and I said that nothing had gone out loud in this yard that goes at the back of that book, and then I said the rest of it, and then I wrote the morning with nothing in it. I did not say one word to you or to anybody else about the two figures. You can put that down or you can not."

The boy put the cap back on the pencil.

"I was never going to write anything down."

"I know," said the clerk. "You never do."
---

At about two she had a bar on her own pan about four feet off the near end of the second table and the wind wanted it, and she let it want it until it came still, which is the whole of what she does with a bar. At about four feet off the near end of the first table a man of thirty-eight went past her twice with the cloth in his hand and made no sound at either pass, and about a dozen feet off him the man of about thirty-four went along his own top and round the end of it with a foot and a bit of pallet still lying where he had put it on the boards, and neither of them came to that rail.

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four of them off it before the light was on it and said his four words to the stone, and nothing came back off that wall, which is what that wall does twice a morning.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and the clerk had the pen out of the holder at the time and did not use it.

---

At about half past three the turner came through the gap with his hand still warm off the frame and stopped about four feet off her.

"You had it out."

"I have it out about four times a day."

"You had it out at half past one and you had it out at ten to three and you had it out a minute ago," said the turner, "and it is not my business what you do with a pen."

"It is not," she agreed. "It is nobody's business what I do with a pen, and I have found in fifteen years that the things nobody's business are the only things I get to keep."

The turner went up the slope and did not say anything else about it, which is what the turner does.
---

The man of about thirty-two came along his own boards about three with the cloth over his arm and stopped where he stops.

"What is the state of it."

"Two figures and no line."

"Then what have you got to read out at four."

"Nothing that has changed since yesterday," said the clerk. "I read the morning and I say that nothing has gone out loud in this yard that goes at the back of that book, and then I write the morning with nothing in it, and that is the third kind of morning and I have not got a form of words for it, and I have had about as long as you have had your table up there."

"You will get one."

"Will I," said the clerk, "or is that the sort of thing a person says at three o'clock in the afternoon to somebody who has to say something out loud at four."
---

At about half past three the clerk walked as far as the near end of the first table and back again, which is about as much as she walks in a morning.

A man of thirty-eight went along his own top and past her and round the end of it and up the slope and back down, and made no sound at any point of it, and she stood where she was until he had gone and then went back to the rail.

She has done that perhaps once a fortnight for fifteen years. There is a worn place in that paper she has never corrected for, and a morning she has no form of words for, and a boy at those boards who says a count out loud every morning and a figure nobody has ever asked her about. That is the whole of what a clerk has got to walk about with.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four of them off it in order, and his hand came down flat over the lowest one and stayed there.

"Two hundred and sixteen mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and there is not one of them in which I have got a reason out of a person instead of a number."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and sixteenth time running, and nobody has ever asked him a single thing about it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk was still standing where she had been standing at half past one with the pen out of the holder and not in her hand, and she gave that morning out loud against the rail before she put the pen near any part of it.

"The three hundred and twenty-seventh morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the pen was near the page."

The man of about thirty-two put his head round a corner of that book and read the back of the page upside down.

"Six hundred," he said.

"Yes."

"Six hundred marks off that board and the last of them was cut before the light came over that wall."

"It was not cut this morning," said the clerk.

"I know it was not," he said. "It does not change anything. I have been three years at a table in a yard where nothing gets looked up at, and now there is a round figure sitting out there at the end of it, and I have been at this table every single one of the days it took to get there, and nobody looked up when it came."

He went up the slope with the cloth.

The clerk stood at the rail and looked out at the far end of that ground, where the board is, and where nobody was, and had been, and would be at about ten to eight tomorrow.
---

She shut the book and put the pen back in the holder and stood at that rail for the length of about two of her own counts.

Then she did not open it again.

There is a worn place in that paper about four inches in from the foot of the page, about the width of two fingers, and she has never tried to correct for it, and every line she has ever written across it has sat a little lower and a little further right than the lines above it.

She has stood at that rail and looked at the lines on that back page perhaps once in ten mornings for the whole of fifteen years, and this morning she did not do it, and she knows exactly where she did not do it, and that is the whole of what happened to her today.

Then she wrote the three hundred and twenty-seventh morning under the last line in her own small careful hand.

It went over that worn place about four inches in from the foot of the page and it did not sit level with the lines above it and she left it where it was.

That page was carrying sixty-two lines when the covers came over it.
---

At about half past four the light left those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight.

Two figures went out into that yard at about half past one in the same minute, about four feet apart, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line for either. Nobody in that yard looked up at anything that day, least of all at a round figure standing out at the end of it. The lane count was thirty and nothing came down that lane. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have still not been read to anybody here. The fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted by a clerk and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
