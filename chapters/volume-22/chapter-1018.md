# Chapter 1018: The Three Hundred And Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Turner's Wheel Went On Turning At The Speed It Goes At Through The Whole Of That Morning Without Slowing And The Man At The End Of The Second Table Said One Thing Once About His Own Table And Was Answered By Nobody And Nobody Asked Him For His Fifth Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and sixteen. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty-two days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-six days. Nine hundred and seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was never run and it is not open and nothing in this district or any other has opened it since. Six hundred and twenty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this yard is. Six hundred and twenty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood where it was said. Six hundred and ninety-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-five days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-nine days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-five marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and sixty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing whatever. Four documents this district does not own. Four places can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nobody put a reason out loud in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-two mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty-two mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and two mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the three hundred and first morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and forty-third of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-seventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-six of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-eighth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninety-first time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines. The record has stood two hundred and seventeen days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-seventh afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and will not be raised again or paid a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

Thirty. And there is no other figure coming this morning.

He says that last part out loud as well, because a number on its own at those boards leaves about four people standing about nine feet off him waiting for the rest of it, and he has found out the hard way that the rest of it has to be said too.

---

There are nine spaces between the boards in front of him where he sits with his elbows on the rail.

He counted them before there was light on the water in the ditch and he counted them again after the light got on that wall at the top of the bank, and he got nine both times, and he has got nine every morning of the run of his being at these boards.

That is the whole of what he does at ten to eight. It is one number and it does not move and there is nothing on the boards behind him that says where it came from or what it is for, and nobody has ever asked him where the thirty came from, and he could not tell them if they did.

---

The wheel at the near end of that ground went all morning.

He could hear it from the rail and he could hear it from the boards when the boards were down off the cart, and it did not slow once in between. Not at nine when the second table went a foot along its own boards, and not at half past nine when the man of thirty-eight came down that slope, and not at about four when the light came off the top of that bank and lay down along the stones.

That wheel is the only thing in that yard that never changes its mind. Everything else in that place goes up and down with the wind and the light and what a person has in their hands, and that wheel goes at one speed from the first hour of the morning to the last.

---

At about half past twelve she came down that bank with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and worked there all afternoon and said nothing out loud at any point of that day.

He watched her set up from where he was sitting on the rail, which is the way he watches her set up, without any expression at all and without looking away once.

He is seventeen, and there are about nineteen people in that yard, and he is the one who sits at the place where the counting gets done, and he has had a good many years to notice that those two arrangements have nothing to do with each other.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it.

It reached him where he was sitting without his elbows shifting off that rail, and he let it reach him that way. It went up the bank and four voices picked it up out of step with one another and it came back down again, and it has been doing that every morning of the run of his being at those boards, and it has never once been in a book.

Nobody at the boards wrote it down. He had a pencil in the pocket of his coat that morning and he did not take it out.

---

At about twenty past two the man at the end of the second table said something.

He has been at that end of that table about fifteen years. There is a stove going at that end and there is a place on that table where his hand lives, and he has not said a word out loud at that end of that table for a long run of mornings.

He straightened up off the bench and turned round to the length of his own table and said, once, in the ordinary voice:

"I could set this corner by the sound of it."

---

Nobody answered him.

Nobody at the near end of the second table looked up over the cloth. Nobody at the far end of it put his hand down to steady himself on the wood. Nobody at the boards turned round on the rail, and nobody at the top of eleven feet called down about it, and about nine people stood along the top of that bank and said nothing at all.

He said one thing and the thing was about his own table and there was nothing in it that anybody could have gone back on, and nobody went back on it, and that is the whole of him and the whole of that morning for him.

---

That man has been at that end of that table about fifteen years and there is a place on that wood where his hand lives and the grain of it has gone flat under the hand.

He keeps his own count of that, the way a man keeps a count of anything he has done every day for longer than he can remember the beginning of. He has never said it out loud in that yard and nobody at that yard has ever asked him for it, and the two of those things have stood side by side for so long that nobody in that yard could now say which of them came first.

---

That wheel went on turning at the speed it goes at all morning and it was still going at half past four when the light came off that bank, and it was still going when the boards came down off the cart face down, and the turner did not stop it for either.

---

A yard where a number goes out into the air at about half past one and four people take it up is a yard where something has happened that morning.

That yard is at the foot of a bank and the number has been going out of it every morning for longer than he has been sitting at these boards, and he has never once been able to get at where it comes from, and he has never once been asked to.

A number of his own goes out at those boards in front of about nine people on some mornings and not on the others, and on the mornings it does not go out he says so out loud, because the four people standing about nine feet off him are waiting for the rest of it and they are entitled to know.

---

At about half past three the sun came over the top of the low wall and lay itself down along the stones, and the man standing in that strip of it had been standing in it since about nine and said good afternoon to the man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him, and got one word back, and neither of them said anything else.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall with his hand up on the stone and got all four of them off it, and then laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"You can have them all four twice a morning and not one of them will tell you its use," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and ninety-first time running and nobody asked him anything about it.

---

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards to the near end of the second table with the book in her hand and gave the account of that morning out loud before the pen was near any of it.

"The three hundred and second morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my hand and there is no line in it, and that is the whole of the account and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and second morning under the last line and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book and carried fifty-nine lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said that afternoon by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He counted the nine spaces between the boards again from where he was sitting and got the same number he got in the morning, and thirty pages have come down that lane, and a page going up that lane is not a page coming down it and a person going out of that yard is not a page and a person not asking is not a page either.

He took his elbows off the rail and wrote nothing down.

---

The turner's wheel went on turning at the speed it goes at through the whole of that morning without slowing and did not stop until the light came off the boards. The man at the end of the second table said one thing once about his own table and was answered by nobody and nobody asked him for anything else. The page at the back of that lot book took no line and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut it. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing.
