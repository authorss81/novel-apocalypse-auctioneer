# Chapter 1041: The Three Hundred And Twenty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Back Of His Heel

Nothing has ever been done to that sheet by anybody in this district, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighty days is the age of that figure and it has not shifted in one of them.

The board carries nine hundred and thirty-nine and belongs to nobody here. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fifty-five days without a wagon coming off it. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and sixty-nine days at that rail. Nine hundred and thirty days lie between the second of January and this morning.

It stood open four hundred and fifty days and was not run, and it is not a lot and it is not open. That yard is six hundred and forty-four days behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and forty-nine days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and nineteen days have gone by since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and fifty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and twenty-two days back from this morning. Five hundred and ninety-eight marks have been cut off that board with a knife. Five hundred and eighty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table have not come off in any weather this district has had.

Four documents are not owned in this district. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen things have been made in this district and five were never got, and the fifth of the five is unpaid. Seven months has been counted by a clerk and is written on nothing at all.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and that was before the page at the back of that lot book was anywhere a reason could go, and no line was written on that page for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because until that morning it could not hold one, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason went out loud in that yard this morning, and the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines all day. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank for three hundred and fifty-five mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for three hundred and five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-five mornings, entered on the word of the man who keeps a tally and put into that air before it goes on paper. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and twenty-fourth morning and it is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and sixty-sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eightieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and seventy-nine of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and seventieth afternoon running and one word is the whole of what he says. And that is the one hundred and eighty-first morning running with nobody asking that man anything. The flat answer that man says to that stone is the two hundred and fourteenth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards and nobody has entered anything in it. Five lines are ruled across the first of its pages and the column on the second of them has one name in it under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has ever been asked to put a word in it. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane this morning. That record has stood two hundred and forty days and nobody has ever asked what it is for. About nine people are standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and nobody is going to raise it or pay it a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have never been read by anybody in this district.
---

Ten to eight, at those boards, and the count.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and there is nothing behind it this morning."

The clerk did not look up from the rail. She opened that book and shut it again.

"You have said that every morning this week."

"I have."

"Does it get any better by saying it."

"No," said the boy. "It gets said."

He went on up the slope with his hands in his pockets and did not count the boards on the way, which was the first morning of that run he had not done that.
---

At about half past eight the man of about thirty-two put his own table up at the end of that ground, the way he has put it up every morning for three years, and got the leg on the board he knows and did not look at the leg.

Three hundred and twenty-four.

He had that figure before the leg was on and it had been in him since before he got to the end of the boards, and he had been three years finding a sentence to put round it, and every sentence he built had the number at the front of it, and a number at the front of a sentence means the rest of it has to say what the number is for.

So he said nothing out loud this morning, and he worked along his own top with the cloth, and the number stayed where it was.

---

Across about four feet of boards from where his own table stood, the near corner of another table was out of square.

The boy had been looking at it since he was nine. It had been out then. He had never once known it any other way and he had never asked anybody about it, because you do not walk up to a man about a corner at eight in the morning.

At about half past nine he got his knee on the boards and put the flat of his hand down across them, the way a person does without deciding to.

The boards went down.

Not much. About half an inch, across four feet, from the table leg towards him. He lay his hand on it a second time to be sure, and then he sat back on his heels and looked along the length of that run of boards and could see it, because a straight line that ought to be straight is not straight when the ground under one end of it has gone somewhere it was not.

So that was why. It had not been set wrong by anybody. The ground had gone and taken the corner with it and nobody had laid anything under it, and three winters of looking at it had not fixed it, because three winters of looking at a thing does not move the ground.

He got up and knocked the dirt off his knee and did not tell anybody, because he had found it out himself and he did not know what a person was supposed to do with a thing like that.
---

The turner came along the boards about ten with his hand still warm off the frame and stopped about four feet off the boy.

"You were down on your knee there."

"I was."

"Along those boards, or along his."

"Along the corner," said the boy. "His."

The turner looked at it for about as long as a wheel goes round twice.

"That ground went about four years ago," he said. "Everything on the low side of that bank went about the same time. Nobody has laid anything under those boards because nobody is going to take up a run of boards that goes under a working man's feet six hours a day."

"Then how is he going to square it."

"He is not going to square it," said the turner. "He is going to do whatever he does with it and then it will be out again in a season and he will do it again. That is what that corner is."

The boy looked at it.

"You have watched him do it before."

"I have watched him not do it," said the turner. "Go on."

He went up the slope with his hand off the frame and did not say anything else about it.
---

The man of about thirty-four came down off that bank at about eleven with a cloth over his arm and a piece of pallet in his coat pocket, and the boy knew about the pallet because he had watched a woman of about fifty-two put it in that man's hand four mornings ago and tell him which end of it was wrong before she let go of it.

He did not say good morning. He did not say anything at all, then or at any other point of that day.

He put his own table up about four feet along from the second table, on the boards, and set the leg on, and went round the end of it and back, and then took his heel off his boot and set it against the near corner of that top where it came out past the leg.

He pushed once.

The corner came round about half an inch and stopped.

He took the heel off and set it again a little further along and pushed again, and the corner came round the rest of the way, and when he took his heel away the corner stayed where his heel had put it.

Nobody in that yard looked up.

The man of thirty-four went along the top of that table with the cloth, from the near corner to the far one and back, the whole length of it, and he did not say one word to anybody for the rest of that day.
---

The boy went and got his knee back down on those boards about half past eleven, after the corner was done, and put his hand across the same run of boards.

It still went down about half an inch.

Nothing had been laid under it. He put his fingers in the gap at the edge of the boards and felt the edge of something down there, a strip of wood, an inch and a bit wide, gone grey, and that was all that was under that corner.

So the heel had done it. Three winters of out, and about one push of a boot, and the ground had not moved at all while it happened.

He sat there a while longer than he needed to.
---

The man of fifty-six was at that wall with his hand up at about a quarter to eight and got all four off it before the boy was down off the slope, and came down the strip of stone with his hand shut and said nothing to anybody about it.

The boy went down and stood about four feet off him because that was as close as he ever got.

"You read them before the light's on it."

"I read them before the light's on it."

"What are they."

"That is one question," said the man of fifty-six, "and I have got four figures, and you are not getting one of them out of me this morning."

The boy laughed, which he did not mean to do.

"Sorry."

"Do not be," said the man of fifty-six. He opened his hand and looked at the four marks on it and closed it again. "I have said the same four words to that stone twice a morning for longer than you have been standing at those boards."

"I did not think you would mind."

"I did not say I minded," said the man of fifty-six. "I said I was not going to do it."

He went up the slope with his hand still shut.
---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down off that bank and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and worked there all afternoon with the wind on every pan.

The woman of about fifty-two came down that bank at about two with a frame under her arm and a bag in her hand and stopped where the boy was kneeling.

"Get off that, you are letting the dirt back into the gap you have made."

He got off it.

"That piece I gave him," she said, "came off a pallet that had been out in the weather two years and one end of it is sound and the other end is not. I told him which end before I let go of it."

"Then why has he not laid it in."

"Because he has not," said the woman of about fifty-two. "And he has had it four mornings, and every one of those mornings he has had a hand on that corner and not one of them has gone near his pocket, and I am not going to be the one who asks him why."

"He does not say much."

"He said one thing to me once, a good while back, and nothing has come back off it, and that is not a thing I am going to ask him about either." She looked at the boards. "Your knee has been in that gap. What did you find."

"The ground went," said the boy. "That is all. Nothing is under it."

She stood looking down at it for a while with the frame under her arm and did not say anything at all about that, and then she went back up the bank with the bag in her hand and the frame knocking against her leg all the way.
---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices said it back after him out of step with one another, and it went up the bank and came off it again.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices said it back after him out of step with one another, and it went up the bank and came off it again and the day carried on after it the way that yard carries on after a number.

The boy heard the whole of it from the boards and wrote nothing down, which is what he has always done with the ones he cannot put on a page. The woman who keeps a scale was at her own scale about four feet off the near end of the second table with the wind on every pan and she did not look up when it went past her either.
---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it one after another and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Two hundred and fourteen mornings," he said out loud to that stone. "And this morning somebody did in one push what I have been telling that stone about for a run of mornings."

About nineteen people heard that. Nobody asked him what he meant, because there is no question in it.
---

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards with that book open against the rail and gave the whole of the account of that morning out loud, and the pen was near no part of it while she did that.

"The three hundred and twenty-fifth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the pen was near the page."

She wrote the three hundred and twenty-fifth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.
---

At about half past four the light left those boards. About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard.

Orren Pike counted the nine spaces between the boards on his way up the bank, and got nine, and did not say the number out loud, because he had said a number out loud at ten to eight already that day and there was no second one behind it.

A corner that has been out of square for three winters was squared this morning by the back of a man's heel and nothing was laid under it. Nobody thanked the man who squared it and nobody asked him why it had taken three winters. The page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines and did not move. The lane count was thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have gone through this district without anybody hearing them read. What this district does not have is five, and the fifth of those five is unpaid. Seven months is counted by a clerk and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost.
