# Chapter 1016: The Three Hundredth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Count Of Mornings A Man Has Stood At The Foot Of That Bank Came To Three Hundred And The Figure Block Of That Morning Carries The Same Figure In A Second Place And Nobody In That Yard Looked Up And Nobody Asked Anybody What It Is The Three Hundredth Of

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and fourteen. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-four days. Nine hundred and five days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not open. Six hundred and nineteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book the writing in this yard has got. Six hundred and twenty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood where it was said. Six hundred and ninety-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-three days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-seven days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-three marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and fifty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven months is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing. Four documents this district does not own. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

There was no reason said out loud by anybody in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-ninth morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and forty-first of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-fifth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-four of them, and this is the one hundred and fifty-sixth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-ninth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines. The record has stood two hundred and fifteen days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-fifth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

Fifteen years is a thing that comes round on your own arithmetic and not on anybody else's.

She had done the sum in her head standing at the top of that bank on the way down, the way she does it now most mornings, and the sum had come to three hundred, and she had not said a word about it to anybody and she was not going to.

There is no ceremony in this yard for a figure coming round onto a round number. There is a number in a hand late in that afternoon and that is the whole of what happens to a figure in this district when it lands on one.

---

She came to the boards at about ten to eight with the book under her arm and let that boy say the count before she put a hand on anything.

It is thirty. He said it out loud at those boards in the voice he keeps for it and he put nothing after it, and she had a run of about a hundred mornings of that number in her ear by now and had never once let it get to her, and it did not get to her this morning either.

The book is a lot book. It has five lines on it and it has been on those boards longer than she has been a clerk, and she did not bring it down that hill this morning so much as carry it down with her, the way she has carried it every morning of the fifteen years.

---

The man of fifty-six had his four off that wall before there was light on it. She saw his shape come up out of the grey at that wall from about sixty feet off and she turned away from it and did the ordinary morning.

The man of thirty-two turned up at nine with his cloth over his arm and halted at the boards and laid the flat of his hand along the top of that rail.

"There is a gap under that rail this morning that was not there when I walked in," he said.

"There is not," said Orren Pike.

"I have been setting tables up off this rail for years and I know where it sits."

Orren Pike got down off the stool and went along the near side of that rail on his knees with his face about a foot off the ground, and there was no gap under it anywhere along that length, and he came back up and said so, and the man of thirty-two went on along the top of his own table with the cloth and did not argue about it.

That is the amount of talking that gets said at those boards, and it is enough, and there is nobody in that yard it is for.

---

Three men came down that slope at nine with the second table between them and the rail on the near side of it going along with it, and the man who works at that end stood with his heel jammed on a wedge until the last of them had gone past, and then lifted his heel and dropped the wedge by the leg.

Then the man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the bank and back down and in at the same corner, and the cloth went along the boards in his hand without any noise at all.

Nobody looked up when he came down that slope, and it has never once been a thing anybody at that bank has looked up at, and that is the whole of the notice that gets taken of his coming down there.

---

About half past twelve the woman came down that bank with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and worked there all day and said nothing out loud at any point of it.

The clerk did not go over to her and the clerk has never gone over to her.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it after him out of step with one another, and it came back off the top of that bank.

That number is in no book in this district. It is not in that lot book and it is not on the back of it and it is not on the sheet at the gatepost and it has never been on a wall. It goes out at about half past one and four voices pick it up and it comes down again, and it has been doing that for a run of mornings long enough that nobody at those boards remembers a morning when it did not.

---

At about four the clerk came along the boards to the near end of the second table with the book in her hand and stopped where she stops.

She says it out loud first. That is the whole of the rule in this yard and nobody has ever asked her where it comes from.

"The three hundredth morning," she said.

She stopped there. She did not put anything on the word and she did not go on and she was aware, standing in the sun with the book open against the rail, of about four people within about nine feet of her who had heard the number and were not doing anything about it.

Then she gave the rest of it out loud before she had the pen near any part of the page.

"Nobody put a reason into the air in this yard this morning. So the back of that book gets nothing from this morning, and this morning goes into my own hand with nothing in it, and that is all I have to say about it, and I have said it before I made a mark."

---

She wrote the three hundredth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand.

She wrote it the way she has written every other morning for fifteen years. The hand is the same hand and the nib is the same nib and the letters are the same size, and there is a worn place in that paper about four inches in from the foot of the page where an old pen stood and was let stand too long in a year nobody in this district can name.

Three hundred is a figure with nothing on the end of it and nothing missing off the front of it, and she wrote it out in three words, and the three words are the same size as the two hundred and ninety-ninth was yesterday and the same size as the two hundred and ninety-eighth was the day before.

Fifteen years is a run of about five thousand mornings at that corner and she has written one line in that small careful hand for every morning she has been a clerk, and there is not one morning in the whole of the fifteen in which the hand has done anything different.

That is not a thing she is proud of and it is not a thing she is ashamed of. It is a hand.

---

She sat there with the book open against the rail for a minute after that, with the pen in the holder and the afternoon going on about nine feet off her, and she looked at the line she had written the way you look at a nail you have driven and know is in.

---

Nobody at that bank said anything about it.

Nobody asked her what it was the three hundredth of. Nobody asked the man who keeps a tally what his own count was the three hundredth of. Nobody said that anything had happened. The wheel went on turning at the near end of that ground at the speed it goes at, and about nine people stood at the top of eleven feet, and a man of fifty-six laid his hand flat on a stone about four hundred yards off.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was back at his own wall and had his hand up on the stone again, and he got all four of the figures off it and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"A man can put his hand on this stone twice a morning for years and still not know what these four are for," he said to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and eighty-ninth time running and nobody asked him anything about any of it.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said that afternoon by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

She shut the book and put the pen back into the holder and set the book down against the near rail and that boy put his thumb under the corner of it to settle it and took his thumb away again.

That page carried fifty-nine lines when she got the covers of that book over it and it was carrying the same fifty-nine when she had it shut.

---

Nobody in that yard looked up on the three hundredth morning and nobody asked anybody what it is the three hundredth of. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred mornings on the word of the man who keeps it, and the page at the back of that lot book took no line and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven.
