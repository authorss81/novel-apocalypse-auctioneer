# Chapter 1020: The Three Hundred And Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of About Sixty-Four Was At The Foot Of That Low Wall And Was Asked Nothing By Anybody On Any Part Of That Day, Which Is The One Hundred And Sixtieth Morning Running Of That, And The Lane Count Is Thirty And Nothing Came Down That Lane

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and eighteen. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty-four days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-eight days. Nine hundred and nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and is not open and no second opening of it has ever been made. Six hundred and twenty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this district is. Six hundred and twenty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and ninety-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-seven days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and one days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-seven marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and sixty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing at all. Four documents this district does not own. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

No reason was said out loud in that yard this morning by anybody, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty lines and carried sixty lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-four mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty-four mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and four mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the three hundred and third morning. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and forty-fifth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifty-ninth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-eight of them, and this is the one hundred and sixtieth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninety-third time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries sixty lines. The record has stood two hundred and nineteen days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and forty-ninth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

He came to that wall at about nine and stood in the strip of ground that the low wall keeps the morning sun out of, and he waited for the sun to come over the top of it, which it did at a little after half past three, and that was the arrangement.

About four feet off him on the cold stones a man of sixty-one had his coat folded beside him and had been sitting there since before he came.

They have been at that wall and those stones at the same hour for longer than either of them would care to be asked about, and neither of them has ever asked the other one a single question, and the whole of the arrangement between them is two words at about half past three.

---

The morning goes along that yard at the foot of that wall without touching it.

He can see the bottom nine feet of the second table from where he stands and the leg of it and the boot of a man who stands with it on a wedge while three men get a table past him. He can see the near end of the first table and the cloth going along the top of it and in at the corner at the far end of it.

He can see about nineteen people and about nine of them are up on that bank and he has never once gone up it himself and does not go up it now.

---

Nothing in that yard has ever come to him.

That is not a complaint and it is not a thing he has ever gone looking for. He stands at the foot of that low wall and the yard does what the yard does about four feet away from his boot, and nobody crosses the strip of stone to put a question in front of him, and nobody has.

This morning he noticed it.

He noticed it about eleven, in the middle of nothing in particular, the way a person notices a thing they have done every day of their life. Nobody came. Nobody stopped. The whole length of that yard went on from nine in the morning without a word of it coming in his direction.

And he stood there and found that he had no idea how long that had been going on. Not a figure for it, not a run of mornings, not anything he could put a hand on. It had been going on for as long as he had been standing at that wall and he had not once gone to find out where it began.

---

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down off that bank with his hands showing and worked his round of the first table and came in at the corner he comes in at.

Three men brought the second table a foot down that slope at nine with the rail riding along on the near side of it, and the man at that end held his heel on a wedge until they were clear and then dropped the wedge by the leg.

A wheel went round at the near end of that ground at the speed it goes at and did not change.

---

About half past twelve she came down that bank with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and worked there all afternoon and said nothing out loud at any point of that day.

At about half past one the man who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and four voices took it up after him out of step with one another and it came back off the top of that bank.

None of that came to the foot of that wall either. None of it has come to the foot of that wall in as long as he has been at it.

---

About half past three the sun came over the top of the low wall and lay itself down along the stones at the foot of it and reached his boot, and he moved his foot about a foot further along into it without moving anything else about himself.

The man of sixty-one got up off the stones about the time he always gets up, which is before the sun has finished coming down them, and stood with his hand on the low wall for a minute and then let go of it.

---

"Good afternoon," he said.

"Afternoon," said the man of sixty-one, and the man of sixty-one went up that bank, and neither of them said anything else.

It is the only exchange that yard has with him. Two words and a word, at about half past three every day, from a man of sixty-one who has seventeen years of a thing of his own and has never once put a question to anybody at the foot of that wall.

The man of sixty-one has never asked him where he came from and he has never asked the man of sixty-one what the seventeen years are, and neither of them has ever wanted to, and that is a thing two men can do for a long time without either of them noticing that they are doing it.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall with his hand up on the stone, and he got all four of the figures off it and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"I get all four of these twice a morning and nobody has ever told me what they are for," he said to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and ninety-third time running and nobody asked him anything about any part of it.

---

Just after four the clerk came along the boards with the book in her hand and gave the account of that morning out loud before she had the pen near any part of it.

"The three hundred and fourth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my own hand and there is no line in it, and that is the whole of the account and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the three hundred and fourth morning under the last line and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty lines when she shut the book and carried sixty lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard went along that bank said by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stayed where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He did not say the goodnight. He has never said it. He has been at the foot of that wall for a run of evenings long enough that about four people out of about nineteen have stopped putting their heads round to see whether he is still standing there, and the goodnight goes along that bank without him in it and goes down the other side without him in it.

---

Then the bank emptied.

About nineteen people went up it and the light was still on the top of it for a while after the last of them, and then that went too, and the two tables were standing there in the dark with the rail along the near side of the second one, and the wheel was standing still at the near end of that ground, and there was nothing in that yard but him and a low wall and a strip of cold stone.

The lane goes up off the top of that bank and he cannot see it from where he stands and has never seen what comes down it, and nothing has come down it in as long as he has been standing at this wall.

---

Thirty pages have come down that lane at some point in the run of that yard, in a hand of somebody's, and that is a number the boy at those boards says out loud at ten to eight before anybody in that yard has moved a hand.

It has not moved since, and it is not moved by a decision, and a person going out of that yard is not a page and a person not asking is not a page either.

---

He stood at the foot of that wall until the strip of stone had gone the same colour as the ground either side of it.

Nobody asked him anything on the three hundred and fourth morning. Nobody has asked him anything for one hundred and sixty mornings running, and he does not know that as a figure and does not know it as a run and nobody has ever told him and nobody is going to. The page at the back of that lot book carried sixty lines when she shut the book and carried sixty lines after. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
