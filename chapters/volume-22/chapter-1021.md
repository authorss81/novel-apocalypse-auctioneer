# Chapter 1021: The Three Hundred And Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of About Twenty-Nine Who Drives The Cart Came Through That Gap And Did Not Stop In It, And Nobody Said A Figure Out Loud Behind Him

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and no person in this basin has ever done anything whatever with it. Seven hundred and sixty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and nineteen this morning and has carried one more than it carried yesterday. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and forty-nine days. Nine hundred and ten days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and it was never run, and nothing here has opened it since. Six hundred and twenty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book this yard has got. Six hundred and twenty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood where it was said. Six hundred and ninety-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and a body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and two days back and no night has been named. Five hundred and seventy-eight marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and sixty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is on nothing whatever. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen things is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and that was before the page at the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because until that morning it was not a place a reason could go, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

Nobody put a reason out loud in that yard this morning, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty lines and it stood at sixty lines when she shut the book. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-five mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eighty-five mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and five mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood there for the three hundred and fourth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and forty-sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and sixtieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifty-nine of them, and this is the one hundred and sixty-first morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and ninety-fourth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries sixty lines. The record has stood two hundred and twenty days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fiftieth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles is between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and is not going to be raised or paid a second time. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read by anybody in this district.

---

He has four gaps in a day and he has never once come to a stop in any of them.

That is not a rule he set out to keep. He is twenty-nine and he drives a cart, and a cart at a walking pace goes where the opening is, and an opening does not wait to be looked at. The first gap is at the mill end, where a wall comes down to about the height of the shafts and the ground under it is chalk that holds a wheel. The second is between two stacks that a longer cart would not fit between at all. The third is this one, and the fourth is a field gate with a hinge that has to be lifted with the left hand while the right one holds the rein.

---

The gap is not in the same place twice.

That is the thing about the yard at the foot of that bank, and it is the only thing about it that has ever made him slow down. The second table in that yard gets carried a foot further along its own boards every morning of the run of it, and the near rail goes a foot with the table and has never once been moved on its own, and what that leaves is an opening at the low end of that ground which is about the width of his cart in the morning and was about the width of his cart and a hand's breadth behind last night.

He has never once come to a stop to find it. He slows to a walk at two hundred yards, he takes the opening on the way the table has gone and not the way it was, and he has never once had to back up.

---

The load was four sacks of meal and a barrow frame with one wheel missing, which is why it was on the cart at all.

He came down the track at about ten to eight with the sun behind him and the yard laid out below the bank in the flat white light that comes before a day has decided anything about itself.

A wheel going over at the near end of that ground at one speed. Three men walking a table down a slope with a rail riding along on the near side of it, and a man at the end of that table holding his heel on a wedge until they were past. A stove at the far end of the second table with a fire already in it. Somebody going out along the top of another table, round the end of it, up the slope and back down and in at the same corner, and making nothing at all.

---

That is what he has for the yard: four sounds and one silence, and the silence is the one that takes him longest to get used to.

He drives four gaps a day and every gap in the world tells him what kind of place it is by its noise before he is in it. A yard with about nineteen people in it ought to be a loud place by the time the cart is in the second third of it. This one is not. He has come through it enough times now that he can stand at the top of that bank and take the whole of it in and find that what comes up off it is a wheel at one speed, a spade about four hundred yards off, a door on a stove, and a number that one man says at half past one and four other people say back at him out of step with one another.

And one figure in the middle of that yard that comes out of it as no sound whatever.

---

He put the mill sacks down at about half past eight and took the barrow frame on again with the one wheel still missing, and he came back to that yard about ten to twelve because the ground on the other side of the bank is soft after the rain and the hard strip along the foot of it is the one piece of that road a two-wheeled thing can stand on without going over.

It is not an arrangement he made. It is the arrangement of that yard, and it will be there after he has gone.

He came up the track and stopped the cart at the top of that bank and did not get down, and he looked at about four hundred yards of air the way he looks at a yard, which is to say the way a man looks at a thing he has no part in.

---

At about half past one the man at the bottom of that ground put his number into the air.

From the top of a bank it is one noise. It comes off the hard ground and up the slope and arrives at the cart as the fact that a number has been put out down there rather than as the number itself, and the four voices come back about half a beat behind it and out of step, the way four people come back when none of them was ready first.

He has heard it in that yard enough mornings for it to be a sound he could find in his sleep. It is the loudest thing that yard does.

---

He sat on that cart at the top of that bank until the light came off the boards and then he took the rein and brought the cart down onto the hard strip.

He came through the gap without slowing it any further than he had already been going.

The second table was a foot further along than it had been at ten to eight and he found the opening the way his hands found it, and the cart went through on four feet of clear ground and out the other side, and the rail was not touched and nothing on the boards shifted an inch.

Nobody in that yard said one word to him. Nobody has said one word to him in any morning he has come through there, and nobody has ever said a figure out loud behind him after his cart has gone out of it.

---

He went on up the hill with the barrow frame on the cart and the sun now on the side of that yard rather than behind him, and by the time he was at the top of the next rise there was nothing between him and it but the crest of the bank.

That is the sum of what he got off that yard this morning. About nineteen people on about an acre of hard ground with a wheel turning at one end of it and a stove burning at the other, and one man who goes round the same table all day and does not once make a sound any human ear could pick out at the distance of a cart.

He has driven four gaps a day for eleven years and he has never once stopped in any of them, and this morning at the top of that bank he sat on a cart for the better part of an hour, which is longer than he sits anywhere, and what he did with it was listen to a yard he had been passing through.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall with his hand up on the stone and got all four of the figures off it, and then laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"There is not one of you knows what these four are for and I have got them all off this wall twice a day," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and ninety-fourth time running and nobody asked him anything about any part of it.

---

At about ten past four the clerk came along the boards to the near end of the second table with the book in her hand and gave the account of that morning out loud before the pen was near any part of it.

"The three hundred and fifth morning," she said. "Nobody put a reason into the air in this yard this morning, so nothing goes on the back of that book for it, and this morning goes into my own hand on its own with nothing in it, and that is the account of it and it went out loud before I wrote any of it."

She wrote the three hundred and fifth morning under the last line in her own small hand and took no line for it, and the page carried sixty lines when she shut the book and carried sixty lines after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four people out of about nineteen of them said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

---

The man of about twenty-nine came through the gap at the foot of that bank at a quarter to four with a load on his cart and nothing in his hands but the rein, and he did not stop in it, and nobody in that yard said a figure out loud behind him after he had gone through. Nothing came down that lane and the page at the back of that lot book took no line and stood at sixty lines. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and its size of error is uncomputed, unexplained and unpaid.
