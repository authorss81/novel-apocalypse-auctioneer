# Chapter 3: The Auditor's Question

Mara Quill had been taught that a blank space in a record was never actually blank. It was a door left open by whoever had decided that the person behind it was inconvenient.

She came into Lot 17 with a rain-dark coat, a red pencil, and the municipal seal case held against her ribs like a weapon that had learned manners. The sale floor was nearly empty now. Wet bootprints crossed the tram boards. A child had traced a finger through the grit near the inspection block, leaving a small curved mark that might have been a question or a fish.

Mara saw it and wrote nothing.

“Public sale book,” she said to Nessa. “Last four months.”

Nessa looked relieved to have an order she knew. “I can get it.”

“I know you can. That is why I asked.” Mara placed the seal case on the counter. “Private inspection ledger.”

“Private items are not public unless they enter a charter,” Nessa said. It was the kind of answer that made Mara decide she liked her.

Adrian came out of the back office before Nessa could move. His coat was damp at the shoulders, and a red thread was tied around his left thumb.

“Routine audit?” he asked.

“Routine until your sale book and your private ledger disagreed.”

“They don’t disagree. One has public lots. The other has things that are not public lots.”

“Show me the distinction.”

He led her to the inspection corner. The table had been cleared, and beneath it sat a glass jar of preserved lamp parts, a cracked dial, and a brass transit token tied with red cloth.

Mara did not touch it immediately. She put one hand on the table and crouched to inspect the jar first.

“This gasket is marked unsafe,” she said.

“Correct.”

“Date?”

“Year Fifteen. Lot 34.”

“The housing is in a sale as scrap?”

“Correct.”

She wrote the entries in a narrow hand. Then she looked under the table again.

“And the token?”

“Found during the sale.”

“Found by whom?”

“A boy with more paper than manners.”

Mara opened her red tablet to a new page. “That is not a custody entry.”

“It is a description.”

“It is the beginning of one.” She sat on the low inspection crate. “Where was it found?”

“Under the sale block.”

“Who put it there?”

“I don’t know.”

“Who saw it before you did?”

“Nessa did not. The buyer did not. The runner did not remain.”

“Then you have a witnessed transfer into private hands and no record of the witness.”

“I found it on my table.”

“You found it in a public room where the legal custody of loose metal is not particularly clear.” Mara’s red pencil hovered. “Was it in the public sale book?”

“No.”

“Was it recorded as a private inspection item?”

“Yes, in my own ledger.”

“Who may inspect that ledger?”

“Me. Whoever I trust.”

“That is not a custody system, Adrian. It is a rumor with a lock on it.”

His mouth tightened. “The fever shipment is stuck at St. Orra.”

“I know.”

“The official bridge is closed.”

“I know that too.”

“The clinic has one day of certain cases. If you spend the day making a record of a brass token while the children cough behind a river wall, the record will be very well written and useless.”

Mara’s pencil stopped. She had expected him to make the medicine an argument. She had not expected him to make it badly enough to be honest.

“Where is the shipment now?” she asked.

“East of the underpass. The messenger says the fever cases have one day.”

“By whose estimate?”

“Clinic porter. The bridge says three days to inspect.”

“Those are not the same measure.”

“No.”

“Good. Then do not turn two estimates into a deadline for the record.”

A man at the door spoke before Nessa could stop him. “That is the trouble with municipal time. It knows how to count and nothing else.”

Mara did not turn. She had heard the voice in three dispute rooms and once through a market riot. Halloway Reed had a habit of arriving before the person he wanted to meet finished making their mistake.

“Mr. Reed,” she said. “You are obstructing an audit.”

“I’m offering an alternative.” He stepped inside without waiting for permission. His coat was dark with rain, but his boots were dry. “The token is a salvage object. The inspection is a public object. The medicine is a private object. Let me move the medicine and leave the paperwork to the clerks.”

“Your alternative is a counterfeit claim,” Adrian said.

Halloway’s gaze moved to the red-cloth-wrapped token. “Is that what it is?”

“Would you like to buy it?”

“Not at the price I imagine you want.”

“Then keep the offer.”

Mara’s attention returned to the token. The red cloth was tied in a neat square, the same way Adrian marked unsafe lots on the sale floor. His care had not disappeared. It had simply been applied to an object he did not understand.

She wrote on her tablet: *Token found during public sale. Absent from the municipal registry. Origin unverified. No active charter. An old inactive custody transfer only. No authorization to activate.*

Then she underlined **no active charter** twice.

“Do you know what that means?” she asked Adrian.

“It means you cannot use it.”

“It means more than that.” Mara looked at him. “It means no one has disclosed the use, toll, term, or remedy. It means there are no fresh independent witnesses. It means nobody has been given a real chance to refuse.” She tapped the tablet. “It means your touching it does not make you its custodian. If you activate it without a valid closing, the Tally will treat you as a bare claimant. The first immediate toll can become yours by default.”

“I have not activated it.”

“I am writing down what you have not done before the opportunity passes.”

The word **Tally** entered the room and changed its shape. Nessa looked up from the sale book. Halloway’s eyes narrowed. Adrian’s hand moved to his coat pocket, then stopped.

“You recognize that mark?” Mara asked, nodding toward the token.

“The municipal stamp is old.”

“It is older than the stamp. There is a closing impression under the reverse rim.” Mara leaned closer without touching the glass. “A circle with a break in it. I have seen that style on old emergency-use records. Not the same impression, not exactly, but the same hand of the clerk—or the same office.”

“St. Orra records?” Tarin asked from the back room. He had been listening without appearing to listen, which was one of his more useful habits.

“No. The old municipal series from the west bank, before the flood. The records were moved during the first winter.” Mara looked at Adrian. “You said it came from a bus.”

“Runner said so.”

“Then the runner is a source, not a witness.”

Adrian’s jaw tightened. “If you seize it, the public will believe it is safe because it is in a municipal drawer.”

“If I leave it unrecorded, the public will believe whatever the next person tells them.” Mara pulled on thin leather gloves. “I am not seizing ownership. I am sealing custody. The token stays here, in the inspection corner, and neither you nor I move it without writing the reason.”

She opened a waxed envelope, placed the token and the torn manifest inside, and folded the paper over it. Her seal pressed into the red wax at the fold. Then she wrote the date, time, location, and the two custody attestants—herself and Adrian—on a strip of municipal paper. She gave the strip to Adrian.

“If I say accepted risk in a record, it is not consent,” she said. “It is a label someone else can use after the fact.”

“Your brother’s record,” Adrian said.

Mara’s pencil stopped. The silence between them acquired a hard edge. Tarin turned away and began inspecting the tram roof supports as if they had suddenly become the most interesting object in the room.

“Yes,” she said. “My brother’s record ended with that phrase. A bridge failed. The form said the risk had been accepted.”

“Was it?”

“No. He had accepted a shift. He had not accepted dying.” Her voice stayed level. “I learned the difference then. Do not use the same words with the people on that bus.”

Adrian took the custody strip. “I wasn’t going to.”

“You were going to let the shipment decide for you.”

“I was going to find a lawful way.”

“Good. Find it before someone finds a counterfeit.” Mara turned to Halloway. “And you will not move medicine through a claim whose terms you have not read aloud.”

Halloway lifted one hand. “I read them to the crew.”

“To the crew. Not to the people paying the toll.”

“They are not paying a toll. They are buying a route.”

“Then you will call the hidden term what it is.”

Mara closed the seal case. “The audit is complete. The object is not cleared. Adrian, you have until first light to produce a complete proposal: the lot, its condition, the territory, the use, the bearer, the beneficiaries, the toll, the term, two independent witnesses, a release or return condition, and a remedy if the route fails. Those are separate entries. A sentence that hides one inside another is not a disclosure. If you cannot produce them, the token stays sealed.”

“Who decides what is complete?” Adrian asked.

“The people who have to live with the answer do not get that luxury. I do.” She gathered her papers. “My inspection is not a witness. Do not make my job a substitute for consent.”

The messenger was waiting outside, bicycle lamp dark beneath his coat. He lifted the clinic slip as Mara passed.

“One night,” he said. “The cases have one night.”

Mara stopped beside Adrian. The sale floor had gone quiet enough to hear rain sliding from the roof into the gutter.

“First light,” she said. “Bring me terms, or bring me an honest failure.”
