# Chapter 8: The Counterfeit Fare

The kitchen exchange kept its secrets in flour.

Orren Pike knew this because he had spent the first six months of his life learning which cook could be trusted with a message and which one would sell it for a pair of boots. The room beneath the Ward Nine tram line smelled of boiled onions, wet wool, and lamp oil. Three flour barrels stood against the far wall. The largest held nothing but old flour. The second held a water pump. The third had a loose bottom and a cavity that had once been a cold store.

Paper lived in the third.

Orren crouched beside it with a candle hooded against the draft. He drew out a bundle of copied records, each tied with a different thread. The papers were not a history. They were a set of wounds someone else had tried to keep from bleeding.

His mother’s name appeared on the top sheet.

**SELA PIKE — WITNESS COPY — NOT ENTERED.**

The letters were small and even. The name had been copied from a bridge ledger and recopied from the bridge ledger, each time losing a little of the pressure that had made it original. Orren had traced the shape of the S until he could see it when he closed his eyes.

Below it, someone had written a second line.

**HOUSEHOLD TOLL ASSIGNED WITHOUT CONSENT.**

He put his thumb over the line. The paper smelled of flour and old rain. It was not proof of what had happened to Sela. It was proof of what someone had been willing to write instead.

A knock came from above.

Orren slid the papers back under the false bottom and covered the cavity with a dishcloth. Two knocks, then one, meant the household was changing shifts. One knock, then three, meant the street was being watched. He answered with two soft taps.

The courier who came down was a girl no older than Orren, with a red scarf around her wrists and a brass key sewn into her sleeve. She kept her face covered and did not ask his name.

“Warehouse,” she said. “Halloway moved the crates.”

“Which warehouse?”

“The one under the tram. The one with the false west wall.”

Orren shouldered his satchel. “Did the household see you?”

“No.”

“Keep it that way.”

She glanced toward the papers. “The copy is worse than we thought.”

“Show me.”

The courier placed a damp leaf on the flour barrel. It was a sale claim, copied in careful black letters. The first page said **ONE ROUTE ACROSS ST. ORRA. TWELVE MINUTES. MEMORY TOLL PAID BY EACH TRAVELER**. The second page described a return platform, a driver, and a compensation schedule for maintenance.

The return platform was the old salt-loading ramp on the far side of the Fault. It had not been a public return point since before the river wall moved. Anyone who followed it would arrive at a flooded maintenance trench.

The third page was folded under the others. Orren opened it.

**ADDITIONAL CONDITION: IF THE ROUTE CLOSES BEFORE COMPLETION, THE BEARER SHALL REPAIR THE CARRIAGE AND THE ADJACENT ROAD FOR SEVEN DAYS.**

The language was small enough to hide beneath a thumb. The consequence was not small at all.

“They make the carrier build the road,” he said.

“They make the carrier pay for the road they were promised.”

“The last fare does not say that.”

“Does it say what the return costs?”

Orren looked at the damp paper. “No.”

“Then they found a blank and filled it with labor.”

The courier tied the scarf tighter. “My aunt says the house will not keep the copy if the name is on it.”

Orren’s hand stopped on the satchel strap. “The name is not the house’s.”

“It is on the same page.”

He knew. The record had done that on purpose. A name made a private loss look public; a public complaint made the private household visible. Both sides wanted the paper, and neither wanted the person it described.

“Take the copy to Lina,” he said. “Tell her the household stays out of the public book. If the name is copied, copy the route condition too.”

“You trust the registrar now?”

“No. I trust the page more than the man selling it.”

The courier left. Orren waited until the red scarf disappeared up the stairs, then moved through the kitchen passage toward the tram warehouse.

The false west wall was a row of old lockers built over a service stair. Halloway’s men moved crates through the adjoining alley with the bored efficiency of people who had done dangerous work often enough to stop mistaking it for an emergency. One carried a coil of wire. One carried a ledger. The third carried a counterfeit Fare wrapped in oilcloth.

Orren watched from beneath a broken platform. He counted the men, the wheels, the guards, and the places where the rain made black mirrors under the lamps. He had stolen more records than he could carry, but he had never learned to take a record without looking at the room that kept it.

Halloway stood inside the warehouse office with his back to the door. He was reading a claim to a private buyer, a broad man wearing a dry coat and new boots.

“Your return point is wrong,” Halloway was saying. “The route does not care what your driver calls a home stop.”

“The buyer paid for a home stop.”

“The buyer paid for a road that gets him to the other bank. If he wants a map, he can buy a map.”

“The addendum is not a map.”

“It is the price of using the road.”

The buyer turned a page. “Seven days of repair work.”

“Only if the crossing fails.”

“Do you know whether it will fail?”

Halloway’s answer came too quickly. “I know that the public Wardens will call it a failure before a person has finished crossing.”

Orren moved along the row of lockers, keeping one hand on the wall. A loose floorboard gave beneath his boot. He froze.

The guard nearest the alley looked toward the office.

“Did you hear that?” the guard asked.

“The rain,” Halloway said. “It has been falling for three years.”

The guard turned back. Orren waited until the man’s shoulders relaxed, then slid through a gap behind the office wall. A narrow crawlspace opened into the warehouse ceiling. He dropped onto a stack of empty crates and landed without sound.

The master copy lay on a desk beneath a brass ruler. It was not a token. It was a stack of papers tied with black cord, each sheet bearing the same broken-circle impression near the lower edge. The circle was open at the lower right, as if someone had stopped stamping before the line closed.

Orren leaned closer.

The mark was the same one on the Last Fare’s reverse rim. The same incomplete circle, the same heavy pressure at the upper edge, the same small notch where the stamp had met a flaw in the die. It was not a municipal mark from any office he had seen. The papers carried a regional heading he did not recognize, its place-name rubbed away, and a line that ended in **contested return**.

He copied the heading. Then he copied the broken circle.

A voice behind him said, “If you are stealing, at least take the one worth selling.”

Halloway stood in the office doorway. He had not shouted. His men were not behind him.

Orren kept one hand on the papers. “I don’t sell records.”

“That is not a claim anyone in this room believes.”

“Your buyer is here.”

Halloway looked at the counterfeit claim in the buyer’s hands. “My buyer is here because I made a road he can reach. You are here because you still believe a stolen name can tell you where your mother went.”

Orren’s fingers tightened on the desk. “You know her name.”

“Everyone who has ever handled a bridge form knows somebody’s name.” Halloway stepped closer. “I can give you safe passage through the Wardens. I can give you a warm room and a paper copy that will not vanish when someone decides you are inconvenient. Give me the master and I will let you keep the fragment with Sela’s name.”

Orren looked at the black cord around the stack. The fragment in his coat had been copied from a record that had itself been copied. A name could be evidence. It could also be bait with a family attached.

“Why do you care about my mother?”

“I care that you are standing in a room where I own the exits.” Halloway’s voice softened. “Your anger is useful, but it is not a route.”

Orren slid his hand beneath the top sheet and pulled the black cord loose. The stack scattered. The original bus manifest was beneath it, a larger sheet with a passenger column cut away and a route diagram drawn in a firm hand. It was the whole sheet the torn strip at Lot 17 had come from, and the cut matched the strip’s ragged edge. Someone had lifted the strip during the surge that opened the road. The broken-circle mark crossed the lower corner. In the margin, a faded line read **S. Pike — source record withheld**.

He could have taken the manifest and run. He did.

The false west wall opened. The courier’s key turned in a lock behind the lockers, and a narrow passage appeared where no passage had been. Halloway lunged. Orren kicked a crate into the doorway. The guard fired, not to kill but to make the point, and a pellet struck the wall beside his head.

“Run,” the courier said from the dark.

Orren ran.

They came out through the kitchen cold store, beneath the false floor, into a passage smelling of onions. The courier pulled the door shut behind them and pressed a bundle of paper into Orren’s arms.

“Your mother’s name?”

“Left in the kitchen,” he said. “Not on the road.”

“Lina will know where to put it.”

He did not answer. The broken-circle copy lay against his chest. It was not an answer. It was a shape that had appeared in a place Adrian had never worked, on a record that claimed a place no current map admitted existed.

He did not care yet. He cared that the false claim had named a return point where no one could return, and that its hidden labor toll would fall on a carrier who had been promised a road rather than a repair bill. The school children were named as the people the carrier was supposed to protect, which made the concealed condition worse.

At the top of the stairs, he stopped and looked back.

“Take the manifest to Adrian,” he said. “Take the copy to Mara. Keep the name in the kitchen.”

The courier turned back into the false wall without a word.

Orren tucked the broken-circle paper into his coat and followed the courier’s safe route: the old footbridge over the service cut, then the raised tram approach that did not cross the Fault.

By the time he reached Lot 17, Mara had cleared the back office of everyone except Adrian and Nessa. He laid the original manifest on the table beside the counterfeit claim. The paper was damp at the fold and smelled of lamp oil. Adrian did not touch it until Mara recorded where it had been found, who had recovered it, and the condition of the page.

Orren watched her write. “The name stays out of the public book.”

“For now,” Mara said. “The route condition goes in. If the name is later needed, you will decide whether it is evidence, a repair, or a danger.”

“I will decide.”

Adrian looked at him. “You can bring the evidence to the process. That does not make you its witness.”

“Fine.” Orren’s voice was tired, but he did not leave. “I want the copy read where people can see it.”

Mara slid the counterfeit into a sleeve. “Then bring the copy to the public board. Keep the household out of the room.”

That was the shape of the agreement. It was not trust. It was a place for the paper to go.
