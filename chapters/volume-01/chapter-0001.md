# Chapter 1: The Bus Token

The noon bell at Lot 17 had a flat, iron voice. It usually drew a better crowd than a person ringing it by hand.

This morning rain hammered the patched tram roof and the first commuters were already eating the end of their midday meal out of paper bowls. Wet coats darkened the shoulders of the people packed along the sale rail. The clinic porter in front had porridge on one cuff. A tram driver behind him had tied both boots together to keep one from leaking. At the back, two kitchen workers argued in whispers over whether a cracked coupling pin was worth six coppers or eight.

Adrian Vale struck the bell once more and let the room settle before he raised the first lot.

“Forty-two. Surgical lamp, petrol pressure feed. Works when the weather is kind.”

A small woman near the front shouted, “It works now.”

“It works when the gasket agrees with it,” Adrian said.

He set the lamp on the waist-high inspection block. It was a good lamp in the way that most useful things were good: dented, ugly, and repairable by a person who had not yet ruined it. The pressure housing had been dented along one side, likely when the clinic porter dropped it during a flood move. The lower hinge had been replaced with a copper pin that did not quite match the original steel.

Adrian leaned close. The lamp smelled of hot dust and old oil. He ran a thumbnail around the seam, then set a folding ruler against the housing and looked along it.

“Twelve,” someone offered.

“Fifteen,” the porter called.

The clinic matron at the front did not raise her bid. She said, “The regulator reads steady.”

“It will read steady while the cover is closed.” Adrian tapped the pressure housing with the wooden handle of his ruler. A thin, uneven note came from the left side. Too sharp. Metal under strain made a different sound, one he disliked making. “Threaded neck has a hairline crack. Fuel will weep at the joint once the casing warms. This is not safe for surgery.”

The porter turned. “It was on the manifest as serviceable.”

“The manifest did not carry it across three flooded wards in winter.”

“You lowered the last bus fare because you needed the room,” the matron said. Her voice was quiet, which made it worse. “Now you raise it over a scratch.”

“The crack is real.”

“I can see the scratch.” The matron came forward. Up close, fatigue had bruised the skin beneath her eyes. “Half the clinic’s lights are burned out. Another night without this and I close the cots. You know what that means.”

Adrian did know. Two cots reopening meant two more patients waiting on the river stairs, another family sharing one room, and an extra burden passed to people who had not bought anything at his sale. He looked at the lamp again, willing the flaw to become less.

Nothing changed.

“Six coppers for the empty shell,” he said. “The feed gauge works if you find a repairer. I will not sell you the leak as a light.”

The matron stared at him, then at the scattered crowd. A few people had already begun to turn toward the courier board outside. The morning’s only good news—damp letters from a Ward Nine kitchen exchange, a repaired pump, an occasional tram-seat sale—would not fill a clinic ward.

“Six for the shell,” she said. “And tell the other salvers the rest was overpricing.”

She signed the ticket with a hard slash. Adrian watched her go. It was not a kindness. It was the smallest honest transaction he could offer.

The sale moved faster afterward. Adrian sold four copper feed plates as one lot, rejected a cracked trolley axle, and let a young mechanic take a bundle of signal cable that had been too badly scored for municipal use but would serve in a greenhouse. His clerk, Nessa Pell, called each total in a voice made hoarse by the smoke from the wet-coat stove. By the time the forty-second lot came up, Lot 17 had cleared enough to pay the roof mender and half the promised repair bill for Ward Nine.

Not enough. But enough to postpone the word *insolvent* by another week.

The door burst open.

A messenger stood in the rain, one hand holding a folded clinic slip and the other clamped around a bicycle lamp that had not been lit. His coat was older than the weather. Water ran from the hem and made a dark pool across the sale floor.

“Fever shipment,” he said. “The whole shipment. It’s sitting on the wrong side of St. Orra.”

The room’s murmur changed pitch.

Adrian set down his pencil. “The official bridge?”

“Closed again by Selik Marne. Not damaged. Closed. He says the old approach will take three days to inspect and the fever cases have one day to live.” The messenger held up the slip. “The clinic sent me to find a broker who can do better.”

A murmur moved through the crowd. Someone said, “There is the second underpass.” Another voice answered, “There is no second underpass. There is a sink under it.”

“Orra can open,” said a man near the door. Everyone looked at him.

He was thin and rain-dark, wearing a salvage coat with too many buckles. He had the practiced stillness of someone who preferred other people to make the first move. Adrian knew Halloway Reed by reputation and had never yet met the man behind it.

“Can it?” Adrian asked.

“Somebody said so.”

“Somebody also said the coupling pins were brass.”

A few people laughed. Halloway’s eyes moved to Adrian, not the crowd. The amusement did not reach him.

The messenger’s bicycle lamp guttered in the wet. He said, “The clinic will pay what it can. The market cooperative will cover the rest. Everyone wants the same thing.”

No, Adrian thought. The clinic wanted fever medicine. The cooperative wanted a route it could depend on. The people in the room wanted to feel that possession of a thing was the same as control over it. Those wants were related only in the way a spill and a broken cup were related.

He tried to restore order. “The lot is sold. The sale continues. No one is offering a crossing on the floor.”

A boy came along the front rail then, slipping between coats with a runner’s ease. He was seventeen or near it, thin through the wrists and burdened with a satchel stuffed past its seams with paper. One shoelace was tied around his wrist. He looked at Adrian’s inspection block, not the crowd, and crouched as if to retrieve something beneath it.

When he rose, a brass token and a torn strip of manifest paper were under the block.

“Delivery,” the boy said quietly.

Adrian moved his hand over the token before anyone else could see it.

The token was no larger than a thumb joint. Its rim had been worn smooth on one side, and a municipal stamp still showed beneath the tarnish: an old tram wheel crossed by three lines. The brass was warm, though no one had carried it close to a stove. The manifest was damp at the edges, its passenger and cargo columns ripped away. A line near the bottom read **ST. ORRA — EAST PLATFORM — LINE 6**.

The boy looked toward the messenger. Then back at Adrian.

“Found it in a bus,” he said. “The bus went over before the cut closed.”

“Who sent you?”

“Found it,” the boy repeated, and slipped away toward the kitchen-door exit.

Adrian could have called after him. Instead he picked up the token.

The metal did not feel cold. It felt too complete, as if every part of it had been waiting for the same missing piece. The old stamp flashed once in the rain-light. A few lines of black writing appeared on the inside of his perception, small and plain, with no sound and no visible light on the brass itself.

```text
LOT: THE LAST FARE
USE: ONE ROUTE ACROSS ST. ORRA.
TERM: TWELVE MINUTES.
RETURN POINT NOT NAMED.
```

The words stayed for three heartbeats. Then they faded.

Adrian set the token down. His hand did not shake. That was how he knew how frightened he was. A faint taste of old coins had left the back of his tongue. He swallowed it away and wrote the sensation in the private ledger, because a thing that could make a body lie was not a thing to sell on impulse.

The boy had gone. The messenger was still at the door, and the crowd had begun to turn toward the sale block as if the next lot might be the one that solved St. Orra. Halloway watched Adrian from beneath the brim of his cap.

“All right,” Adrian said. “Who was bid on the clinic lamp?”

He sold the lamp’s shell for six coppers, then turned the brass token over with his folding ruler. No hinge, no seam, no place where a spring could hide. Its edge bore a fine tool mark at twelve points. It was a real transit token. It was also too heavy for the old municipal pattern, and the wear was not the ordinary wear of a fare paid at a public platform. Someone had handled it at a private threshold, or a threshold that had not been there before the Fracture.

He took a strip of red cloth from his coat and tied it around the token.

“What is that lot?” Nessa asked.

“Private inspection.”

“Price?”

“Not for sale.”

The room’s noise rose. Halloway stepped away from the door, and the messenger looked from one broker to another as if waiting to see which one would lower the first price.

Adrian slid the token and the torn manifest into an inner pocket. Then he went into the back office, where the depot lamps had been failing for six years and a public record could not be watched without a witness.

He set the token on his desk beside a blank lot card. The red cloth made a dark stain against the old wood.

A road with no end had entered the sale. The only thing he could not put on it was a price.
