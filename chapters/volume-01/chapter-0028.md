# Chapter 28: The Second Backwash

Orren Pike had worked out by the end of the thirtieth that the counterfeit was winning, and he did it with arithmetic rather than an opinion, because arithmetic was the only instrument he had that Adrian Vale had never once been able to dismiss.

Three brokers, and all three of them were carrying the one printing plate with identical miscut type, which he had proved in a yard in front of forty people and which not one of them had denied. Eleven claims in circulation that he knew of, of which four had been refused at a carrier's hands and he could name all four refusals. That was eleven, and a man could refuse eleven.

But at half past eight on the night of the thirtieth he counted the claims in the three barrow yards he could get into, which were the river stairs, the arcades, and the lower end of the tram apron, and he counted nineteen, and he stopped counting at twenty-one because his hand was going and because the ones he was not counting were on the terraces where a man with a wax stick and a shaking hand could sell all night.

Twenty-one, against eleven, in a week, after a public road had failed on camera in front of two hundred people.

"It is not a police problem," he said, to nobody, under the tram line, with his satchel against his chest. "A police problem is eleven. This is a *demand* problem. Nineteen people are holding a piece of paper that says they are allowed to cross a river, and it is cheaper than the footbridge marshal, and it is on the first page, and there is nothing wrong with the first page. That is the whole trick and it is why it is winning. The first page is honest. It is the fold that is a lie, and the fold is *inside*."

Tove Alard was sitting on the flour crates with a sheet of newsprint over her knees and a pen in her hand and she had been taking the claims since seven.

"Seventeen is the same plate," she said. "Nineteen is not. Nineteen is a man's own writing, all the way through, and it is worse, and here is why it is worse—"

"Read me the first line of nineteen."

"Reads, *one route across the east reach of the cross cut, carrier and load, term of five days.*"

"And that is true."

"And that is true, and it is a lie in the ninth line, and it is not a lie in the fold because there is no fold." Tove put the pen down. "Orren. Nineteen is a man who has written his own lie, and I can teach a carter to spot a fold in about a minute, because folds are always in the same three places. I cannot teach a carter to spot a lie in nine lines in about a minute, because that is *reading*, and reading takes years, and you are seventeen."

"Yes," Orren said. "That is what has happened, and you have just said the thing I have been walking toward for four days, and I am going to say it now so that you can tell me if I have it right, because you are better at this than me and always have been."

He got the fourth page out of the satchel.

It was not a warning. Warnings had been tried — he had printed eleven of the third page in the last week of the flood year and put one in front of Sabin Tull at half past four in the morning, and one warning had stopped one claim, and a warning is a thing that has to arrive before the person decides, and a person holding a barrow at four in the morning has decided an hour ago.

The fourth page was a *comparison*. Two things printed side by side, both of them set by hand, both of them in the same size, and the right-hand one was a claim with a fold opened out flat underneath it so that the ninth line was visible:

**CLAIM IN HAND.** *Route across the east reach of the cross cut, carrier and load, term five days. No second crossing stated. Carrier's labour not mentioned.*
**WHAT THE CARRIER IS SIGNING.** *Seven days' repair labour at the southern bank, payable to the claim's holder, folded inside the second fold, not payable in coin, not refusable after the carrier has moved the first load.*

"You are not printing a warning," Tove said. "You are printing a *second page*."

"Every claim gets a second page," Orren said. "Anybody can bring a claim to a table and get the other half of it set in front of them, in the yard they are standing in, in the light they are standing in, by somebody who is not selling anything. And that is not a warning, and it is not the board, and it is not a hearing, and nobody in Alder Reach is going to be frightened into coming and find it. It has to be somewhere it can be *got to* at four in the morning, which means a table, on a street, with a lamp, and somebody on it every night, and the somebody has to be somebody the carrier already knows, or it will be a warning again."

"And where is the somewhere," said Bett Ruhl, from behind the third flour barrel, where she had been listening for some time.

Orren had been ready for that question since the twenty-fourth and he had still not got an answer for it, and he stood in his own kitchen exchange and said so, which cost him something he had not budgeted for.

"You cannot print the address," he said.

"No," Bett Ruhl said. "I cannot. You know why I cannot, and you have known since you were fifteen and you put your mother's name in a different sentence on a different page, and I have never once thanked you for it and I am not going to start."

"So the service has no address."

"The service has an address to about forty people and a description to the rest of them, and the description is *the place with the tea*, and there are four places with the tea in Ward Nine and the people who need it will find the right one, and the people who do not need it will not find any of them." She came out from behind the barrel with her arms folded, which she did instead of raising her voice. "And here is the thing you have not thought about, boy, and you have been thinking about everything else. If the fourth page is printed, then anybody can be taught the nine lines. And anybody can be taught the nine lines means that the shape is public. And when the shape is public, the next man does not fold it, because the next man has read about the fold. He writes it out in nine flat lines where you can see it, and the carter signs it, because it is on the page and they all sign things on the page."

"Yes," Orren said.

"So what you have actually built," Bett Ruhl said, "is the end of the only advantage the small people have got, which is that the cheat has to be hidden to work, and you have made it uncheatable by teaching it, and that is a *good* thing, and it is also the reason that the only way to stop a man with nine flat honest-looking lines is to have somebody standing next to him at four in the morning saying read it, and that somebody has to be a person, and the person has to be fed, and the person cannot be a child of seventeen with a satchel and a grudge."

Tove Alard put her pen down.

"I can read," she said. "I can set it and I can read it and my mother moves a barrow out of the river stairs and there are nine other barrows behind hers, and I am not frightened of Hask Vell."

"That is not—" Orren started.

"That is not yours to decide," Tove said. "You have been deciding for people all week and it is the only thing I have ever seen you do that is actually good at you."

---

They set it up at the tram arch, at four o'clock in the morning, in the same hour the claims got bought, because that was the hour and Orren had checked. Three nights running to start with, because there is no point in a service that has to be permanent to be worth anything and he did not have the right to ask anybody for permanent.

The table was Marda Osk's, which she gave on the condition that the fourth page be printed on the *back* of the ward market's own blank claim stock, which the ward market had been giving out for nine years and could not stop without admitting it had been giving out the paper somebody else printed the lie on.

That was Orren's idea, and it was the best thing he did that month, and he did not think of it as a good idea for about four days.

Tove set the two halves. A cooper's apprentice who had come with his master's barrow read out the ninth line of a claim in his own hand and his own words, and then Orren read the other half, and then the apprentice said whether the two matched, and they mostly matched, and when they did not the apprentice said so in a flat voice and the carrier decided.

Twenty-two claims in three nights. In fifteen of them the two halves matched. Six did not, and of those six, four were refused at the table and two walked away before the apprentice got to the ninth line, and the twenty-second claim had an error nobody had printed yet, which Tove put on the fourth page on the fourth night with Orren's name spelled wrong.

And on the second night a carter from the arcades asked whether the page could be got for a woman who could not read, and Tove said no and Orren said yes, and they argued about it for eleven minutes in front of a lamp, and the answer they arrived at was that somebody had to read it *aloud*, which is what Marda Osk has been doing at a gate for three weeks for nothing, which is why the table is not a piece of paper at all but Marda Osk with a lamp.

---

And then, later on the second night, at about half past four, a carter called Joss Amery came up the tram apron with a claim in his hand and a man behind him who had brought a lamp, and he had bought the claim at the terraces three days before, and he had not brought it to the table, and nobody had sent him to the table, and he had a reason, and the reason was that his wife had been taken to the fever ward on the far bank on the Thursday and there were nine cots and a queue and he had a barrow-load of clinic stores and a claim that said the road was his.

"This is the one," Nell Sarr said quietly. She was on the apron because she had started coming at four in the morning, which was not her job and which nobody had stopped her from doing. "This is the one that does not have a fold. Tove, look at it."

"I have looked at it," Tove said. "It is nine flat lines and every one of them is checkable and there is nothing in it to check."

"So it is a good claim," Nell Sarr said.

"It is a good claim," Tove said, "and the return point in it is the old salt-loading ramp on the far side, which has been under nine feet of water since before the Fracture, and if he takes two men and a barrow over the footbridge at five in the morning without a marshal, because his claim says the crossing is his and does not need asking, then he will get himself to a flooded maintenance trench with a barrow of clinic stores in it, and it will not be anybody's fault but the claim's, and there is nobody to blame, and that is the whole difficulty."

Nobody had blamed anybody. That was the finding, and it took a week.

---

What happened at about five in the morning at the south end of the service cut was this.

There was no marshal, because a man with a claim does not need a marshal. There were two men and a barrow, and the footbridge is an old thing with a plank deck and a handrail on one side only, and the cut underneath it is the place where the ground has been arguing with itself for seventeen years and has never once settled on a side.

The barrow went through the rail at the third plank. The man on the end of it went into the cut with it. The other man got back up onto the bank and ran for four hundred yards in the wrong direction looking for a lamp.

They found the trench at about twenty past five, because the claim's return point is a real place with a real name on a pre-Fracture document and a document that old is not wrong about where a thing is. Nine feet of water and a flooded maintenance trench and a barrow of clinic stores floating.

Joss Amery got the man out. He could not get the barrow out and did not try, and the stores went, and that was on him and on the claim and nobody else.

---

The notice board that changed was not Lot 17's.

It was a shelter board at the top of the river stairs — a coal ration board, hand-ruled, chalked every morning for eleven years, and a Warden had been through and initialled it every day since the flood — and the change was in the third column, on the fourth line, where a household of five had been listed as drawing in a week for a man who was not there because he was on the far bank.

On the second of the month the line read **HOLE, 5, 1 WK**.
On the third of the month it read **HOLE, 5, 1 WK**.

And on the morning of the second, when Bett Ruhl's exchange opened, Joss Amery was on the step of the third stair with his coat open, and he could not get past the third word of a sentence, and he was not a man with a bad head.

He had three days.

He was a carter and he had had a barrow out on the night of the first and had come back on the morning of the second and had done his rounds and had eaten and had slept and had got up on the third and had a wife on the far bank, and the fourth of the month came and he could not have told anybody, in a courtroom or otherwise, what he had done on the second or the third, and when somebody said *the barrow* he said *what barrow*, and when Bett Ruhl said *Joss, you have got a barrow and a half load of clinic stores in you somewhere* he said, with a terrible patience, "then I would rather somebody found them than me."

Three days. Not damaged, not softened, not gone in a way anybody could describe. Three whole days simply not there, in a man who moves a barrow out of the river stairs and has done for nine years, and nobody can put a claim on a thing they cannot point at.

And the household on the third stair cannot ask him anything about those two days. That is the part Orren could not get past, and it is the part that made the argument for a service into something he did not want to make. A person who has lost three days cannot be examined. And a household with a man in that condition cannot be left alone with it, and the people who would ordinarily do the examining are the ones with a form.

---

Joss Amery's wife was on the far bank and had been for four days and came home on the fifth, on the footbridge, in daylight, in a marshal's care, with nine other people, because the old footbridge is still open and somebody was walking it.

Orren went and sat in the exchange with his fourth page and did not print it, and Bett Ruhl came and sat down on the flour crates opposite him and asked him what he was going to do.

"Print the third page again," he said. "The one that says what is in the fold."

"No," Bett Ruhl said. "There is no fold."

"Then print a page about the return point."

"No," Bett Ruhl said. "Because the return point is a name on a document and the name is correct, and if you print a page that says *do not use a return point* then in six weeks a man will write a claim with a wrong return point on it and people will think the danger is the name on the page, and it is the man with the barrow at two in the morning. There is no list of bad things in this trade. There is a person at four in the morning, and that is all there has ever been, and every fool who has ever come through that door trying to fix it with paper has made it worse."

"Then what do I print."

"Nothing," Bett Ruhl said. "You come to the table, and you stand at it, and you read the other half out loud, and if a man cannot read then somebody stands next to him and reads it and he says yes or no, and that is the whole service, and it is a *person*, and it costs a lamp and a pot and four hours of somebody's night, and it is not paper and it cannot be posted and it cannot be found."

"That is not good enough," Orren said.

"No," Bett Ruhl said, "it is not. And it is what there is, and I will tell you the price of it, because you are seventeen and you have not asked and that is the first thing wrong with you. The price is me. It is my kitchen and my third barrel and my floor and about nine hours a week of a woman who has not slept properly since the year before the flood. And I am doing it because of the third stair, and not because you are right, and if the ward market ever asks me where the service is I will tell them it is at a place we do not write down, and I will not feel bad about that, and neither should you, and if you print one line on one of your pages with my floor in it I will put the press in the river and I will do it in front of you."

Orren looked at her for a long moment.

"Understood," he said.

"Say it better."

"No address," Orren said. "No address, and I will not print the third stair, and if anybody asks me where the table is I will say it is at a place we do not write down, and I will say it in exactly those words so that the two of us are saying the same sentence, and if somebody takes that as a threat then they have understood the trade, and if they take it as a favour then they have not and they should go and read the fourth page instead."

"Good," Bett Ruhl said. "Now go and print the back of the ward market's stock, and eat something, and if you ever write the word *Joss Amery* on a page in this kitchen I will take the press apart myself."

---

Halloway Reed came down the tram apron on the third of the month at about ten in the morning and stood in front of a notice board at Lot 17 on which somebody had taped up the fourth page, and read it, from the top, the way he read everything, all the way down, twice.

When he had finished he turned round, and there were perhaps thirty people in the yard and about ninety at the gate, and he did not raise his voice, because he had never in six years needed to.

"Two nights ago a carter out of the river stairs went over a footbridge in the dark with no marshal and lost three days and a barrow of clinic stores," he said. "And there is a page on that wall that anybody can read, and it is a good page, and the boy who made it is seventeen and honest. And I am going to say something about it that everybody in this yard is going to find irritating, and I am going to say it out loud, and then I am going to sell a claim in this yard this morning, and both of those things are true and neither of them stops the other one."

He held up a sheet.

"Nineteen claims a week ago, at three brokers, and a plate. That is a criminal trade, and it is not a criminal trade *any more*, and Mr. Vale's book is the reason, and I have read the book. When a public road fails in front of two hundred people and the man who ran it publishes the whole of it, with a list of the nine things it did not contain, then every man in Alder Reach learns that paper is a thing that can be checked, and the first thing a checked paper does to a dishonest trade is make it honest and make it cheap. It puts the second page on the table. And a man who can read the second page does not need a dishonest offer. He needs a *fast* one. And the fast one is mine, and mine is on the first page, and it is fifteen days, and it falls on me, and it is more expensive than the fold, and it is honest, and I have never once pretended it is not a better offer than the fold for anybody who can read a page."

Somebody in the yard said, "Then stop selling it."

"I will not," Halloway said. "And here is the sentence I came down here to say, and I would like the yard to have it in the right words. **Your public road failed and the counterfeit got more credible in the same week, and both of those are true, and the reason is that your road failed for a reason a person could not check, and the reason it failed for a reason a person could not check is that the person who ran it put the thing that could not be checked in a field and called the field a term.**" He looked at the page on the wall. "That page is worth more than my warehouse and I am going to say so out loud in a yard, because a man who will not say a thing like that in public is selling the same fold with a better coat on."

He unrolled the sheet on the crate.

"Fifteen days, on the first page, and I read it out loud to anybody who wants it read, and the answer to a carter who cannot read is that I will read it to him myself and he may bring his wife and if I have lied to either of them he keeps the load and I pay it. That is my offer, and it is available this morning, and there is no public road, and Mr. Vale has told this yard himself that he cannot open one, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that my offer is a way of being good. It is a way of being quick and legible and mine."

He looked along the rail until he found him.

"Mr. Vale. You published the failure and it cost you a workshop, and I mean that, and I am not going to insult you by pretending it balances anything. But you published a *road that failed*, and a road that fails in public is a road everybody can check, and a road everybody can check is the only thing in this city that is going to beat me, and it is not going to beat me because of the book. It is going to beat me because of the four hundred people who read it and can now say, out loud, in a yard, that they will not sign with me because they can check the next one. You did that. In eleven days, from a stool, with a bad hand and a good eye, you have built the only thing I have been afraid of since I started, and I have been in this trade six years and nobody has ever built it, and I would like it noted in your book that I said so at ten in the morning on the third of the month, in a yard, before I sold a single thing."

"Say it in the book," Adrian said, from the rail, in a voice with nothing in it that anybody standing there could name. "All of it. Including that you sold a claim in my yard this morning."

"Especially that," Halloway Reed said.
