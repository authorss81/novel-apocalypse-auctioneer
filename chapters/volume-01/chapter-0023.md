# Chapter 23: The Maintenance Spur

The news came in through the gate at ten past six in the evening, from a Warden on a bicycle who had been sent out of the yard by a man who was the most junior person in it and who had got it wrong in a way that happened to work.

"There is an engine running in Callow Street," the boy said, "behind the old laundry wall, in the yard that has the service gate."

Behind the service gate, four streets west of the depot, was the near-side maintenance spur.

Nobody in Lot 17 had ever called it that in public, and Adrian understood, in the second it took him to get his coat, that he had never once said the words *maintenance spur* out loud in a yard, and that a book with an empty place in it is the only place a thing like that can be a secret.

"What kind of engine," he said.

The boy, who was twenty and named Wick and was extremely pleased to have been the one who was sent, said, "It is a bus engine. It has been running since we started. Me and Coll went past the gate at six and Dad says the sound comes up the drain, and we could not see anything because the gate is shut, and it is a bus, Mr. Vale, it is a *bus* engine."

---

The service gate was thirty feet high and it had not been opened since the flood year, and there was a hole in the brick beside it about as wide as a man's shoulders, and the yard inside it was a yard that had been a yard before it had been a service cut, with a service wall at the back of it and a ramp down and a bus standing on the ramp with its nose against the wall.

The engine was running.

That was the first thing Adrian understood and the thing he understood worst. Not a bus that had stopped. A bus that was *idling*, with the sound coming up through a grating, after eleven hours and forty minutes, in a locked yard four streets west of a public lot, with nobody in it.

They got in through the hole. Selik's marshal went first with a lamp, and Adrian went second because there was no argument to be had about it, and Nessa Pell went third because she had decided, in front of the yard, that if a thing was found it would be written down by somebody who was there when it was found, and Adrian had not argued.

The hole was the width of a man's shoulders and about three feet off the ground, and it took Adrian eleven minutes to get through it with his coat on, and he had to go in on his side with his arms above his head, and forty years of carrying crates through the front doors of auction rooms had not prepared him for it. On the way out, with a child in his arms, he put his right shoulder against the brick on both sides and felt the wall move a quarter inch under his hand, which is nothing, which is a wall, which is the ordinary behaviour of a wall in a district where the ground has been arguing with itself for seventeen years, and which made him sit down on the flat afterwards for a while without meaning to.

The bus was the Road House bus. There was no question. It had Tarin's colour on the near side, because Tarin had painted it himself in the fourth year of owning it and had painted it badly, and the wheel that Tarin had changed himself three weeks earlier was on it, new, with the grease still on the hub.

Its headlights were off. Its doors were open. The fare box above the driver's seat had its slot cover hanging on one screw. The route-control socket was empty.

And the engine was running at a low idle, and the bonnet was warm, and there was nobody in the driver's seat.

"Oh God," said Wick, from behind Adrian's shoulder, in a whisper, and Adrian turned round and made him stand behind the marshal, and did not tell him why.

---

They came out of it the way people come out of a place they have been in for a long time.

The first was the steward, and she came out backwards, and she was the escort steward of the third, and she had been on that bus since nine minutes past in the morning, and she was grey-faced and absolutely steady, and she came out backwards because she had been sitting in the seat with the arm across the door and she did not trust herself to turn round in a space that narrow.

"Eight," she said, before anybody asked her anything. "I have eight. Six of the children and the driver and the clinic woman, and I have had all eight since nine minutes past, and I want it said in that order because I was on that bus with six of them and I am the only grown thing on it that came out of the Fault whole, and I would like that in the book in that order."

"Say it again at the rail," Nessa said, and wrote it down anyway, on her knee, in the open, by lamp.

The six children came down the steps one at a time, holding the rail with hands that were white to the second knuckle, and they came down the way the eight came down off a bus a fortnight earlier, which is the way people come down who have been sitting since before they were cold and have only just discovered it.

They were blue. That was the word Adrian kept coming back to and could never get to the bottom of in a way that satisfied him: not pale, not shivering, blue, in the lips, in the nails, in the ridge along the outside of the hands where the blood had gone and stopped caring.

The steward had the school roll. It was folded inside her coat against her body, and it was eleven days old, and she had it still.

"I did not have a blanket," she said, to nobody in particular, in a voice with nothing in it. "I want that said before anybody is kind to me. There was a barrow on the platform and I took the barrow, and I put the six of them around it and I put the two smallest under the sacking and I had no blanket and I had no fire and I am not saying it to be forgiven, I am saying it because it is a fact and I am the only person who can say it and I have had eleven hours to think of a better answer."

"Get blankets," Adrian said. "Get every blanket in this building and get them to that ramp and do not send one of them walking to find a blanket."

"And the barrow's wheel is off," Wick said. "It went off coming down the ramp. I did not touch it. I want to say that I did not touch it, because I heard somebody say my name about a barrow in a yard this afternoon and it was not this barrow and I would like that on the record as well."

---

The clinic woman was Dessa Rill and she came out of the bus last, on her own, without a hand on the rail, and she walked down the ramp and across a service yard on a day that had taken her the memory of why she drives.

She was the only one who looked afraid, and Adrian understood about ten minutes later that the reason she looked afraid was not the cold.

"You came down the spur on foot," he said.

"I came out of the bus on the spur."

"You came down the spur." He said it again, because Nessa was writing, and because he had a feeling that he was about to hear something he would need to have heard precisely. "Dessa. The route came up. Where did it come up?"

"It came up," she said. "There was a service cut, and the wall on the left was not a wall, and there was a smell of hot oil and wet brick, and I put my hand on the seat in front of me because I did not know where we were, and then it was a yard, and the engine was running, and there was a gate and the gate was shut." She stopped. "I got the children off the bus. There were six of them. I am a driver and I know how to get children off a bus, and I do that, and I have done that four times this week, and I did it, and then I stood in the yard and waited for a man to come, because I did not know who was coming, and I do not know why I was waiting for him, Mr. Vale, and that is the part I cannot give you."

Adrian stood in a service yard in the dark and understood that he had just been given the exact shape of what was missing from her, in her own words, in the plainest words anybody had used about it in three weeks.

"Then say this," he said. "Carefully. In the words you are about to say them to the book. Do you still know the route?"

"I know Line Six. I know the wall on the left is not a wall and you do not touch it and it agrees with you for about a length of a bus. I know green is the water and red is lamps and I go on red. I know where the far receiving ramp is and the roof of stacked boards and the lamp under it and the handcart." She was looking at the ground. "I do not know why I am the one who drives the medicine. That is the thing that has gone. I have been in the yard for eleven hours waiting for a man and I have been waiting for somebody to tell me I am the wrong person here, and I am not, and I can hold the place in my head, and I cannot tell you what the place is *for*, and I have been a driver for nineteen years and this morning I found out that the job and the reason for the job are two separate things and I have only ever had the one."

"And the name of the man on the far side who stands under the lamp with the cart," Nessa said, from the light. "Is it there?"

"No."

"That is what we expected," Nessa said. "Write it down exactly as she said it and do not improve it."

---

They got the first child down the ramp and across the flat and up to the marshal's lamp and then the second, and then somebody said a name and Adrian turned round.

The steward had gone back up the three steps.

"Get her off," she said. "Get her off, she is the blue one, she has been the blue one since about two o'clock and I have been saying her name at her for two hours and she has been answering me and she has been answering me the way a person answers when they are not in the room."

Adrian went up the three steps and got a nine-year-old off a bus, and she weighed nothing, that was the thing he could not get over afterwards, she weighed nothing at all, and he carried her down the ramp and across the yard and up onto the flat where the marshal had the lamp, and Nessa Pell took one end of the blanket and he took the other and they got her onto a handcart and made the handcart move, and the Road House wheel man came up beside him and did not say one word and took the other end of the cart and pushed, because there were nine hundred people on the far side of Alder Reach who had been told by a man with a clean hand to move, and the wheel man had been one of the ones who moved, and he had not needed to be asked.

"Take her to the depot," Adrian said. "Take her to the depot and put her by the stove and get Halva Sen's people out of the clinic and get somebody who can sit with her and say her own name at her, and do not stop, and do not let anybody put her down to rest because she looks asleep."

"Mr. Vale," Nessa said.

"Go."

The wheel man went.

And Adrian stood in a service yard with eight people out of a bus and nine found, and there was a fourth street to walk.

---

They found Tarin Kest at twenty past eight, at the top of a service cut on the far side of the wall from the maintenance spur, sitting on a length of rail with his back against a pier and a sheet of oiled paper round his shoulders that he had taken off the bus.

He was on his feet, which was the first thing Adrian registered, and it took him a moment to understand why that was a thing to register, and it was because Tarin Kest was a large man and had been outside since a quarter past seven in the morning and had walked four streets in the cold in wet clothes, and a large man on his feet at that point in the day is not a thing anybody expects.

His mouth and his fingers were white. He could not stand up without putting a hand on the pier.

He had a sheet of paper in his inside pocket and Adrian knew before anybody opened it what was on it, because Adrian had watched the page that thing was cut from.

Tarin got it out himself, with two fingers, and could not unfold it, and gave it to Adrian.

**FIFTEEN. THE RETURN.** Second rail east of the bell post, under the lamp, where the ballast drops and the plate is stamped, in the bus of Tarin Kest, coming down from the far bank.

**The driver is authorised to hold the line, take the outer shoulder, and come down by any route he can see. If the far side is not a road, he stops the bus and he gets out and he walks back toward the wall, and nobody follows him onto it.**

In the middle of the second paragraph, in a hand that was not Tarin's, in pencil, and pressed hard enough to have gone through the paper onto the dash of the bus:

**7.38. The cut is not a road. Everybody off. Nobody follows me.**

"Twenty-three minutes to eight," Adrian said.

"Nineteen of it was in the cut," Tarin said. "I came up out of it at eight minutes to eight and I did not know the yard and I did not know the street and I could feel the gate on the left and I put the left wheels on the plate and I went along it, and the gate is the bus's own decision and it is a good decision and it brought us into a yard and it shut behind us, and then I looked down and the marker was pointing along the spur."

He stopped, and it was a long stop, and Adrian did not help him through it.

"I could not find it," Tarin said. "I want to be very clear about that, because I know what it sounds like. I have missed that joint by nothing in my life and I could find it with the lights off with no reason in my head at all. And I came up out of a service cut on the wrong side of the wall with the arm pointing the other way, and I could not find it. Not late. Not four yards. Not at all. I stood in a bus with the engine running for about four minutes and looked at it and it was not the place and it was not anywhere."

"You stopped the bus."

"I stopped the bus." He said it like a man laying something down that he had been carrying up a hill. "And I got out, and I did the thing on the paper, and I walked back toward the wall. Four streets. And I have been sitting here since about half past eight in the afternoon because I could not get warm and I did not want to walk into a yard with a hundred people in it and no road in front of me, and I have been sitting here thinking about it, and I want to say the part that is going to be in the book and I want to say it now, while you are all standing there in your coats, because in an hour I will have talked myself into a better sentence."

He looked up at Adrian.

"It was the correct thing to do. That is what I want said. Not that I was clever. I did not stop because I was clever. I stopped because the far side of that cut was not the road I left on and the clause says to stop, and I have spent three weeks telling a registrar that the clause is worth more than the road." He coughed, and it took him a while. "And I want the yard to hear the rest of it, which is that the clause is worth more than the road and it is not free and it did not cost eleven hours. It cost eleven hours, and it cost four streets, and it cost six children in a bus in a locked yard with a woman who had no blanket and no fire for eleven hours, and one of those six is on a handcart on the way to your depot right now, and if I had got that bus to the joint on the marked spur on the strength of my own memory I would have got it to the joint four yards further along and the arm would have been where it was and I would have taken nine people past the place they were supposed to stop, and one of the two things that is true is that nobody can ever say that again, and the other one is that a child is not waking up in your depot tonight because of a sentence I insisted on having."

Nobody in that cut said anything for some seconds. There was a lamp and a rail and a sheet of oiled paper with pencil through it.

"Write it exactly like that," Adrian said to Nessa. "Both halves, in that order, and put my name on it as the person who paid forty hours of a mechanic's labour to make that clause the standing rule of a public lot, because that is what forty hours bought and I would like the yard to be able to find out what it was for."

Then he crouched down, which he had not done in four days, and looked at the bell-post arm from four yards away in a lamp, from inside the thing.

The hinge was clean where a hand had been in it, on the inside of the socket, in a clean arc about two fingers wide, and the iron under it had begun to go again in one night of rain, exactly as Nell Sarr had said at six o'clock that evening on a rail in a yard.

"There is a rail joint here," Adrian said, to the lamp more than to anybody. "There is a rail joint four yards short of that post and there is a second one and there is a third, and there is ballast that drops away, and there is a plate. That is the return point. Not the arm. The arm is a piece of municipal iron with a handle on it and the rule says a landmark is not a return point because a person with a spanner can move it, and I wrote that rule into this lot's standing and the rule was right, and the reason we are all standing in a drain four streets west of a public lot at half past eight at night is that the arm moved."

"And who moved it?" Nell Sarr said from the top of the ramp, where she had arrived with a lamp and a face like a closed door.

"Nobody knows," Adrian said. "That is the answer and I am going to give it to you now, in this cut, out loud, so that I cannot be given a better one later. I do not know who moved it. I do not know how it was moved. I do not know why. I have had six hours to want it to be a person, because a person I can be angry at and a thing I cannot, and I have been at it all afternoon and the best I have is a boot."

"And the boot?"

"Nineteen people walked a line and found one nailed heel going toward the river wall and it is not one of ours and it is older than this morning." He stood up, which took him longer than it should have. "Put in the book: *the marking moved; the marking was moved by a hand; no hand is identified; the reason for the arm being turned is not known; the reader declines to record a cause and will record any cause proposed by another person in that book as that other person's proposal and not as a finding.* And if I have to read the word *sabotage* or *enemy* or *Tally* in that book somebody is going to have to stand up in this yard and say that they put it there."

---

The nine were on Lot 17's rail at twenty past nine at night, which is where the count happened, and the count was read out because Mara Quill had stood up in the middle of it and said that the only thing she was going to do with the rest of the night was insist on a number read out in a room, and Adrian had not wanted to argue with her about it.

"Nine found," Nessa Pell said, holding the lamp up so the book was in the light. "Tarin Kest, found on foot at the top of a service cut four streets from the maintenance spur. Dessa Rill, found at the spur. One escort steward, found at the spur, marked and on a sealed sheet, not named in this book. Six of the children of the second shelter group, found at the spur, marked and on sealed sheets, not named in this book. Nine found, nine unaccounted-for resolved, and none of them is a name this book is entitled to print."

"Read the twenty-eight," Mara said.

"Twenty-eight people travelled on the nineteenth. Twenty-nine were offered and one child refused and the refusal is deducted aloud. Nineteen were on the far bank. Nine were on the bus. Nineteen and nine is twenty-eight, and the twenty-eight is closed."

"And the tolls."

"Twenty-eight tolls on the outward leg of the nineteenth, and no return leg, because there was no return leg, because the charter has no field in which to record one." Nessa's voice did not go anywhere unusual. "Nobody in this yard has paid a return toll. The road has not been paid. That is the state of the account and Mr. Vale can tell you what it means and I would rather he said it than me, because it is not my road and I am not the one who left the field out."

Adrian stood on the rail with his hands in his pockets and said it.

"It means the token is cracked through the plate, and the route has no condition in it, and the thing the return was supposed to cost has not been paid, and the object in the case on this crate told two hundred people at ten past nine this morning that it does not know either. So the account is open. I do not know what an unpaid return toll does. I have a reading of that token and it is four lines long and it has been four lines long for eleven days, and none of the four lines is about this."

---

The child got to the depot at a quarter past ten and was on the floor by the stove with three blankets and Halva Sen's people and Nessa Pell's lamp, and she was alive, and her name went onto a sealed sheet because the steward gave it into Mara Quill's ear and Mara Quill wrote it and signed the corner and did not say it in the room, and the sealed sheet went into Mara's coat.

She did not wake.

Adrian stayed until two. He did not read the token and he did not touch the bus and he did not go out to the yard, and the two hours were the emptiest he had spent since the north stair at twenty-one, and at about half past midnight he understood what the two hours were for, which was that there was nothing in them that he could do.

At some point in the first hour she stopped breathing the way a sleeping child breathes, and one of Halva Sen's people said so, and the other one said no she had not, and Adrian got up and went out to the yard and stood under the lamps over the public board and looked at the yard in the rain with nothing to do.

At some point in the second hour a sheet of paper came up on the notice board at Lot 17 and nobody in the building heard it, and the first person to see it was Nessa Pell at ten past four in the morning, when she came out to open the shutters and found Teo's complaint gone from the middle of the board and something else in its place.

She read it in the yard lamp and then went and woke Adrian, and when he came out he was still in the coat he had not taken off.

It was in his handwriting. He knew it before he was two feet from it, because he had been writing that hand for twenty-two years and there is nobody in the world who can tell you what your own hand looks like at four in the morning but you.

Four lines, on the depot's own notice paper, in the middle of the board, where the complaint had been:

**SECURITY OFFERED ON THE CHARTER OF THE NINETEENTH.**
**INSPECTION PRESS AT LOT 17, THREE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY BY WARD MARKET.**
**REPAIR-CREW TERM, ONE SEASON, THREE NAMED HANDS.**
**ENTERED IN THE PUBLIC BOOK TO FOLLOW THE RETURN TERM.**

There was no signature under it. There was no auction stamp. The line *entered in the public book to follow the return term* was the sentence he had written at four in the morning on a table with the stove going and had put in his inside pocket, and it had never been on this wall, and it had never been in the book, and there was no possible way for anybody in Alder Reach to have known it except one person.

"Well," Adrian said.

"Adrian."

"Well," he said again, and then he stopped, and read the four lines a third time, and the yard came up behind him in the grey of four in the morning with the lamps still on, because in this city the lamps over a public lot had never once been out when anybody needed them, and they all read it over his shoulder.
