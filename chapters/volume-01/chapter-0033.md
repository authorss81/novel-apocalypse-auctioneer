# Chapter 33: One Office

The flag was still on the marked spur, and it had been through eleven days of weather, and Selik Marne went out and looked at it on the morning of the twenty-fourth because the first published inspection day was the twenty-fourth and because a man who is about to ask a district for a permanent office ought to know what the district currently has instead.

The flag is a square of oiled canvas on a length of bed iron, set in the ballast four yards short of the second rail joint, and it marks the spot a walking party does not go past. It is not a warning to travelers. It is a flag on a piece of ground where a surveyor's chain has been.

It had not moved. That was the first thing.

The second thing was that somebody had re-hemmed it.

The bottom edge of that flag had been fraying since the nineteenth — a two-inch strip of canvas gone along the lower corner, where the wind comes up off the water — and it had been re-hemmed since, with new thread, in a different colour, by somebody who was not the Warden's office, and Selik stood in the wet and looked at it for a good deal longer than a man looking at a flag needs to.

He did not know who.

He noted it in his own book with the date and the stitch and the fact that he could not account for it, and then he did the thing he had done for nine years and which nobody in Alder Reach had ever once accused him of, which was to write *I do not know* in his own hand, and to go on doing the job.

---

The certificate was down off the wall of his own span and in a case, and the case was standing open on the floor of the near abutment house with a red line drawn across the certificate in a fitter's pencil, and the paper it was folded in said *SUSPENDED — WATER OFFICE — TWENTY-FIRST*, and above that, in the fitter's hand, the clause:

*A certified housing shall be suspended where its readings cannot be accounted for by the structure.*

"I have read that clause every day for three days," Selik said, to the yard, from the step of the abutment house, at ten in the morning. "I put it in my own regulation in the twelfth year, on the strength of the March evacuation, and I want the yard to hear me say that I have not had a single night of it, and I want the yard to hear me say the next part as well, which is that the clause is not about a bridge.

"It is about a man at a desk. It is about *if you cannot account for it, do not carry anybody on it.* That is the whole of it. There is no argument in that clause. There is no interest in it. And nine years of this office have been built on it, and today it is about a line on a trace with nothing on it."

There were perhaps three hundred people in that yard, and it was the yard of the ward market's public inspection, and the ward market had paid for the yard and for the timber and for two clerks, and had refused to pay for a vote, which was by then the most interesting fact about the ward market.

And Selik said the other thing, in the same four minutes, before anybody had arranged themselves.

"Now. I am going to ask you for something, and I am going to ask you for it before I tell you why, and I want that in whatever book is being kept, because I have watched four men this month do the opposite of that and get it right.

"I am asking for **one office.**

"One office responsible for the crossing at St. Orra. One manifest. One inspection. One set of days, published. One name on it that can be given to a magistrate, and removed from it by a process that is written down before the man takes the post, and not by a man in a room with a door on it at eleven o'clock at night. I have written *one office is responsible for the crossing at St. Orra* into a public order of this district, and I am not going to let anybody in this yard believe that the sentence was easy. It is not a sentence I wanted. It is a sentence I had to write because I had nine years of a bridge with a queue on it and a certificate on the wall and no office.

"The queue is the reason the manifest was cut. That is one thing, and it has nothing to do with the certificate, and the two get said together in every bar in this district.

"The certificate came off my wall on the twenty-first because my own instrument drew a line on a shut and empty bridge at twenty-three minutes to eight and nobody can tell me why. **That is a second thing.**

"And a bus failed three hundred yards from my abutment on the morning of the nineteenth, and a man put a list of nine things that bus did not have on a public wall, and that is a third thing, and it is the reason anybody is reading anything anybody has said about me this month, and it is not the reason the certificate is off the wall.

"**Three things. I have spent nine days refusing to let the first two be the same sentence, and I have spent nine days refusing to let the second and the third be the same sentence either, and I am refusing now, in front of three hundred people, and I will put the refusal in the minutes if Mr. Quill will read it out.**" I am the man who caused the sentence, and the yard is to have that in the right order, because I have got a record of winning arguments and I intend to be careful with it."

Nobody cheered, which he had expected, and which he had wanted.

Adrian Vale was standing at the rail with a piece of paper in his hand that was not the paper, and Mara Quill was on a crate with the wax tablet, and Culler Bray was at the back in a wet coat, and the ward market's clerk appointed by the review table — a man named Ravell who had a cold and had not wanted the job — was standing at the reading board with the days on it.

And Selik Marne put the four paragraphs of his proposal on the rail and let a yard read them.

---

**I. ONE OFFICE.** One office responsible for the crossing at St. Orra, named in the order, holding the manifest, the inspection days, the certificate, and the log, for so long as the office exists.

**II. NO EXPIRY.** The office is not dissolved by a year expiring, by a clerk, or by a letter from a floor. It is dissolved by a hearing, at a date stated in the order, of which forty days' notice is given, and the hearing is in a public yard.

**III. NO REPLACEMENT.** No other office, Warden, ward, water office, clerk's department, or private holder may open, close, or authorise the crossing at St. Orra while the office stands. Any such claim is a nullity, and the holder is answerable to the hearing.

**IV. THE COST.** The cost of the office, the days, the fitter, the notice, the yard and the lamps is on the ward market until the crossing is reopened, and the ward market may name one person to sit in the inspection and that person may be named to nothing else.

And then, in the margin, in a hand that was not Selik's, in chalk, by the man who reads the board, because the order of the ninth makes it his duty to read what is on the board and not what is behind it:

**"REVOCABLE, I ASKED. HE WROTE 'DISSOLVED BY A HEARING'."**

---

Mara Quill had got off the crate before the yard had finished laughing at nothing much, which was the sort of thing she had been doing for eight years and which the yard had stopped finding remarkable, which had been her private opinion of the best result of her career.

"Mr. Marne, in a yard, because you are right that a chamber is where men like me go to lose things — may I say the part you have not said, and say it in the plainest words I have."

"Go on."

"You have written a permanent office." She had the tablet down and her hands flat on it, which Selik had watched her do at a review table in the ninth. "That is what it is. Not a service. An office. And an office that cannot be ended by anybody but a hearing forty days hence, in a yard, on days that you publish, is a permanent office, and it is the largest exception in this district, and you are the man who has spent nine years refusing exceptions."

He did not flinch, and she had wanted him to, and the not-flinching told her she had about four minutes of his attention and no more.

"I know what it is," he said. "Say the rest."

"The rest is that it is right," Mara said. "That is the whole of my objection and I have got very little time before somebody starts defending you against me, so I am going to spend it on the sentence that is true. It is right. There should be one office. There should be one manifest. A road with two masters is not governed, it is contended, and this district has watched that happen with a bus, and it will happen again.

"The difficulty is not that it is wrong. The difficulty is that from inside an office, an office is a service, and from outside an office, an office is a claim, and both of those are true at the same time, and a man who has been inside one for nine years cannot see the second one, and I have watched him find that out in front of three hundred people this morning, and that is the entire content of what I came to say."

"Then say the other half, Registrar, because you always do, and it is the half that has ruined me twice this month."

"Which half is that."

"You have never once in eight years told this district what it was getting for its money until after it had been spent. You held a remedy over a dead child for a day and wrote down that it was not discharged, and I was the registrar who wrote it. You came into this district to stop my closure and could not construct an argument against it. And I want to know how the Warden administrator of St. Orra has got a standing order drafted in a room he is not in, and a certificate that came down, and a flag, and a yard inspection on a stated day, all inside nine days, with three hundred people watching, and I want to know the answer to that question, and I think I know the answer to that question, and I am not going to say it in my own hand in a yard."

Selik looked at him for a moment.

"You have not earned the right to that question yet," he said, "and I am not going to pretend to be offended. I have asked for nine days and I have been the most visible man in this district and I have been given nothing by the room. I have got a public order, a suspended certificate, a published board, and a flag that somebody has re-hemmed, and the person who re-hemmed it is not on my list and I have written *I do not know* about it in my own book. Mr. Vale will notice that the second half of your question has just been answered in front of three hundred people by a man who has been handed a district as a favour."

"I noticed," Adrian said.

"Good," said Selik, and meant it, and did not stop, and carried straight on, which was the mistake and which was also the only way he knew to do it.

---

Then a trader at the front asked whether the office would take the eleven out of the queue, and Selik said it would, and a chandler asked what it would cost him, and Selik gave him the figure, and a man at the back asked what happened if the office closed anyway, and Selik said the hearing, and the man at the back said what happened to the people on the far bank if the hearing said the wrong thing, and Selik said that was a question for the hearing.

And at about eleven o'clock the ward market's traders came in through the gate, eleven of them, and Ivo Stenn at the front with a roll of paper under his arm, and they had not been invited and Stenn had said so at the gate, and they were not there to argue with Selik Marne, and Selik Marne, who had been ready for everything except that, took about four seconds to understand what was happening in his own yard.

Stenn unrolled the roll on the reading board, which the appointed clerk did not try to stop, because the reading of the board is his duty and the writing of something else on it is not his business.

"There is no vote on this," Stenn said. "I would like that said first, because Mr. Quill is here and she will otherwise think a ward market is doing something to a hearing."

"There is no vote," Mara agreed.

"Then here is what eleven trades have to say about a permanent office, and I am going to read it and I am not going to argue with a Warden about bridges, because he is the Warden and I sell copper."

He read it, and it took about nine minutes, and it was not what Adrian Vale expected and it was not what Selik Marne expected, and it had nothing to do with bridges.

"We are not against the office. We are against two words in the second paragraph, and Mr. Quill can tell you that we have had a copy of the proposal since six this morning, because Mr. Culler Bray carried it to the counting room himself at five o'clock and would not go home without somebody's signature on it. **A Warden of eleven years walked a document to a market at five in the morning so that a market could read it before it voted, and that is not a thing any of us is going to be able to say again in a year.**"

He put two fingers on the paper.

"**No expiry.** And the hearing has forty days' notice, and the notice goes where? To the office? Who writes the notice? Mr. Quill, a hearing with forty days' notice that is called by the party that benefits from the hearing is not a hearing. It is a formality with a queue in it. And I am not a man who says that because I dislike Wardens. I am a man who has a *paper levy* I have been collecting for eleven years for a thing that does not exist, and the reason I know what a formality with a queue in it costs is that I have been collecting four coppers a quarter off four thousand households against exactly that, and nobody ever came and told me, and I found it myself in a book in my own back office three weeks after the fourth household asked me what it was for.

"So: we will fund the days. We will fund the fitter and the yard and the notice and the lamps, and it will cost this district forty-one coppers a quarter on the paper levy and it will be felt every quarter it runs, which is what Mr. Marne asked for and he did ask for it, and I want that said because nobody is going to say it for him.

"And we will not accept an authority we cannot remove. Not because you are a Warden. Because there is a draft in a room on the second floor of the water office with a door on it, and it has no expiry in it either, and Mr. Bray is not supposed to be telling us about it, and I have read it and it is signed by a floor and not by a man, and I have a bad feeling about a document signed by a floor, and I am telling a yard about it because I have decided I would rather be wrong in a yard than right in a counting room."

He put the roll up again.

"Give us an office that ends. Not this year. Ever. I am not a man asking for a date and I have watched this district refuse a date for a month and I will not do that again. Give us a clause in paragraph two that says the office is **reviewed**, and reviewed is not the same as closed, and a review is a day somebody has to stand in front of, and Mr. Marne can stand in it every seven years and I will be dead or I will be sixty and I will still come, and if he is still good then it gets renewed in public, and if he is not then a man with a rope in his hand is not on a bridge by himself and does not have a hundred and sixty people on it because of a signature."

Selik Marne stood on the step of his own abutment house for a moment with his hat off.

Then he said: "Mrs. Kraus, the dissent you entered on the ninth."

Oveta Kraus was at the rail. She had come to hear the inspection, and she had heard it, and she had not expected to be called, and she came out anyway, which was the reason she was on the review table in the first place.

"Eleven claims, and I would like to say one of them out loud, because this is the last chance somebody in a yard will ask me about it. My brother-in-law has a woman in the fever ward across there and he goes on the Wednesday list and the Wednesday list has been cancelled twice. I am not here to say the Warden's right or wrong. I am here to say that on the ninth I dissented, and I was the only one, and the office this man is proposing is the answer to the queue, and my dissent is the reason the queue is still eleven deep." She looked at Selik. "So I am not going to pretend to a yard that I oppose the office. I opposed a cost with no end, and an office with no end is worse, and I will move for the renewal clause before I go home, and I will not be thanked for it."

---

The motion was put at a quarter past twelve, in a yard, on the day of the first published inspection, and it was not carried, and it was not defeated either, which was a distinction that had not existed in this district's public life before the nineteenth and which took two people explaining it to the crowd.

**TABLED**, said Selik Marne, reading his own minute, in the yard, in the rain, in a voice that carried and did not need to.

**Not adopted. Not refused. Tabled at the request of the proposer, and laid on the table with the reading of it, to be taken up at the first published day after the certificate is replaced or after forty days, whichever is the earlier.**

"And I am tabling it because I have been beaten," he said, "and I want the yard to know the shape of it, because a man who says *I have been beaten* and then explains nothing is a man who has been beaten.

"I have not been beaten on the instrument. I have not been beaten on the manifest. I have not been beaten by Mr. Vale, who has said nine words this morning and all nine of them were about my flag. I have been beaten in a counting room by a man who sells copper, and what he said to me was that a party who benefits from a hearing should not be the party who calls it, and that is correct, and it is the first time in nine years that anybody has stood in a room and told me that my own safety rule was the thing that made me unsafe, and it is correct, and it goes in the book in that order.

"So the office is not adopted and the closure stays. The inspection goes on its stated days at the ward market's expense. The board is re-founded. The flag stays on the second rail joint. And the span is shut to all carriage, including a supervised cargo exception, including a box, including a cylinder, until a replacement housing is certified by two people who are not me.

"There is one more thing and then the yard can go, because three hundred people standing in the rain have come here for something."

He put his hat back on.

"The district has just refused the safest answer available to it. I want that said in a yard, in those words, and I want whoever writes the minutes to write it in those words and not in mine. There are three men in this city who could open a road across St. Orra this winter. One of them is a man on the terraces who will sell you the first page and never mention what is inside it. One of them is a man in a room with a door on it, whose document I have not read and whose name nobody will say. And the third is me, and my crossing has been shut since the ninth and my instrument since the twenty-first, over a line on a trace, and I am right to be.

"Two of those three are dangerous and one of them is a good administrator, and this yard has decided today that it would rather have none of them, and the yard made that decision for a reason it gave me, which is that it does not want a man it cannot remove, and that is a reason I would have given if I were you, and it is in the record that the Warden administrator of St. Orra does not think it is a stupid reason."

Then he took a piece of paper out of his coat, which nobody had seen, and put it on the reading board and read it out himself, because he wanted it in his own voice and because a man who has just lost an argument in a yard has to be the one who says the next thing.

"**I ask, on my own motion, with no other person's name on it, for a public review of the order of the ninth, the one from last month. Of the order, not of the certificate. I ask for it on a day, and I am asking for the day now and the yard can hear it, and Mrs. Quill is to enter it, and the ward market is to be told, and it is to be published on the same board as the days.**

"**I have been the Warden administrator of St. Orra for nine years and I have never once asked anybody to look at my own order. I am asking now. The day is to be the day the ward market has voted on the paper levy, because that is the day this district will be looking at its own money, and a review of a closure ought to sit in the same room as a rate.**"

Nobody in that yard had asked him for that. Adrian Vale understood, about nine seconds after it was said, that it was the most expensive sentence Selik Marne had ever said in public, and that it was going to cost the man his standing in his own office, and that Selik had offered it before anybody could use it, which was the only reason it was worth anything at all.

Mara Quill wrote it down exactly as it was said, in the yard, in the rain, and read it back, and Selik Marne corrected one word in it and let all the rest stand.

Then the yard emptied, and the appointed clerk read the days on the board one more time because it was his duty, and the appointed clerk had a cold, and the inspection was four hours away and had not begun.
