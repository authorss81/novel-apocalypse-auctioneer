# Chapter 14: The People Who Refuse Names

Someone had copied the public board onto the kitchen wall in charcoal, and done it badly, and that turned out to be the most useful thing that had happened in Alder Reach in a week.

The seven headings were there in a heavy hand: **THING USED**, **WHAT IT DOES**, **WHO CARRIES IT**, **WHO IS AFFECTED**, **WHAT IS PAID**, **HOW LONG**, **IF IT FAILS**. The last one had been lettered twice and was still crooked. Whoever had done it had used a burnt stick off the fire and had got soot on the plaster, and beneath the headings they had written, in a small careful addition of their own, two lines that were not in Mara Quill's board at all.

**WHO CAN SAY NO.**
**WHO PAYS IF IT FAILS.**

Lina Vale looked at those two lines for a while, standing in the doorway with the rain coming off her coat, and then she went in and started the morning.

---

The news came in with the bread.

Nobody in Alder Reach was carrying a Fare claim that morning. Eleven sheets had gone out in the night, printed in a bad type that everybody could read, and by the time the lamp-trimmers opened at six the sentence about seven days of road repair was in three barrow yards, two rope shops, a tea table on the upper terraces, and the pocket of a man called Sabin Tull, who had walked down to Lot 17 at half past six with a folded claim inside his coat and asked, in front of the yard, to have it read out loud so that it could be entered in the book as refused *before* he had signed it.

"He is at the yard now," the baker's girl said, wiping her hands. "The registrar has him."

"Good."

"Good?" The girl was fifteen and impatient. "He had already signed it."

"He had signed it at a tea table with a fold in the paper," Lina said. "He has now said so in front of four people, and he has asked to have that said in a book. That is not the same as being a liar and it is worth a great deal more than being a liar, and in about a week you will understand why."

She said it to make the girl feel that something had been done, and because it was true, and because she was tired before eight in the morning and did not have the patience to be admired quietly.

---

The exchange filled the way it always filled: not all at once but in a sequence, and the sequence was a map of who had heard what.

The first three were mothers with the school shelter's children, who had come to ask whether it was safe now, meaning safe from records. The next two were men off the tram line who wanted to know whether the eleven sheets were true, meaning whether the man who printed them was a Warden's man. Then Bett Ruhl came down and stood behind the counter and said nothing, which was how Bett Ruhl took a chair.

Alma Dren arrived in the middle of it, in the grey canvas coat, with the child Teo a step behind her and one hand flat on the back of his neck, and the room made space for her the way water makes space for a hull.

"There are four in the shelter tonight who were not in it last week," Alma said. "Eight of the nine named children are on the far bank. One of the nine is here." She moved her hand off the boy's neck and let him stand on his own, which he did, with his chin up and his hands behind his back, in the manner of a person who has decided to be looked at.

"Petra and Bel came in last night," she went on. "Petra Sill, a woman with a bad cough. Bel, five, fever, and the draught the matron on the far bank has has not crossed and will not cross for four days because your bridge has halved its slots and put a line on a pier that was chalk on a pier." She said it without heat, because she had been up most of the night with a hot child. "So the household is in two places. Eight of the children are on the wrong bank of a river with two escorts and four of the clinic crew, and the rest of us are in a gym on this bank with a woman whose name is not on anything, and a boy who refused to get on a bus and is now the only person in this room who has watched a crossing happen and kept his reason."

Teo said, "I have not kept my reason. I never had a reason. I was frightened."

"You were frightened," Alma agreed. "That is a reason. Say it that way if you want it recorded."

---

Lina let it run for a while without steering, because a kitchen exchange is not a meeting and the moment it starts being run it becomes a room where the clerk is in charge.

What came out of it, in the end, was not one opinion. It was two, and they did not merge, and everybody in the room understood by ten o'clock that the argument they were having was older than all of them.

Bett Ruhl spoke first, from behind the counter, to nobody.

"Write nothing. That is the whole of my position and I have had it for twenty years. A name is a handle and a handle is what you pull on. You pull it and the household comes out of the room and into somebody's file, and after that every clerk with a lamp knows where we sleep, and there is a great deal that can be done to a household once it has an address in a place like this." She set down the cup she had been holding. "I will take the road or I will not. I do not care which. But I am not putting a mark on a page so that a man with a good hand can find us."

Wenna Keel stood up from the bench by the fire, and she had not been expected to.

She was thirty-four and thin, with a laundress's red hands and a boy's jacket folded over her arm that she had not put down since she came in. Tam's jacket. Tam was nine years old and was on the far bank, and had been on the far bank for four days, and his mother had not been able to say his name in a public room until she said it now.

"Then write mine," she said.

The room went quiet in a way it had not been quiet all morning.

"Write it," Wenna Keel said. "Not all of them. Mine. I am not a household, I am a person with a boy on the other bank, and I have been sitting here for two days listening to a good argument about handles, and the good argument has not once got my son home." She held up the jacket. "He got on that bus because I told him to. He was frightened and I told him to get on, and he did, because he is nine and I am his mother, and that is the most ordinary thing in the world and it is the only reason he is not on this bank being sensible. And now there is a rule that says I can have the road without writing down that I am the person who sent him, and I would like to say to whoever comes looking for him that somebody did write it down, and her name was Wenna Keel, and she sent him, and she would like to be found."

Somebody at the back said, "That is exactly what they wait for."

"Then it is exactly what they wait for," Wenna Keel said, "and I am not going to spend my son's second crossing being clever."

---

Lina had been watching the two of them and had arrived at the thing she had been carrying up the stairs since the false alarm.

"The two of you are arguing about a door," she said, "and there is only one thing in this city that is supposed to be a door, and she has not been in the room yet."

Mara Quill came in at twenty past ten with a wax tablet under her arm and rain on her good coat, and she came in badly, on purpose, from the stair door and not the front, because the front had a municipal plate over it and the stair door went through the cold store and past the third barrel, and everybody in the room understood that she had chosen that door on purpose and that the choosing was the message.

She stopped with rain running off the hem onto a floor that had been mopped an hour ago.

"Nobody has said anything yet," Lina said. "You are early."

"I am forty minutes early. I have been standing under an awning for forty minutes deciding whether to come." Mara moved the tablet from one arm to the other. "Registrar Quill, municipal. I am not here to take a roll. If anybody wants a roll taken, I will leave now and you can say so to my clerk at the hall."

Nobody said so, which took about four seconds and cost her something visible.

"Then here is what I have, and then you can all be as angry with it as you like." She set the tablet on Bett Ruhl's counter, which was a thing no municipal officer had done in that room in living memory. "A rule, from last week, written in a public book at Lot 17. It says that beneficiary households may refuse public names. It says the steward and the count stay accountable. It says a responsible party who must be identified may be named on a sealed page held by the registrar instead of in the book, and that the page is opened only if the remedy is called, and that refusing a name does not remove anybody's duty or the remedy."

"And you have come to tell us it is finished," Bett Ruhl said.

"I have come because the rule is not finished and it is going to break somebody, and I would rather it broke in front of me than in a courtroom in six weeks." Mara put her finger on the line. "Here is the flaw, and I found it myself, and nobody had to catch me at it. A remedy works by pointing at a person. It says: this use was done to this person, here is what it cost, here is who answers. The moment the remedy is called, the sealed page opens, and it opens in this room, in my hand, and a person who is on it becomes a person who can be found. So the rule as written offers a household a road and holds the price of the road until the household needs it most. That is not protection. That is a deferred seizure, and the difference is only in the calendar."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke.

"Which is what she has been saying for twenty years," Bett Ruhl said, and did not point, and did not need to.

"Yes."

"So what have you come to offer, if not a promise with a lid on it?"

"Not a promise." Mara took her hand off the page. "A shape. Five things in the book and two things outside it. In the book: a group, a count, a steward, a destination, and a remedy. Nothing else about the household. Outside the book, on paper, held by people you choose and not by me: a note of who has chosen to take part, so that a group is not a number that can be padded or swapped, and so that a person who says no can be shown to have said no. A person who refuses a name still gets water, still gets a cot, still gets the draught, and still has the same right to stop a closing as anybody in the yard. And if the remedy is called and the page cannot be opened because the registrar is dead, or bought, or has lost it, then the remedy fails *loudly* and in public, and the person it failed is written into the book as a person the remedy failed, by name or not. The cost lands on me, on the room, and on the board, and not on the household."

"That is a person trusting herself," Bett Ruhl said. "I have watched people trust themselves before."

"It is a person trusting herself *in front of witnesses*," Mara said. "Which is the only kind that has ever worked, and I would rather say that out loud than let anybody think I have found a way to be reliable."

---

Lina spoke for the first time since the door.

"You have both been talking about whether a name is a handle," she said. "I have been talking about Adrian."

The room gave her the attention, which she had not wanted and could not refuse.

"Since I was fifteen my brother has kept a book, and every few years one of the people in this room has disappeared off a municipal roll and gone quiet, and every few years Adrian has come back from a municipal building with a piece of paper about it that he reads out in a flat voice and then never mentions again. That is who he is to me. He is the man who eventually finds out." She looked at her own hands. "I did not come down to the hall today because a registrar asked. I came because Mara Quill sent a courier up to a kitchen with a rule in it that is *about* people like us, and I have been sitting here since seven deciding whether to be glad about it, and I could not get to glad, because glad means the machine has noticed us in a kind way, and I have seen what happens when it notices."

Mara did not interrupt. She had the tablet against her side and she was listening the way she listened to everything that could become a record, and Lina understood that, and was not sorry for it.

"He is not coming," Lina said. "He is at the yard, and there is a man there with a folded claim and four people reading it out loud, and I asked one of the bread girls what he was doing and she said he was standing at the rail with his hands in his pockets waiting to be useful, and I said *good*." She looked up. "I said good, and then I sat on the stairs and was ashamed, because I have spent nine years expecting to be filed and this morning I wanted him in this room. I wanted the one man in the city who reads things to be in the room where the rule gets decided. Which is a filthy thing to want, and you know it is filthy, and I am saying it out loud in front of eleven people so that it stops being the kind of thing I only think."

Alma Dren made a small sound that was almost a laugh. "Say it again, and add that you would rather he were not needed."

"I would rather he were not needed," Lina said, "and I would rather he were in the room, and both of those are true, and the second one is the reason the first one might happen."

Mara put her tablet down on the counter.

"Then let me say what I can offer instead of him, and let it be worse." She turned the tablet so Lina could see it. "I cannot read a lot the way your brother reads one. I can hold a record that a man like him has written, and I can tell you what is in it and what is not, and I can be wrong in public. When a crossing is proposed, I will be the one who reads the terms out loud to the yard, and I will be the one who says *this part is not written*, and if the answer is that the part is not written, then the road does not open, and I will be the person who said it and not the person who is difficult. That is not power. It is a job with a bad reputation and no authority whatever, and I am offering it to this room because the room is the part that keeps it honest."

"And if the man who reads things writes a term you cannot see is there?" Bett Ruhl asked.

"Then you will say so, and the crossing waits until it is written down or thrown out," Mara said. "That is the entire offer. I have no power to make anybody obey it. I have a red pencil and a book and a habit of writing down what happened, and I am telling you that I will use the habit on you and not only on your enemies, which is more than the last four offices in this city offered you."

---

The rest of the morning was work, and the work was the part Lina would remember.

Wenna Keel gave her name for her son, and only for her son, and the clerk who came up from the hall wrote it in a way that made it clear the name belonged to one person and not to a household: **WENNA KEEL, named by her own choice, mother of one of the eight, answerable for her own crossing only.**

Bett Ruhl's household gave nothing, and got a group, a count of two, a destination, and a remedy, and the paper note of participation went under the third barrel in a hand that was not Lina's and would never be in her keeping.

The shelter group was written as a group, with its count, its steward, its destination, and the two escort stewards referred to by the mark on Mara's sealed sheet and the school copy, which is the second copy, and which two people now held.

Teo asked, twice, whether anybody was going to put his refusal in the book as a refusal, and was told yes, and asked for the third time whether refusing twice was allowed, and was told that refusing twice was allowed and that nobody was going to argue with him about the third time either.

And Petra Sill came in at noon, because Alma Dren had sent for her, and did not want to, and came. Bel came on her hip, asleep, with a fever that had come down half a degree in the night and no draught in her at all.

Mara asked whether the book should carry the two extra mouths in the shelter group.

"Carry the mouths," Alma said. "The names, no."

"Carried," Mara said, and wrote **two persons newly resident at the shelter, unnamed, one child under treatment, counted and not named**, and read it aloud, and Bet Ruhl looked at the ceiling for a while and then said it would do, which from her was a decoration.

---

Rell Bode came down the river stairs at half past two with a washboard under her arm and a complaint she had been carrying since the previous night.

She was forty and had two children in a room off the terraces and a household that had been struck off the rolls after a trouble call in year twelve, and she had heard about the shelter's rule from a woman on the stairs and had come down, against her own judgement, to see whether it was true.

"You have a book," she said. "With a line in it about not writing names."

"Yes."

"Then I have a question and it is not for you." She turned around and looked at the stair door, and there was a man on the landing she had not seen, and she did not go toward him and did not leave. "I will take the road. I am not writing. I have a boy with a bad chest and the far bank has a clinic and this bank has a shed. That is the whole of my decision and I have made it and I do not need anybody to talk me into it."

"Then you have the road," Mara said, "subject to terms that do not exist yet, and I will not pretend otherwise."

"I know. That is what I came to say the other thing about." Rell Bode set the washboard down against the counter, which was the only piece of furniture in the room she trusted. "Your book says that if the remedy is called, the registrar opens a page, and on the page is somebody who must be found, and then it says the right to refuse does not remove the bearer's duty or the remedy. I have read it. It was copied onto this wall in charcoal and I can read charcoal."

Mara said nothing at all.

"So tell me what happens the day a child of mine comes off that bus and cannot say why he went." Rell Bode's voice did not rise; she was not a person who had ever needed it to. "Because it is going to happen. You told us so yourself, you wrote it down, every traveler loses the reason. And on the day it happens, somebody is going to come to you and say *open the page*, and you will open it, because you are decent, and you will find me, because that is what is on it, and I will be a person in a book with a loss attached to me and a name I did not give anybody, in a city of two hundred thousand people, and every clerk in it will now know a thing about me that only I was supposed to choose."

"That is what would happen," Mara said.

"And that is why I am refusing the road today, while refusing it is still free." Rell Bode picked the washboard up again. "Not the road. The *after*. Because the after is the only part of this that is going to last, and I have watched one arrangement in my life make the right thing happen once and then use the same arrangement for the rest of my life, and I am not going to be a thing that happened once."

Mara looked at her for a long moment, and then she took out the wax tablet and wrote on it in front of the room, in a hand that did not shake, and turned it around.

"Challenge received, this date, from a household that refuses to be named and would accept the route. The registrar's own rule is unenforceable against a person the record cannot point at, because the remedy is reached by naming the person, and the naming is the harm. The challenge is upheld in part: it is not true that the page is protection, because the page is a list with a seal on it. The remedy must be named and workable *before* a crossing opens, and it must work for a person who is not found."

"The board will refuse to open a crossing," Bett Ruhl said.

"The board will refuse to record a closing," Mara said. "Those are different sentences and the second one is a sentence I am allowed to say, and I am going to be extremely tiresome about the difference for as long as it takes."

She wrote at the bottom, under Rell Bode's challenge: **Next proposal must state a remedy that does not require finding the affected person. Nothing opens until it does.**

Then she tore the page off, folded it, and gave it to the courier on the landing, who had been listening to all of it and who took it and went down at a run toward Lot 17.

---

The board was still sitting when it arrived.

That was the part Lina had not expected. The sheet went into the public lot book, and Nessa Pell read it out because reading out loud was what the board did, and the four seats that had come in through the wet that morning — Mara in the registrar's chair, Nessa behind the counter, Coll Ferrin on his crate, Marda Osk on hers with the tea tray she had brought again on purpose — took about nine minutes with it.

They could not grant a use. They could refuse to record a closing, publish a result, call a review, and set the public custody term. What they did with Rell Bode's challenge was the second of those things and no more, which was exactly the kind of thing the room found maddening and exactly the kind of thing that was the only reason the room could be trusted at all.

"The challenge is valid and it is upheld in part," Nessa Pell said. "It does not name a person, and the board does not require one to answer a challenge. The registrar's proposed remedy is adopted as a condition of the next proposal. The board will not record a closing for any use whose remedy depends on identifying a person who has refused to be named."

Ferrin put his hand up. "That means no crossing at all until somebody writes a remedy that works on a person nobody can find."

"That is what it means."

"Then the clinic does not cross."

"Yes," Mara said.

---

The exchange emptied by four. Rell Bode went up the stairs without saying goodbye, which was correct, and Bett Ruhl began wiping the counter in the way she wiped a counter when she had been thinking about something and would not say what.

Lina stood under the charcoal board and looked at the two lines somebody had added that were not in Mara's version.

**WHO CAN SAY NO.**
**WHO PAYS IF IT FAILS.**

Then she picked up a burnt stick off the fire, and under the second line, in heavy letters, she wrote a third.

**WHO ANSWERS WHEN NOBODY CAN BE FOUND.**

She looked at it a moment, and she was surprised to find that her hand was steady, and she understood that this had to do with the workshop on the far bank being empty and with a great many other things, and that she was not going to think about any of them until she had brought the bread up.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Four streets away a lamp burned over a public board, and a man with a folded claim in his coat was standing at a rail with his hands in his pockets, being useful, waiting for the board to want something, and the board had just refused to want anything at all until somebody could answer a question that none of them had known how to write down.
