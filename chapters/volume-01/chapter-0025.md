# Chapter 25: A Second Emergency Exception

Adrian did not sleep on the twenty-second and he did not sleep on the twenty-third either, and by the middle of the second day he had stopped noticing that as a fact and started noticing it as a kind of clarity, which was the worst thing about it. He had learned some years ago, in a bad winter, that a man who has been awake for thirty hours stops arguing with himself and starts composing, and that the compositions are better than the arguments, and that this is the reason he had spent eleven days being so careful about what he said in an empty room.

So on the morning of the twenty-third he got up at five, made tea he did not drink, and wrote a return term, and it was a good one.

**RETURN.** The return is a second crossing on the same use and carries the same toll on every person who returns, being the memory of the reason for the journey, which is not a smaller thing the second time and is not the same thing and is not stated by this charter to be either. The driver is authorised to hold the line, take the outer shoulder, and come down by any route he can see; if the far side is not a road, he stops the vehicle, he gets out, and he walks back toward the wall, and nobody follows him onto it. The return ends at the second rail joint east of the bell post, under the lamp, where the ballast drops and the plate is stamped, in the bus of Tarin Kest, coming down from the far bank, and if the marking is not where the pair says it is, the joint is the return and the arm is not, and the driver stops at the joint. The return passengers are the people who got on the far side, and any of them may come back, and each of them is asked separately before the outward case opens, in a yard, in the words in the preceding sentence, and may refuse without a reason.

He read it four times. It was the best sentence he had written since the token came out of the inspection block, and it was nine lines long, and it did the work, and he knew exactly what it was going to cost, and it was going to cost the road.

That was the whole of his thinking and he was not proud of how fast it came.

---

The yard filled from seven, and it filled differently from every other morning of this week, because there was a new thing in it, which was that the public notice board had been altered in the night and everybody had heard about it before they came through the gate.

Ivo Stenn was there with sixty people, which was the ward market, which was how you knew a thing was serious, and he had come with a ledger and a coat and a face Adrian had not seen on him before and did not want to look at.

"We want a published service," Stenn said, before Adrian got to the rail. "That is what I have been refused twice and I have come to ask for it a third time and I am going to ask for it in front of sixty people so that you cannot refuse it quietly."

"Stenn—"

"Six hundred and twenty-nine coppers." Stenn did not raise his voice and the yard still heard him, because sixty people go quiet together. "That is what your workshop is worth, and I have the page, and I have read it, and I am not here to sneer at it, because it is the first honest figure anybody has put in that book in three weeks. And here is what it means to me. It means that when the nineteen people on the far bank ask me on Monday why there is no road, I have got a sentence, and the sentence is *there was a road and it cost six hundred and twenty-nine coppers and a child, and the man who ran it has published the whole of it, and here is what the whole of it was.*"

He put the ledger down on the crate.

"And I want a published service anyway, and I am telling you why I want it after reading that page, which is that a market which has been told there is no return condition for anything is a market where nobody can plan a season. I do not need your road to be safe, Mr. Vale. I need your road to have a *shape*. A market can live with a bad answer. It cannot live with no field in the sheet for the answer to go in."

---

Adrian stood on the rail at ten past eight and put the seven items out, because that was the schedule and the schedule was the only part of him that still worked, and he was going to use it to do this properly, and the fact that he was going to use it to do this properly was itself the shape of the morning.

"One: a route request with the use, the destination and the count," he said. "Use: one route across St. Orra, twelve minutes, out and back on one activation. Destination: the far receiving ramp on the far bank, and the far school house, and the far end of the signal cut. Count: nineteen, which is four of the medicine crew, nine signal hands, four children of the second shelter group, and two of the three escort stewards, and I have got the names of all nineteen in a book in this building and anybody may read them.

"Two: the toll, in the words the travelers will hear. Nessa."

Nessa Pell read it, twice, to a yard of six hundred inside and a hundred at the gate, and Adrian let her, and did not interrupt, and had not interrupted for three weeks.

"Every traveler loses the memory of why they are making the journey," Nessa said. "That is the toll. On the way out. And a person who comes back pays it again, and it is not a smaller thing the second time, and this charter does not say that it is the same thing, because nobody knows, and I am not going to guess at it in front of six hundred people. Mr. Vale has written a sentence about the second payment and I am going to read you that sentence in a moment, and it is the only sentence any of us have got, and it is a sentence a man wrote at five o'clock this morning in a kitchen, and I want that said before I read it."

"Three: a named bearer and a named remembering passenger and what that passenger is being asked to carry."

"I will drive," said Tarin Kest.

He was standing at the back with his cap in his hand and he had been in the yard since seven, and he had come in with a coat over his shoulder and a colour in his face that Adrian had seen before and had last seen on a man pulled out of a service wall in the flood.

"You have been outside for three days," Adrian said.

"I have been outside for three days because there is nothing else to do and because your lot is the only place in Alder Reach that anybody writes anything down." Tarin came forward. "And because I have been thinking about a sentence in a bus. And I will drive it, and I want to say one thing first so that the yard has it before the sheet. If that arm has been turned once, it can be turned again, and if it is turned again while the bus is running then the return is the joint and not the arm, and that has to be on the sheet in those words, and not as a promise, as an instruction to a man who cannot look up. Write that."

"That is in the return," Adrian said.

"Then read the return," Tarin said. "Which is what the sheet says you are going to do next, and I would like to do it here in the yard, and I would like you to read it out, and I would like the six hundred people to hear the same four lines at the same time as me."

---

Adrian read it out.

**RETURN. Term held by the reader and entered before the token leaves the case.**

He read the line and then he did not say anything for some seconds, and there was no way for the six hundred people in that yard to know that the line was four words long and that nine lines had been sitting in a tin on the crate beside his hand, and that he had decided at ten past six that morning, in a kitchen, with the tea he had not drunk, that nine lines in a tin was a thing that would empty the yard before nine o'clock.

"It is entered before the token leaves the case," he said. "That is the same commitment I made on the nineteenth and I am making it again, in this yard, with the board present, and any person who does not understand it says so now."

The yard was not silent. The yard was worse than silent, because it was *attentive*, and Adrian had spent eleven days learning the difference, and he knew that four hundred people had just heard a sentence they had heard before, in this yard, on the nineteenth, and had begun, all at once and in their own time, to go and find it.

Nessa Pell had the book open. She did not have to look. She turned to the page and read the line that was in her own hand, above his signature, four days old, in ink, and she read it in a voice that did not do one single thing to it.

"*The return is unwritten.*"

Nobody in the yard said a word.

"Registrar's entry," Mara Quill said. She had been at the crate for the whole of it and had not moved. "The board's page from the nineteenth stands, unstruck and unaltered, and it says that the return is unwritten. The sheet put in front of six hundred people this morning has a return field which says the return term is held by the reader and will be entered before the token leaves the case. Both statements are on the public record of this lot at the same time, in the same yard, four days apart, and they cannot both be true, and the registrar is obliged to say which of them she believes."

"Which one do you believe?" somebody said.

"That is not my question to be asked," Mara said, "and I will explain why in four days' time, and today the yard has a person in it who is better at reading than I am, and I would ask him to read it, and I would like the book to record that I asked."

---

Sabin Tull had the page in his hand before Mara had finished the sentence, and he had it in his hand because he had been standing at the crate with it open, which was not what a sitting board member is supposed to do and which he did not appear to have noticed.

"I will read it," Sabin Tull said, "because I am the man who has refused four claims in nine days and refused them by reading them, and this is the first one I have ever wanted to sign."

He read page eleven, the undertaking, in Adrian's hand.

"*Before any next use, I will state the full known toll, the return toll, the unresolved terms, and the remedy. I will not use a private promise to make a public charter appear complete.*"

He read the board's page from the nineteenth.

"*The return is unwritten.*"

He read the sheet from this morning.

"*Term held by the reader and entered before the token leaves the case.*"

And then he stood in the middle of a wet yard at a quarter past eight in the morning and said the thing that ended it, and he said it in the flat voice of a man who has carried goods for six years and is not impressed by anybody.

"That is the same act," he said, "in the same yard, in the same words, four days apart, and you did not get it wrong, and I want to say that out loud for the record because I can see three hundred people in here who have just decided he got it wrong. He did not get it wrong. He did it again. He has the words for it now. He has had them since Tuesday."

"Seconded," said Marda Osk, from her crate, "by a person who read the toll out loud to the gate this morning, and I would like it entered that I have been reading the toll out loud for three weeks and I read it to four hundred people this morning and the sheet they were reading from had four words in the return field, and nobody in the four hundred could tell."

"Nessa," Adrian said.

"The board does not grant a use," Nessa Pell said.

"Write the return."

"The board does not grant a use," Nessa Pell said, "and the board cannot accept a term out of a man's coat, and the board cannot record a closing whose fourth item is a promise, and the board has already refused to record a closing on the ground that no proposal was in the book, and I will not write a term into a book tonight that is not going to be read to the people who pay it until after the case opens, and if I do that then this book is not a book, it is a hand, and a hand can be interrupted."

---

And that was when Mara Quill stopped it, in front of the whole market, and she did it in the flattest voice she had.

"Registrar, and I am going to do the one thing I have the standing to do, which is to refuse, and then I am going to do the thing I do not have the standing to do, which is to say why, and I am going to say the second one in front of six hundred people, and the six hundred are not going to be able to un-hear it, and that is a cost I am choosing."

She put her hand flat on the crate.

"The public road is suspended. It was suspended by me on the twentieth and I said at the time that I was not suspending it because the road is dangerous, I was suspending it because the document that let twenty-eight people onto a bus had no field in it for what the trip back would take out of them, and I said it would not run again until there was one. There is not one. Mr. Vale has told this yard, twice today, that he has one, and has not entered it, and is asking this market to believe that it will arrive before the case opens, which is what he asked this market to believe on the nineteenth and which is the sentence that is on page eleven of that book in his own handwriting as a promise not to do.

"So: no token opens at Lot 17. And I want the book to hold the reason correctly, because I am the registrar and the reason is mine as much as anybody's. **I did not suspend the road. I cannot lift the suspension either. The suspension is the board's.** The board refused to record a closing on the twentieth because there was no proposal in the book, and the board will refuse again for the same reason this morning, and the board is four seats, and I am one of them, and I am not a majority, and if I stood here alone and announced a suspension on my own authority I would be doing the thing I have spent eight years preventing, which is a registrar with a thumb on a scale."

Nessa Pell said, "The board is asked whether there is a proposal in front of it, and there is not one, because the return field on the sheet says the term is held by the reader and not entered, and a term held by the reader is not a term."

"Not a proposal," said Sabin Tull. "Not a proposal," said Marda Osk.

"Not a proposal," said the sitting steward, "and Mr. Vale is not offended, because Mr. Vale offered to have it entered and I have declined to enter it, and the whole of this morning has been him telling me in front of six hundred people that he is holding a term in a tin and asking me to trust him, and I will say this as a person who has signed his hand to his counts for three weeks: no. Not this one. Not on a week like this one."

Mara Quill said, "Recorded. And the four seats have said no, and the registrar abstains, and the road is shut."

---

And then the yard began to shout, and Adrian did the thing that is not a thing anybody can take from a man, which was to stand still.

He had known. That was the part he could not get past for the rest of the day and could not explain to anybody afterwards. He had known at ten past six that morning, in a kitchen, with a tin on the crate. He had known while he was writing the four words. He had known when he read the line out loud and let the yard hear it, and he had watched the yard go and find the other page, and he had not been surprised, and his hands had been steady, and there was no confusion anywhere in him, and that was what he had been afraid of for three weeks and had not been able to name.

He had a good argument. He had had it since five o'clock in the morning and it was a genuinely good argument and it was true in every part.

He said it.

"Nineteen people. On the far bank, in a school house and a signal hut and a receiving ramp, with the nights starting, and the far bank has begun to count its own rations against the number of mouths on it, and the clinic there has a third of its cots empty because the medicine is on this side. If the road is lost, they are there until the river freezes, and it froze once in the year I was twenty-two, and I have seen what that does to a district that has been counted wrong." He had his hands at his sides and he was not appealing to anybody, which was new. "A return term in the book, read out at a yard, in front of the people who are going to pay it, is exactly the mechanism that emptied this yard on the fourteenth when the three-day service was refused. Stenn can tell you what happened. Sixty people asked for it and Stenn had to tell them no. I am telling you the same thing with a different reason, and the reason is that the crowd will read a toll as a refusal and a refusal as a closing, and by noon there will be nobody left in this yard who wants a road, and that is how a road dies — not in a Fault, in a morning, in a queue of people who have read their own price and gone home to be angry about it."

The yard listened to the whole of it. He was not interrupted once. It was a good argument, delivered well, by a man who was the best reader in the city, and it was true, and the six hundred people believed every word of it.

And then Stenn got up off a crate at the back and said, "No," in a voice Adrian had known for two years, and Adrian turned round.

"Say that again."

"No," Stenn said. "I have read your page and I have read the six hundred and twenty-nine and I have read the child, and I am telling you that I do not care what this yard reads into a toll, because I have been the man who has to take a *no* back to sixty people for three weeks, and I have got rather good at it, and I know exactly what it looks like when somebody in a warm room tells me a crowd will be frightened of a price. They will. That is true. And then we do it anyway and we argue afterwards, and I would rather argue afterwards with nine people than lose nineteen on a bank I cannot get to."

"Nineteen would be four medicine hands, nine signal hands and four children in a school house," said a signal hand with a signal case under his arm and a copied page in his coat, from the middle of the yard, and Adrian turned round again, and the man was the tenth of that crew and the only one of them on this bank and had said so every time anybody had asked him. "I did not go. I have been standing on this side with this page since six o'clock on Tuesday morning and I have written down every number that was called across that opening and I have read them back to four sets of hands on the far side, and I have not got one of the nine back, and none of the four, and none of the two. That is five hundred and forty coppers of signal cable sitting on the far bank with my crew, and I would like the other nine and the four children and the two stewards, and I will tell you the thing nobody wants: I do not want your road. I have read the page you published. You have listed the nine things that charter did not contain and one of them was *a witness who had heard the whole of it*, and I have been a witness to four charters of this lot and I did not hear the whole of any of them, and I am the tenth of nine, and I am the only one of us still standing on this bank, and I am going to say this once in a yard and then I am going to hold my page: give me a road in a year, with the whole of it written down before the door opens, and I will put nine names in it and I will not complain about one of them."

---

It ended at eleven, and the yard went, and it went without a resolution, and the resolution was the absence of one.

Adrian put his palm down on the crate one more time, and nothing came up through it, and the echo did not come, because he had not read anything, because he had not touched the brass, because for the first time in eleven days he had spent a whole morning in a public argument about a document and had not once put a hand on the thing it was about.

"Registrar," he said. "I am going to say one more thing in this yard and I am going to say it to you and not to the six hundred, and if you object to it being said at all, say so now and I will not say it."

Mara Quill looked at him for a long moment.

"Say it to the yard," she said. "If it is worth saying to me it is worth saying to them, and if it is not, then it is a thing for a corridor, and you have had a corridor this week and it has not worked."

So he said it to the yard, and it took about ninety seconds, and the yard listened to all of it and did not accept one word of it afterwards.

"Mr. Quill. I am going to say a name and I am not going to say anything about whether the man is alive."

The yard went quiet in a different way.

"Iven Tallow," Adrian said. "He taught me to read a lot. He gave me the blank lot card I have never been able to fill in and he vanished in the first night of the Fracture and I have believed for seventeen years that he is dead, and I still believe it, and this morning I would like the book to record that I am not saying one word about whether he is alive. Here is the only thing I have got and it is worth very little. He did not teach me the method. He taught me what a thing costs. The method is the part I invented at four in the morning with a stove going and a page in front of me, and the man who invented it is standing in this yard, and you all watched him do it on Tuesday, and none of you needed to be told twice, and that is the strongest argument I have got and I would like somebody to use it against me more efficiently than I am managing."

"And that is your answer," Mara Quill said.

"Yes."

"Registrar's entry." She had the book open and she wrote as she spoke, which she did not usually do, and Adrian understood that she had decided in advance to write it in front of him. "Mr. Vale's answer is that the method was his own, that the name he has introduced has no bearing on the breach of the nineteenth, and that the yard is the body that has to find him guilty. It is recorded. It is not accepted by the registrar and it is not a matter for the registrar, and I am going to say the rest of it, and I have thought about how to say it for two days and there is only one way to say it that is honest, so I am going to say it badly and I am going to say it in the yard so that it is not a thing anybody can take back into a room."

She put her pencil down.

"Mr. Vale, I have a name in a book from before the Fracture, and I have never met the man, and I do not know whether he is alive, and neither do you, and it is not the point. The point is that a municipal records office on the far side of this river had a man in it with emergency authority and a seal and a gift for reading damaged paper, and he was very good at it, and this is a city that has spent seventeen years blaming its failures on a flood. And there was a way of working that he had, and I have read enough of his handwriting in other people's complaints to know what it sounds like, and it sounds like a decision that is the emergency, and a piece of it written down, and the rest of it to follow, because the rest of it is always the part that costs more delay than the delay is worth. That is not a conspiracy and I am not going to stand in a yard and pretend to a thing I cannot prove. That is a *shape*. And the shape is the same. And you have described yours out loud, twice, in your own handwriting, in this yard, and you have done it again this morning while the board was refusing you, which is the part I cannot get past, because the man I am describing would have said something at that exact moment. He would have said: *the shape is mine, the emergency is real, and the people who are not here today will understand the arithmetic in a week.*"

"I did not say that."

"You did not say it *yet*," Mara Quill said, "and that is what frightens me, and I will not soften it in a yard for the sake of your dinner or mine, and I am not finished."

She picked the book up off the crate.

"Mr. Vale, you have written the best return term I have read in eight years and you read four lines of it to six hundred people. I have read nine lines. I am going to want the other five, in the book, in the yard, read to the people who pay it, and I am going to want them tomorrow, in daylight, and I will not stop asking. And when you give them to me I am going to read them out and I am going to let this yard hear what a proper return condition sounds like, and then this yard is going to decide whether it wants a road, and I promise you that it will not be me that decides, and I promise you that it will not be you either." She held the book against her chest. "And tomorrow, at nine, in this yard, I am going to read the charter of the nineteenth out from the top, every line of it, in public, and you are going to stand on that rail while I do it, and it is not a hearing and it is not a review and it does not need a board and I do not care what you say at the end of it. You wanted to be reachable by something other than your own character. I am going to make you reachable by a piece of paper in the middle of the day, in front of everybody, and I am going to do it to the whole of it, and it is going to take three hours."

Adrian stood on the rail and did not answer, and the yard watched him not answer, and it was not the hush of a man who had nothing to say. It was the hush of a man who had said everything he had.

Nessa Pell closed the lot book at ten past eleven and the yard emptied into the rain, and the token stayed in its case on the crate under the two lamps with the wax on it that had been broken four times, and nowhere in Alder Reach was a road.
