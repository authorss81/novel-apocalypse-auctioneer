# Chapter 11: The Next Morning's Price

The floorboards at Lot 17 still smelled of the flood that had come through the roof three winters ago, and every damp morning since then the smell had come back with it: wet wool drying on a coat, lamp oil burning in a badly trimmed wick. Adrian liked it. It told him where he was before he had finished opening his eyes.

This morning it told him that, and his right hand would not do what he asked of it.

He tried three times to make a heading for the week. The first attempt leaned hard to the right, as though the whole page were sliding out from under the nib. The second skipped in the middle of a word and left a pale scar. The third was legible, and he disliked it, because legible was not the same as accurate.

He set the pencil down and read what he had left on the slate.

**ROUTE SCHEDULE — ST. ORRA — WEEK 40**

Underneath it, in a smaller hand he did not remember making, he had added four words.

**The medicine first.**

He drew the line through them with one hard stroke, which took more force than the words had deserved.

A bucket scraped at the far end of the sale floor. Nessa Pell came out from behind the inspection block with the public lot book under one arm and a cloth in her hand, wiping the counterweight scale where somebody had left a wet handprint.

“Steward,” Adrian said.

“Seven days,” Nessa said. “Two left, if nobody spends them on something.”

“What could they spend them on?”

“That is not my job. Keeping count is my job.” She set the book on the crate beside the board and opened it, and did not look at the slate. “You wrote a schedule.”

“I wrote a heading.”

“You wrote **the medicine first**.”

“I crossed it out.”

“I can see that. I am not the one who has to live with it.” Nessa turned a page. “The registrar is here. She is early.”

Mara Quill came in through the side door with rain still on her shoulders and a roll of waterproofed paper under her arm. She had been at the Brass Verdict Hall since before the lamps were lit. She set the roll on the inspection block, took out a sheet, and read Adrian's heading upside down without picking it up.

“Say what that says,” she said.

“You can read.”

“I want to hear whether you will say it.”

Adrian felt the echo sitting in his jaw, the ordinary hangover of a reading three days old. “Route schedule. St. Orra. Week forty. And a line I struck through.”

“Why did you strike it through?”

“Because the medicine does not get to be first by my handwriting.”

Mara unrolled her sheet and pinned one corner under the token case. It was a grid with four columns and a great deal of empty space. “The clinic's night clerk filed a request at four this morning,” she said. “It is a legitimate request. It is in the book before you finish your tea, and it is going to stay in the book whatever you decide this morning.”

“It does not go first because I wrote it.”

“It does not go first because *anyone* wrote it. That is the part you have not accepted yet.” She set the red pencil on the crate. “A request is a claim on a scarce thing. Three of them arrived before the yard filled. All three are honest. Not one of them can be satisfied this week, and possibly not this month, and the token may not survive being used again at all.”

“Somebody has to put them in an order.”

“Somebody has to. It is not going to be you, and it is not going to be me either, and I would like to have that written somewhere before the morning gets worse.”

---

The clinic matron came in at ten past six, which meant she had crossed the river stairs in the dark, and her boots said so. Halva Sen stopped at the sale rail, looked at the eleven people already standing in the wet, and took off her hat.

“Six coppers,” she said to Adrian. “For the shell of a surgical lamp with a hairline crack. You told me the regulator read steady and the feed would weep.”

“It does weep.”

“I know it weeps. I have been holding a lamp with a wet joint for four days because you were honest and the ward is short of everything honest.” She put a folded slip on the crate. “Fourteen cold boxes. The last six are still on the depot rail and these eight came down from the upper yard in the night. Ninety-six hours of coolant, and the coolant is not the problem. The problem is that the ward is across the water and the boxes are on this side of it.”

“Your request is in the book.”

“My request is in the book. That is not the same as my request having a place in the week.” She glanced at the board. “What I need is the route, once, outbound, heavy, with a driver who knows the water. I do not need a schedule. I need a road that exists for twelve minutes on a morning I can name.”

The market cooperative sent Ivo Stenn, who had a seal on his coat and a ledger he did not open.

“The clinic is asking for a favor,” Stenn said. “The ward market is asking for a service. The last two crossings were goods moving, which is what a road is for. A road that only runs when somebody has a sick ward is a favor. The ward market wants three fixed days a week, published, with fuel and lamp rights attached, so the repair crews can be counted on before they are loaded.”

“That is a permanent schedule,” Adrian said.

“That is a service.”

“There is no such right. The use is one route across St. Orra for twelve minutes. Repeated use may strand the route permanently.” He heard himself say it and wished he had said it slower. “I cannot sell you three days a week. I can sell you a crossing on a day you name, if the token opens at all.”

“Then the ward market has nothing to plan on.”

“The ward market has a road that does not exist yet. That is not nothing. It is worse than nothing, because nothing can be budgeted.”

Stenn's mouth moved toward something that would have been a smile if he had allowed it to finish. “You are telling me the difference between a service and a favor costs us a season.”

“I am telling you the difference is the whole argument, and I do not have a way to pay for it.”

Then Alma Dren came in, and the room changed its weight without anyone standing up.

She was in the grey canvas coat of the school shelter, still damp at the shoulders, and she came straight to the crate and put her hand flat on the book. Not on the page. On the closed cover, as though she were steadying something that might otherwise be taken.

“Eight of the nine are on the far bank,” she said. “The two escorts and the four crew who stayed are with them. Fourteen people are living in a receiving ramp on the wrong side of a river, in a building that belongs to a clinic, eating what a clinic eats. One of the nine is standing on this side of the yard because he refused, and he is dry, and I would like the room to sit with that for a moment before anybody says the word *schedule*.”

Nobody said anything for a while. Somewhere under the roof a tram bell that had not been rung in years moved in the wind and gave one flat note.

“I am not asking for the medicine to wait,” Alma said. “I am asking to be told whether you are going to the far bank, and if you are, I am asking to be the second person named, because the first person named is the driver and the driver forgets why he drove.”

Mara came around the crate. “For a new closing you cannot both answer for the shelter group and stand as a witness.”

“I know what I cannot do. I came anyway, so that whoever writes it down has to write that I came.”

She said it without heat, and Adrian understood that she had learned the trick from Mara, and that the trick worked.

---

The three requests lay on the crate in the order they had arrived, which was the order they had been frightened in, which was not an order at all.

Adrian looked at them for a long moment.

“The clinic goes first,” he said, and heard the sentence leave him before he had decided to let it.

The room did not move. That was the trouble. Nobody argued, nobody stormed out, nobody threw a chair. Halva Sen closed her eyes for a moment with the relief of a person who has just been told her sister will be taken tonight. Ivo Stenn wrote something in his ledger. Alma Dren did not move at all.

Adrian felt the shape of what he had done arrive a half-second after the words: he had ranked three strangers' children and fevers and freight by the speed of their arrival, and every person in the room had accepted it, because he had been the one who had read the token and slept badly for it and stood on the rail while they were on the bus.

Mara did not reach for her pencil. That was worse than if she had.

“Take it back,” she said.

“It is the right order.”

“It is the *arrival* order. The clinic arrived first because its night clerk was awake. If the repair crews had sent a man at midnight instead, you would have put the ward market first, and Alma Dren would still be standing there with fourteen people on the wrong bank, and neither of them would have heard a word about it.”

“The medicine has ninety-six hours of coolant.”

“The medicine's countdown is a fact. It is not a ranking. You have just used a countdown to make a decision that eleven people have to live inside of.” Mara's voice did not rise. It never rose; that was what made it so hard to argue with. “Adrian. Who is the board?”

“I do not have a board for this.”

“You have a board for everything else. There is one constituted panel, four seats, and the sitting steward in this room with a book. It cannot grant a use. It cannot wave a toll. It can refuse to record a closing, publish a result, and call a review. What it can do is decide what it is willing to write down, and right now what it is willing to write down is a schedule with a preference at the top of it.”

“You want me to hand this to four people.”

“I want you to stop being the only person in the room who can rank, because the moment you are, every honest person with a sick child loses to whoever reached your crate first.”

Stenn made a small sound. It might have been agreement.

Adrian put both hands flat on the crate. He was aware of his own fingers as objects, which was what the echo did to him after a real reading: everything became slightly detachable, including his hands, including his own wanting.

“I want to do it myself,” he said.

Nessa Pell's pencil stopped.

“I want to be the one who decides which of those three gets the road,” Adrian said. “I have wanted that since the first crossing, and I have not said it out loud because saying it out loud would make it a thing I did instead of a thing I was.”

Nobody spoke.

“I can read this token well enough to tell you what it will do, what it will take, and what standing a driver needs. I cannot read which of you deserves it. I do not know what a fair use is. I know what a fair *price* is, and a fair price is the thing I have been reaching for since I was twenty-one, and it is not the same thing, and every room I have ever been in has suffered because I could not tell the difference until afterward.” He took his hands off the crate. “That wanting is why there is a board. Not because the board is clever. Because I am not, on this, and neither is anyone else in here, and something has to stand between my wanting and the list.”

The room had gone completely quiet. The rain on the roof had stopped without anybody noticing when it stopped.

Mara picked up the red pencil. “Registrar's record,” she said. “At ten past seven, in the yard, in front of the open book, Adrian Vale stated that he wishes to rank the competing requests himself, and that the reason is his own judgment, and that the board exists in order to stand between that wish and the list. He has no vote in the selection and no veto over it. He may read the lot and state what he finds, and that is all he may do.”

“You are writing down that I want it.”

“I am writing down that you admitted it. If I do not, the next person who needs a road will be told that a ranking is a reading, and they will believe it, because it is convenient to believe it and I will not be in the room when it costs them.”

Stenn closed his ledger. “So who decides?”

Nessa Pell had already stood up. “The board sits at four. Registrar, sitting steward, and two seats taken from whoever is in this yard and has no claim on the lot.” She looked around the room with the flat competence of a person who had counted a great many things. “I will not take a seat. I hold the steward key. That is the whole of what I have.”

Two people moved at once. A tram driver in a wet cap, who had been standing by the rail since six, and a woman with a tea tray and a boilermaker's burned forearm, who had come in behind the matron.

“Coll Ferrin,” the driver said. “I drive the outer tram. No claim on the lot, no claim on the road, no money in it.”

“Marda Osk,” the woman said. “I sell tea at the tram arch. Same.”

Mara took the registrar's chair on a three-legged stool. Nessa brought the book and stood behind it with her hand flat on the cover. Ferrin and Oks sat on crates. Adrian was given a stool on the far side, apart from the table, where a person sits when a person is not the board.

---

“What the board can do,” Mara said, reading from the rule page Nessa had copied out loud at the appointment, “is refuse to record a closing, publish a result, call a review, and set or renew the public custody term. It cannot grant a use. It cannot waive a toll. It does not vote on whether people should be brave. Those are the four things and no more, and if somebody in this yard wants a fifth, they can have the fifth over my dead body, which is a sentence I am allowed to say because I have signed a lot book.”

Ferrin laughed once. Oks did not.

Adrian said, “The board can decide the order of the list.”

“The board can decide what it will write on the list,” Mara said. “That is a smaller thing and I would like us all to be precise about it, because in nine days somebody will say the board chose the medicine over the school, and the board did not choose anything. The board refused to record a preference. That is all it did.”

She opened the book to a fresh page and set the pencil to it.

“Three requests, no priority, no schedule. Ward Nine clinic line, fourteen cold boxes, outbound heavy load. School shelter group, eight children and two escorts and four crew of the medicine party, inbound, fourteen people. Ward market cooperative, a published three-day service, which I am recording as **not currently a right that exists**, because that is the honest description of it.”

She looked up. “Anything anyone here wants to say before I put a date on the top?”

Nobody spoke.

“Date,” she said, and made one.

Then she turned to Adrian. “Open the case.”

---

Opening the case required three people and a record. Mara, Adrian, and the sitting steward. Nessa Pell wrote the time and the reason and the condition of the seal before the wax was broken, and the breaking of the wax made a second mark, which was the point of it.

The token lay in a bed of oiled cloth. Up close it looked worse than it had a week ago. The hairline that had reached toward the center after the first crossing had not grown, exactly, but it had gone from a bright line to a dull one, and around it the brass had gone the color of a bruise.

Adrian put two fingers on it.

The writing came the way it always came, without light and without sound, small and plain, and it arrived in the order he had learned to expect: use, then toll, then the boundary he could not cross by wanting to.

```text
THE LAST FARE
USE: ONE ROUTE ACROSS ST. ORRA. TWELVE MINUTES.
TOLL: EACH TRAVELER LOSES THE REASON FOR THE JOURNEY.
CONDITION: CRACKED. A SECOND ROUTE MAY NOT BE AVAILABLE.
```

He read it twice. The second reading told him nothing extra, which was its own information.

He said it out loud for the board, because the board was the thing that had to carry it: one route, twelve minutes, every traveler loses the reason, and the object is cracked and may not give a second road. He said the last part first, because it was the part with the fewest words.

Then he put his hand flat on the crate to keep the room from going past him, and the toll echo came up through the floor of him the way it always did. Old coins on the back of the tongue. The yard going flat at the edges. His right hand going slack inside its own grip, the fingers staying a little open, like a man who had let go of something on purpose.

Mara wrote. **Condition read and stated. Crack advanced. No assurance of a second use. No assurance of a route that can be opened again.**

Marda Oks, who had not spoken until then, said, "So the next time you open that road, it may be the only time anybody gets."

"Yes."

"And nobody will know until afterward."

"No."

"And that is a condition of the sale, not a fault in it."

"That is exactly what it is," Adrian said, "and I have never had a piece of equipment in this room that was honest and cheap at the same time."

Somewhere near the rail, a boy said, "He says I do not have to go again."

Everyone turned. He was small and he had a school shelter coat over his knees and his shoes were dry, which was the whole of his argument. He was the one who had stepped back from the bus and asked Alma Dren whether her name still stood for him.

"No," Alma said. "You do not have to go again."

"Then I will say what it cost," the boy said, "because I was the only one who got to watch, and the eight cannot say it, and the two who stayed behind can only say what they agreed to." He looked at the board, not at Adrian. "You are all going to argue about who gets on. Somebody should be the one who says what it was like. I am volunteering for that and it does not cost anybody anything."

Nobody offered to argue with a nine-year-old who was volunteering to be a witness to his own exclusion. Alma Dren put her hand on his shoulder, and he moved out from under it, because he was a person and not a coat.

Mara wrote a line under the condition. **One refusing passenger offers to testify to the cost of the two crossings. Recorded. No role assigned to him; a witness is not a role a child is given because it is available.**

Adrian looked at the page and did not know what to add to it, which was a sensation he had had perhaps four times in thirty-eight years.

---

The courier came in at ten past nine, soaked to the shoulder, with two messages and no patience for the room.

She gave the first to Mara. "Selik Marne's review. It was set for the eleventh of the week. It is now the second day. Warden order, sealed this morning."

Mara read it and did not show it to the room, and then she decided better, and read it aloud.

"Reason given: the span's gauge housing is being replaced and the line is under reduced manifest until the housing is certified." She looked up. "That is the official reason. It is not the real reason. The real reason is that he is tightening, and the tightening started before the review finished, and he has the authority to do it because the review is supervised and the bridge is his."

Ferrin swore quietly. "The reduced manifest halves the slots."

"The reduced manifest halves the slots," Mara agreed, "and the clinic's fourteen boxes are not a small-cargo exception. They are a convoy. They are out of the official count."

The courier held out the second message.

"This one is not from a Warden."

Halloway Reed's clerk stood in the doorway with rain running off the brim of a good hat. He was young, and he was not smiling, and he was holding a sheet of stamped paper in a way that suggested it had been read many times by people who had chosen to keep it.

"A private buyer has paid," he said. "Full price, in coin, at the warehouse counter, at first light. He has bought the next crossing on the Fare claim, and he means to take it before dawn tomorrow, when the Wardens are thin and the clinic is loading." The clerk's eyes went around the room and came to rest, briefly, on the tea tray. "Mr. Reed says to tell Mr. Vale that a public board is a wonderful thing, and that a public board cannot open a road at four in the morning for a man who has already paid."

Mara's hand had already gone to the book.

Adrian looked at the three requests on the crate, at the dull hairline in the brass under the broken wax, at the four seats of a board that could not grant a use, at a boy on a rail who had offered to be the one who says what things cost, and at a woman from the clinic whose sister was going to be taken tonight or not tonight and who had heard a stranger in a wet coat say the word *first* out of his own mouth.

The medicine had ninety-six hours of coolant, and it might be the last road the token ever opened, and a man with coin was going to buy the next one before dawn, and the only thing standing between a forged claim and a bare-claimant accident in the dark was a panel that could not authorize anything at all.

"We defend the board first," he said. "The road can wait an hour. If it cannot, that is the road's business and not ours."

Mara looked at him for a moment, and then she turned to a clean page.

"Record the time," she said. "The registrar proposes to hold an open session at Lot 17 for the rest of the day, and anybody who wants to object to the sequence may come and object. That is not permission to use. It is an invitation to be in the room."
