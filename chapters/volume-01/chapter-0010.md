# Chapter 10: The Public Lot Book

Several days later, the rain had turned Lot 17’s floor into a map of muddy footprints.

The first part of the review had been held at the Brass Verdict Hall, where the clerk’s complaint book was officially entered. When the glass roof began to leak over the public table, Mara moved the hearing to Lot 17. The board had been moved to the north wall after the first crossing because it now made the ordinary sale floor useless for ordinary lots. She had chosen the wall herself. She said the old sale notices had been written for people who already knew which prices were dangerous. The new board was for people who did not.

She had also chosen the book.

It was a broad ledger bound in gray board, bought from a retired registrar who had used it for grain receipts. The cover did not say PUBLIC LOT BOOK. Mara had painted those words in white on a strip of old signboard and fixed it above the hinge with four brass screws. The sign was slightly crooked. She had not corrected it.

Adrian arrived before the room filled. He had slept, though the shadows beneath his eyes said that sleep had not been generous. His right hand moved differently after the toll echo. He could lift it, write with it, and close a sale. At the end of a long reading, the fingers tended to stay open.

“Your hand,” Mara said.

“It will close.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It is what I have.”

She set a cup of hot water beside him. “Drink that before you argue with the record.”

He drank. The water tasted of metal, though the cup was ordinary.

The token stood in a glass-fronted case near the board. It had been returned to Lot 17 under a documented arrangement. Mara’s red municipal seal crossed the case’s rear hinge. Adrian’s auction stamp marked the front. The case could not be opened without breaking a seal, and breaking the seal would create two records: the public one and the private one someone might try to hide.

The opening rule was written beneath the seal: Mara, Adrian, and the current public steward had to be present. The public board appointed Nessa Pell steward for seven days, renewable by the board. The board was four seats — Mara in the registrar’s chair, the sitting steward, and two that morning taken by a tram driver and the woman who sold tea at the tram arch, neither of whom had any claim on the lot. The vote was taken out loud in front of the open book, because a short term that nobody heard granted was not short. The term made the custody arrangement answerable; it did not make Nessa permanent.

The public charter from the crossing lay beneath a sheet of clear waxed paper. The page had been recopied twice. The first copy had blurred where rain had reached it. The second had a small tear repaired with a strip of linen from Orren’s satchel. Neither copy had replaced the original.

Mara opened the book to the first entry.

**LOT:** The Last Fare, cracked brass transit token, St. Orra Fault, in public custody.

**USE:** One route across St. Orra for twelve minutes, with a marked return at the Ward Nine platform. Repeated use may strand the route.

**BEARER:** Tarin Kest, sole driver and route operator.

**BENEFICIARIES:** The medicine convoy. Nine children in the school shelter group. Ward Nine’s waiting clinic line.

**RESPONSIBLE PARTIES:** Two escort stewards, named on a sealed page and absent from this book, and Oren Fisk remain with the shelter group and medicine crew on the far bank.

**TOLL:** Each traveler loses the memory of why they are making the journey. Return travelers pay the toll again.

**TERM:** One activation. Ends when the second of the two named return travelers, Tarin Kest and Dessa Rill, reaches the Ward Nine platform, or at twelve minutes, whichever comes first. If either fails to return, the Warden remedy applies. No renewal.

**WITNESSES:** Alma Dren, school shelter steward, limited to confirming disclosure and refusal for the shelter group, and remaining on the near bank to hear what the crossing did. Ewan Latch, contracted ward-market freight driver, independent of the buyer, bearer, and medicine purchase. Both witnesses are on this side of the Fault, and neither travels on the lot they witness.

**RELEASE:** The token returns to the public case. The handoff is entered in the book. No private transfer.

**REMEDY:** Warden suspension at the marked spur. Public notice. Any two independent witnesses, or an affected person through an accepting witness, may appeal before another activation. A new closing is required.

Below the entry, Mara added a line that was not part of the charter.

**OBSERVED RESULT:** The medicine and the shelter group crossed once. Sixteen people traveled: eight children, two escort stewards, four in the medicine crew, and two named return travelers. One of the nine children named as a beneficiary refused and did not board, and a relief driver off the St. Orra staging line gave back a place that had been held open for him. The toll was taken on the outward leg from all sixteen, and again from the two who returned. Tarin Kest came back able to drive the marked route and unable to say why he had carried the medicine. Dessa Rill came back able to name Ward Nine and unable to say why she had gone. No memory returned within the observed period. The token was damaged. The route was not shown to be repeatable.

Mara read the line twice before setting down her pencil.

“Does the board require that?” Adrian asked.

“No. The board requires what people agreed to. I am writing what happened.”

“Those are different.”

“They become the same when someone uses the result to sell the next crossing.”

A clerk brought in the first group of visitors. Selik Marne came with his bridge inspector, a young Warden named Nell Sarr, and a folded public review sheet. He looked at the token in its case, then at Adrian.

“The official bridge will be inspected under supervision,” he said.

“By whom?”

“By a registrar, a route keeper, and two people appointed by the ward market. The bridge remains under my operational authority until the review is complete.”

Mara raised an eyebrow. “That is a compromise.”

“It is a temporary one.”

“Write that the review can challenge the manifest requirement.”

Selik’s mouth tightened. “I will write that the review may examine the necessity of the manifest.”

“Not a promise that the names can be withheld.”

“Not a promise that an unverified person can be carried into a fault.”

Mara wrote: **Official bridge remains a lawful alternative; manifest challenge placed under supervised review.** She read it to Selik before he left. He corrected the word *challenge* to *review* and accepted the correction.

Halloway Reed arrived without a coat. Rain had polished his hair and made his boots shine. He carried a narrow ledger under one arm.

“I can manage the next crossing,” he said. “My crews have the carts, the lights, and the labor. You need a road that works, not a board that makes people confess.”

Mara set the counterfeit copy beside the public book. “Your copy has a hidden labor toll.”

“My claim has a visible labor term.”

“After disclosure.”

“After disclosure,” Halloway agreed. “That is why the carrier refused. Disclosure is useful only when there is another road to choose.”

“Your other road takes a false return point.”

“A false return point is still a return point on paper.”

Mara’s hand went to her red pencil. Adrian placed two fingers on the book’s edge, not touching her, a reminder that the public record was not a private argument.

Halloway looked at the fingers. “You have learned to keep a hand on the record.”

“I learned to keep one on the thing that was trying to leave the record,” Adrian said.

For a moment, Halloway looked genuinely impressed. “The public board has changed you.”

“It has made me harder to misunderstand.”

Halloway glanced at the token. “That is not the same as safe.”

“No.”

He left the ledger. Inside were pages of labor schedules, a price for every carrier, and a page of return points that did not match the official map. Mara did not confiscate it. She copied its title and placed it beside the complaint book.

“It stays available for challenge,” she said. “If a buyer claims a private route, the claim can be compared with the public terms.”

“That is how you make a market,” Halloway said from the doorway. “You make the lie compete with a longer form.”

Adrian watched him out. “He pays his crew.”

“He does,” Mara said. “That is why the problem is not solved by calling him a thief.”

At noon, Lina arrived with a courier carrying three copies of the charter and a paper bundle of kitchen receipts. She did not enter the municipal register. She placed the copies on the public table and kept one under her arm.

“The household location is not written,” she said.

“Correct,” Mara replied.

“The children’s names are not written.”

“Correct.”

“The school steward is named because she chose to be. The household is not.”

“Correct.”

Lina turned to the book. “If a public record can be challenged, it must be possible to know who was left out without making them explain themselves in the first place.”

“That is a difficult rule to administer.”

“Then administer it. Don’t call it impossible and keep the list.”

Mara’s pencil moved. She added a line beneath the observed result:

**NAME DISCLOSURE:** Beneficiary households may refuse public names. The steward and the count remain accountable. A private location is not entered in the public book. A responsible party who must be identified may be named on a sealed page held by the registrar instead of in the book, and the sealed page is opened only if the remedy is called. The right to refuse does not remove the bearer’s duty or the remedy.

Lina read it. “Good.”

“It is not complete.”

“No. It is better than pretending it is.”

Alma Dren had been standing at the back of the room since before Lina came in, and she had not come forward. Mara turned to her across the length of the book.

“You are the one who has to hear this.”

“I heard it at the board,” Alma said. “Eight of the nine are on the far side of a river with two escorts and a clinic crew, and the whole of their reason is sitting in a ledger in this room.”

“And if someone appeals the crossing?”

Alma considered the shelves, the sealed case, the crooked sign above the hinge. “I will say that I told them the toll before they boarded, that one boy refused and not one of us argued with him, and that I stayed on this side because the book needs somebody who still remembers what they agreed to. I will not tell them what it was like to lose it. I was not there.”

Mara wrote that down as a limit rather than a claim. It was the first line in the new book that said plainly what a witness could not do, and it was the reason the two who had not crossed were the two who could still be asked.

Orren arrived after her, carrying the broken-circle copy. He set it beside the old charter and unfolded the page until the incomplete circle sat beside the token’s mark.

“Every copy of the counterfeit has the same impression,” he said. “The master and the return record and the buyer’s claim. But the mark is on a regional heading no one in the city can read.”

Mara bent over it. The heading had been scraped away, but a fragment of a seal remained at the left edge. The mark was pressed with the same uneven pressure as the token’s reverse. One small notch in the outer line matched exactly.

“Where did you find the original?” Adrian asked.

Orren’s fingers tightened on the page. “In Halloway’s office. The manifest was underneath. It had a margin note about Sela Pike.”

Mara’s pencil stopped. “Your mother?”

“She is not a part of this lot.”

“I am not putting her in the public book without your choice.”

“I know. I am telling you the record was made.” He looked down. “I thought a name would put her back in the place the bridge took her from. It doesn’t.”

The room had gone quiet enough that the rain against the roof sounded like a second, smaller sale.

Adrian said, “A name can be evidence without being a restoration.”

Orren gave him a hard look. “Don’t turn that into a lesson.”

“I wasn’t trying to.”

“You are good at lessons.”

“I am good at records,” Adrian said. “They are not the same.”

Orren tapped the broken-circle mark. “Then tell me what this record can do.”

“Nothing yet. It can show that the counterfeit was copied from a common form, and that the form reaches beyond the current maps. It cannot tell us who made it, what place it names, or whether the Last Fare and the counterfeits came from the same source.”

Mara nodded. “That is enough for a public entry. It is not enough for a conclusion.”

She wrote a separate page under the counterfeit file.

**MARK:** Broken-circle closing impression shared by the Last Fare, the counterfeit claim, the recovered bus manifest, and an unread regional record. No location or person established. Public challenge invited. No inference recorded as fact.

Adrian watched her. “You could leave the last sentence out.”

“I could. It would be less accurate.”

“You could leave the whole mark out.”

“I could. It would be less useful.”

At the end of the day, Mara placed a cup of water beside the book and opened a new page. She did not write a charter. She wrote a heading:

**NEXT CLOSING: PENDING NEW TERMS.**

Adrian signed beneath it.

The promise was short. He had written a longer version twice and destroyed both. The short one said: *Before any next use, I will state the full known toll, the return toll, the unresolved terms, and the remedy. I will not use a private promise to make a public charter appear complete.*

Mara read it. “You did not post the inspection press as security.”

“Not yet.”

“You will need something inspectable before a more complex charter.”

“I know.”

“You do not get to know and then hide it.”

“I do not get to know and then hide it.”

She wrote **inspection press and repair-crew term: available, not posted** in the book. Then she added a second line: **A toll leaves the ledger when it is due. Security stays until a breach. The next charter must say which is which.**

Near evening, Dessa Rill came to Lot 17 to return a borrowed rain cape. She stopped just inside the door. Her eyes moved across the shelves, the inspection block, the old tram rails, and the new public board. She knew every object in the room. She did not know why she had come.

Adrian was writing a price for a lot of signal cable. He set the pencil down.

“Can you tell me where you are?” he asked.

“Lot 17.”

“And do you know the road here?”

She looked down at the rail seam. “Yes. It comes in under the west wall, past the fare box, then turns at the old bell post. You can’t cross the river wall if the lights are green. That means the driver is lying.”

“Who told you that?”

“I don’t know.” She touched the rain cape’s collar. “I think I did.”

Mara went to the signal book on the wall. Green meant the span was stable in the current Warden rule. Dessa had kept an older rule, or the toll had rearranged the memory. Mara wrote both possibilities and did not choose between them.

Mara opened the book and wrote the exact words. She did not write *memory loss* in the place where Dessa’s reason had been. She wrote **destination route remembered; purpose absent; no cause assigned**. The line made the loss smaller on the page and larger in the room.

Adrian wanted to add a price. A price would have made the moment a cost that could be balanced. He left the ledger closed.

Outside, a clinic runner brought the next shipment notice. The new batch was due at dawn. The medicine had a longer route now, a school shelter waiting on the far side, and a board that had not yet learned whether it could survive the next bargain.

Mara turned to the blank page.

“Someone will have to open the next closing,” she said.

“Someone will,” Adrian replied. “It will not be me alone.”

For the first time, she did not answer that he was wrong.
