# Chapter 29: The Bridge Comes Down

The thing Selik Marne had learned in the flood year, and had not told anybody, was that a closure is easier to obtain than a closure with a date on it.

A closure is a decision. A man can take one on a Tuesday in a wet coat and there is nothing anybody can do about it, and the yard will call it cowardice if it wants to and the yard is not the office. But a closure with an end requires somebody to state what happens at the end, and stating what happens at the end requires somebody to be accountable in six weeks, and that is the part that makes nine small trades with bad barrows go quiet in a room and start looking at the terraces.

So he did not go to the review to ask for a closure. He went to ask for a closure with an end and an inspection, and by the time he got there the closure had already happened and the inspection was a formality, and the only thing left in the room was his honesty about how he had done it, which he gave them in the first four minutes, because he had read somewhere that a man who does not explain how he won is a man who expects to win twice.

The Brass Verdict Hall on the ninth of the month had a table at the head of it and four chairs and a rail behind that, and the rail was full, which was not usual for a bridge review and was usual for anything Adrian Vale had ever been in.

He put the certificate on the table first, face up, because the rest of the argument depended on it and he wanted the room to have it in front of them for the whole time.

"Gauge housing, near abutment, rebuilt and resealed in March and certified on the same day by Dermot Vasey of the water office and by a second fitter whose name is on it and who is not here because she is at a well in the lower terrace." He put his thumb on it. "On the morning of the nineteenth, at twenty-three minutes to eight, that housing drew a line on its pilot channel. There is no instrument on that channel. Nobody can tell me why it moved."

He straightened up.

"Order of business, and I am going to say it before Mr. Quill can, because she has been in this building for an hour and has not written anything down, and the reason she has not written anything down is that there is nothing to write until I have said this, and what I have to say is not to my credit.

"I did not ask the water office to suspend this certificate. I sent a man on a cart at two o'clock on the afternoon of the nineteenth with two words on a slip, and the two words were *pilot line*, and Mr. Vasey came out at ten that night and we looked at it for the better part of an hour. And at about eleven that night, in front of him, in this room, I instructed that the pilot channel be left attached, in writing, marked *unaccounted, under examination*, and that I would carry supervised cargo and no passengers on it, because a certificate that says nothing is better than a certificate that says a thing a clerk will be asked about in six months. That was my decision and I have not reversed it in public and I am not going to now.

"It was reversed for me by a copy. I sent the reading to the public board at Lot 17 at midnight on the night of the nineteenth, in my own words, because I did not want a strange instrument in a drawer. On the twenty-first, at about four in the afternoon, the second fitter came out on her own, with Vasey, and suspended the certificate under the water office's own clause, and she told me that she had read the pilot on the morning of the nineteenth and reached the same conclusion and had said nothing, and that she was annoyed — not with me, she said, with the two words on my slip, because they had been enough that night and they would have been enough before noon, and I had spent three hours reading a piece of paper to find a sentence I already had. She was right to be. I am to be recorded as having been told so by the man whose instrument it was, in a room, in front of the registrar, and as not having argued."

Mara Quill wrote something down. She had been sitting with her hands flat on the table for four minutes, which Selik understood perfectly and had not mentioned, because she had come to the Hall that morning to stop him and had been in the room long enough to find that she was not going to be able to, and that was an expensive thing to watch happen to a woman who had built a process on the principle that anybody can be stopped by it.

---

Ansel Roak spoke first, because he was nineteen years on the line and the route keeper and the only man at that table for whom a bridge was a mechanism.

"Can I ask you a question that is not helping you," he said.

"Go on."

"You cut the manifest on the seventeenth. You said the halved slots had produced a queue you could not supervise. That was two days before the bus. What has changed in two days except that a road failed four streets away?"

"The queue has got longer and I have a certificate that is suspended," Selik said, "and the order of those two things in my answer is the order I actually think in, and I will not pretend otherwise. Mr. Roak, I cut the manifest on the seventeenth for a reason that was good, and I have not pretended since that it was a mistake, and the eleven in that queue are the proof that it was good. I am asking for the closure to be extended today for two reasons, one of which is the same reason and one of which is new, and the new one is that there is a certified instrument on this span which drew a line at twenty-three minutes to eight on the morning of the nineteenth, on an empty bridge, for reasons nobody can give me, and I have carried a hundred and sixty people on the strength of that instrument since the twelfth of March, the day I emptied a sound deck for nothing and was right to, and I did it because the instrument was certified by two people who are not me, and it is not certified now, and I am not a man who carries passengers on an instrument whose readings I cannot account for. I said that sentence in this building in March. It is in your own minutes from March. I have quoted it at myself, which is not a thing that makes me feel good, and I would like the room to have it in a record."

Ewan Latch, who was one of the two fresh witnesses from the first public charter and who had not witnessed since, said, "You are saying you would rather be shut than be wrong."

"I am saying I would rather be shut than be carrying people and not know," Selik said. "That is not courage. It is what a man does with a hole in his own floor when the alternative is asking other people to stand on it."

Then Oveta Kraus, who was the ward market appointee and who had brought a ledger of eleven trades, and who was the reason this review had not finished in ninety minutes.

"You are closing a bridge that four cargo boxes a day pass over," she said. "In a district where there are eleven claims in that queue. Where a cooper cannot get his staves. Where a miller cannot get his grain and a chandler cannot get his tallow and a signal hand cannot get to his own cut for eleven days running. And you want it extended. And you want it extended with a date, and a date means somebody has to answer for it in six weeks, and I have been the ward market's appointee for two years and I have never once seen a date in this room that anybody was still standing behind in month seven."

"I want the opposite of a date," Selik said. "I want a *publication*. I want the inspection to be a thing that happens in a public yard, on stated days, in front of anybody who turns up, and I want the days written on the wall, and I want the man who reads it out to be a clerk from this table and not from my office, and I want it to cost money that somebody has to find, and I want it to be expensive enough that the district feels it every month it goes on."

"That is not a date."

"It is a worse date, Mrs. Kraus, because a date can be quietly moved and a public inspection cannot." He put his hand flat on the table. "And I am going to be honest about the cost in front of this table, because if I do not, then Mrs. Kraus will say it in a yard in a week and be right, and I would rather she did not have to. Eleven trades in a queue. Four boxes a day. A span that has carried a hundred and sixty people a day for nine years with a route keeper on it and a flag and a list, and none of that is a small thing, and none of it is nothing. And twenty-two letters in a fortnight saying this district can manage without the bridge — eleven of them from people in that queue and eleven from people I have never had in this office — and I have read all twenty-two, and the two lists have four names in common, and I have checked that twice, and I would like it in the minutes, because a number everybody can check is the only kind of number I am asking this table to use."

"Four names in common," Delia Fenn said. "Then somebody has told the other half of this room a number that is not true."

"The five are a man whose sister is in the fever ward, a lamp-trimmer who supplies two houses, a rope-maker whose brother-in-law is over there, and two people who would not give a name and whose letters I have not read because they did not write them under a name." He said it flatly, without weight, and Oveta Kraus looked at him for a moment with the expression of a woman recalculating.

---

Then they called Adrian Vale, which he had not asked to be called and could not have refused.

He came to the table with no slate and nothing in his hands, and Selik noticed it and understood at once that the man had decided in the last fortnight that his hands were no longer evidence.

"I am not here to argue with the Warden administrator," Adrian said, "and I want to say the reason in the room before anybody asks for it, which is that I have nothing to offer in exchange and it would be an insult to sit here pretending otherwise. My road may not exist. I cannot read where it comes up. I cannot tell anybody on this table that it will open again, and I have told six hundred people that in a yard, in my own words, and I would like the hall to know that the man who said that is the man standing here."

"Mr. Vale," Oveta Kraus said, "I want to ask you a question and I want a plain answer and I do not care what it does to you. Is the public route safer than this bridge?"

"No."

"You have not thought about it."

"I have thought about it for eleven days," Adrian said, "and the answer is that it is not a question about safety, and everybody in this hall knows it, and I am going to say the true version, which is that a public route can be less safe than a controlled one on any given morning, and can be more safe on the day after, and that the honest way to hold a road that anybody can use is to publish everything about it, including the days when it is not safe, and the man who runs one. And I cannot do that, because when I published everything about mine it emptied a workshop and a ward market and four hundred people out of a yard, and because on the morning of the nineteenth I had nine lines in a tin and a room of six hundred, and I chose the room."

Nobody at that table said anything for a moment.

"Mr. Vale," Ansel Roak said. "You said you chose the room. Say the other half of it."

"The other half is that the room is not a machine," Adrian said, "and I have spent eleven days learning that, and I am not going to stand in a hall and pretend that a thing which can be read by anybody is a thing which is safe, because a page is not a floor. On the second of this month a carter out of the river stairs went over that footbridge in the dark on a claim with no fold in it, and he has three whole days gone out of him and a barrow-load of clinic stores at the bottom of a trench, and no page in this hall would have stopped him, and I would like the hall to hear me say it in that order: the page would not have stopped him. I am not going to be the man who says the book was enough, because I was the man who said the press was enough and the six hundred and twenty-nine coppers is what that sentence cost."

Selik looked at the table for a moment, and then at Mara, and then at the certificate, and Adrian watched him make a decision that was not about bridges.

"Mr. Quill," Selik said, "you have been sitting here for an hour without writing. Would you like to say why?"

"I would like to say that I came to this hall this morning to stop a closure," Mara said, "and I have been sitting here for an hour and I have not been able to construct an argument against it, and I would like that in the minutes in those words, because in four months somebody is going to say that the registrar reviewed the St. Orra closure and found nothing, and the truth is that the registrar tried and failed and the failure is the review."

"Then it is in the minutes," Selik said, "and I will add one line of my own, and I want it read in the hall as well as written. The registrar tried and failed, and I say that with respect, and I say it because in nine years this office has twice had a public challenge to a bridge closure and I have never once had a registrar try and fail, and it is the first time anybody has examined this, and I would like the examination in the record whatever it produced, and next time it will produce something, because she will come back with it and she is not a woman who fails twice."

"The registrar will come back with it," Mara said.

The motion was put at eleven minutes past eleven and the motion was not the closure. The closure had been the water office's certificate and the Warden's regulation, and neither of those belonged to the table, which was the entire point and which Selik said out loud so that nobody in the rail thought the table had been captured.

**THAT:** the St. Orra service approach be closed to all passenger carriage, including supervised and cargo exceptions, until a replacement housing is certified by the water office; that the existing manifest be suspended in full and not renewed; that the inspection be conducted on stated days, in a public yard, at the ward market's expense, the days to be published on a board kept in the trading places of the district, and the reading of the board to be a clerk's duty appointed by this table and not by the Warden's office; and that the order be published in full, with its end, and that the end be brought back to this table whether it is finished or not.

**AND THAT** the river stairs and the old footbridge over the service cut are not crossings under this order and are not declared to be safe, and are not closed by it, and that the Warden office will continue to place one marshal and one lamp at the south end of the footbridge in the passable hours, and will enter no name in any book in respect of that footbridge, and gives its reason, which is that a crossing without a manifest is not a crossing.

Four in favour. The ward market appointee voted against and said so out loud before she voted, and Selik entered the dissent in the minutes in her own words, which was that eleven trades were being closed for an instrument reading that four people had looked at and none of them could explain, and that the district would be paying for somebody else's unfinished work, and that she would bring it back.

The certificate came down off the wall and went into Selik's case, and he carried it out himself.

---

The yard at Lot 17 got the notice at four in the afternoon, read out at the gate by Marda Osk in the voice she used for tolls, and the yard made the noise it made.

Then the tenth hand of the signal crew stood up at the back, with his copied page in his hand, and asked the question everybody in the yard had been holding since the words *no passenger carriage, including supervised and cargo exceptions* had been read out twice.

"You have just closed the only crossing this district has," he said. "To everything. Including the boxes."

"Yes."

"Then how does anything get to the far bank at all."

"By the footbridge, in daylight, with a marshal and a lamp, five people on the deck at a time, and it is not a crossing and it is not supervised and it is not in a roll, and I will not put a name in a book for it, and I will not take responsibility for it either." Selik said it without heat, in the middle of a yard of two hundred people, in a room he was not in and never would be. "I have been telling this district for nine years that a road you cannot account for is not a road. I am not going to start making an exception for a footbridge in the worst month of my service because it is the only thing left and I am tired. If the ward wants that footbridge kept open then the ward has to say what it is and pay for it, and it has not asked, and I am not going to ask it on your behalf."

"And if the nineteen on the far bank starve? And if the nineteen on the far bank are still there?"

"They are still there," Selik said, "and I know it, and I closed the crossing on the seventeenth and the certificate came down today, and neither of those things is the reason there is no road for them, and I would like it said in this yard by somebody other than me, because I have said it in three rooms this week already and it has stopped sounding like a fact and started sounding like an excuse, and that is the point at which a fact becomes an excuse and I am watching it happen to me in real time."

Adrian Vale was standing at the rail.

He had not said anything for about ten minutes, which was the longest silence anybody in that yard could remember from him, and he had a piece of paper in his hand, which was the notice, and he was not reading it.

"You are right," he said.

The yard waited for the rest of it, and it came out slower and worse than anything Adrian had said in a yard in three weeks.

"On the nineteenth I stood on a rail in front of two hundred people and told them that the road I had for sale was safer than a bridge, and I did not say it in those words but I said it in every other way a person can say a thing, and every one of them was true and not one of them was about the nineteen people on the far bank." He folded the notice. "This morning a warden who has closed a bridge for nine years on one bad day is standing in my yard telling a room that the road which cannot be accounted for is not a road, and the room is treating him as the reasonable one, and he is, and it is me who is the other one, and I would like the yard to be very clear that this is not modesty. I have lost a workshop and I have not bought a road, and the bridge administrator has lost his instrument and closed his crossing and taken the blame for it in a public yard, in front of a hundred and forty people at the gate, and I have not done one single one of those things, and the difference between us at this moment is that I would not have closed it and he did, and he was right to."

"It is the most useful thing anybody has said in a week," Mara Quill said, from the crate, without looking up, "and I am writing it down, and I want it noted that the man who said it has been the wrong man in a yard every single day for eleven days."

---

Selik Marne did not gloat, and that took him more work than the motion did.

He shook hands with Adrian Vale in the middle of the yard, in front of everybody, which was not necessary and which was deliberate, and he said, quietly, in the middle of it, so that only four people heard it and two of them wrote it down:

"You published the failure and it is the only reason I got the certificate suspended, and I will not pretend otherwise, and I want you to know that I know it. If you had not put a list of the nine things that charter did not contain on a wall, there would be nothing in the district's hands to put beside a pilot line, and Dermot Vasey would have gone back to his four hundred housings and I would have carried a hundred and sixty people on an instrument I could not account for and slept perfectly well. So the honest accounting is that your disaster and my closure are the same event, and only one of us is going to get thanked for it, and I have made sure the other one is in the minutes."

"I would rather you did not do that."

"I am not doing it for you," Selik said. "I am doing it because the day a bridge closure is won because a road failed is a day the district believes that roads are the answer, and in four months somebody is going to stand in a yard and say *the public route failed, therefore the private road is the safe one*, and I have read that argument in other wards and it is always made by a man with a good record, and the good record is why the argument is dangerous."

He put his hat on.

Then he said the thing that Adrian did not know about until the evening, and which arrived in Lot 17's yard at about six o'clock from Culler Bray, who had been told not to say it and said it anyway, in the manner of a Warden who has been on a bridge for eleven years and considers an instruction to be quiet to be a matter of timing rather than truth.

"There is a drafting instruction in my office from the water office and the municipal clerk's office together, signed this morning, and it is not about the inspection," Culler Bray said. "It is for a standing order for a St. Orra crossing. One crossing, one office responsible for it, one manifest, and it does not expire. It is in a room with a door on it, on the second floor of the water office, and there is a man in that room at this moment who is not me, and I was told not to mention it, and I have decided that a thing which will decide what this district can do for the next ten years ought to be mentioned out loud, in a yard, before it is finished, and I would like Mr. Vale to understand that I am a Warden and I have done a thing tonight that will cost me, and I would do it again in the morning, and I am going home."
