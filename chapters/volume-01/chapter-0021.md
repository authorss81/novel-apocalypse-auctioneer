# Chapter 21: The Search Is a Public Thing

By twenty past one the yard at Lot 17 had stopped being a place where people waited and become a place where people stood in the wrong order, which was Adrian's diagnosis and the reason he was up on the crate with a slate instead of sitting on the stool where he had spent the whole morning.

The first party had come back at one o'clock with wet boots and nothing, and the second party had come back forty minutes later, and in the interval between those two facts the yard had arranged itself into a queue at the gate. Not a queue to leave. A queue to go. Forty, fifty, more coming down the terraces behind them, and the noise in the room was the noise a crowd makes when it has been told there is a thing to do and has not been told what the thing is.

Adrian had seen this shape before. It had a name in his head that he did not use out loud, and the name was *wanting to be useful at four in the morning*, and it had a body count attached to it, and he knew from long experience that the count was where the whole thing went wrong.

"Gates," he said. "Both of them. Chains on to a half."

Nessa Pell had the chains. Nessa Pell had the chains in about six seconds and then had a bar across them, and a second bar, and the noise came down about a fifth, and Adrian could hear the yard deciding to be a room again.

"You are not going to talk to them from up there," Mara said, at his elbow. She had come back from behind the inspection block with her hair wet and her jaw set in a way he had learned to read as *I have already been sick once today and I am not doing it twice*.

"I am going to talk to them from up there."

"You are going to tell them four things, and three of them are going to be about the Fault, and the fourth is going to be a promise you cannot keep." She held up the tablet. "Say the version I would write, and it will take four minutes instead of nine, and the nine is what we do not have."

"Say yours, then."

"I would not. I would make them ask me." She looked up at him. "You made them ask you at six o'clock this morning and it is the only reason anybody in this room knows a known from an unknown. So do it again, and do it now, before the fourth person in that queue decides the river is where the answer is."

He put the slate down on the crate and got his hands out of his pockets, which was a thing he had trained himself out of years ago and had to do deliberately, and then he stood in front of a crowd that had a door open behind them and put the thing he wanted most in the room on the rail where anybody could pick it up.

"Nineteen people," he said. "That is the number on the far bank. Four of the medicine crew, nine signal hands, four children of the second shelter group, and two of the three escort stewards. That is nineteen and I have got it in the book and the book is on this crate and anybody may read it."

The room made the small confirming noise.

"And there are nine of ours unaccounted for between the two banks, and I have told you what I know about that and what I do not, and neither of those lists has got anything new in it since ten past nine this morning, and I have not been to the river wall and I have not been to the maintenance spur and I have not touched the token today."

He let that sit, because the room had been waiting eleven hours for a man to say a new thing and the not saying was the news.

"I am going to ask you to go and look for them. All of you. In about four minutes I am going to open those gates and about two hundred people are going to walk out of this yard toward the river wall, and if I do not give you a shape for it you will get there in a crowd and you will put your feet in the ground where the prints are and you will find nothing, and then in three days somebody will say the search failed, and it will not have failed. It will have been trampled. And I have been on a platform in this city when a crowd with no shape was told to move, and I have written down what that costs, and I am not doing it again at fifty."

Somebody at the back said, "The Fault."

"Which is the fourth thing, and it is the one I cannot give you."

---

The terms took twenty-two minutes and he wrote them badly, twice, and threw the first version away in front of everybody, which Nessa entered as *first draft destroyed by the reader in the yard at ten to two*.

"Read the second one," he said. "It is worse than the first one and it is the one that is true."

Mara Quill read the second one, all six of the terms, in the flat voice she used for tolls.

"**A search is a public thing. It has a route, an end, a person who answers for it, and a way to stop it.** One: the route. From the gate, down the tram apron, along the marked spur to the old Ward Nine platform, then to the river wall on the near side, then to the top of the service cut. Nowhere else. Two: the end. At dark, or at four hours, whichever comes first, and the party comes back inside the gate when it is called, and the call is made by the person named in three, and nobody argues with the call. Three: the person who answers for this is Adrian Vale, alone, by name, in this book, and if anybody finds a thing I do not know about it, that is mine and not theirs."

The repair-crew foreman in the wet coat said, "And if you are the one who calls it in and you call it in early?"

"Then it is in the book that I called it in early, with the time and my name on it, and any one of two hundred people can read it tomorrow." Adrian looked at him. "That is why it is me and not the board. The board is four people who turn over. I am one man who can be named at the gate in the dark."

"Four: nothing is moved, nothing is lifted, and nothing is carried off, until two people have called out loud what it is and Nessa has written it down. Five: nobody goes past the river wall. The river wall is a line and the line is not mine, it is the Warden's, and I have asked for it and been refused, and the refusal is part of these terms."

He stopped, because Nessa was looking at him.

"And six," Nessa Pell said, slowly, "is the rule that was read to two hundred people at the rail on the day of the last crossing, and I have not written it on this slate because I did not think of it this afternoon and Mr. Vale did not either, and the man who wrote it wrote it for a charter and meant it for a room. Six: one person in this yard says stop, in public, without giving a reason, and the search ends, and the book records who said stop and nothing else."

"Yes," Adrian said. "It is the same rule. I did not think of it first. I have been reading it to people for four days and it took me until this afternoon to notice that it works on a room that is looking for a bus."

Nobody said anything, and the yard understood something at about the speed the yard usually understood things, which was a good deal faster than it would have an hour before.

Mara Quill put her pencil down on the crate and said, quietly, "Registrar's note, and I am not going to be small about it. That rule was written for a charter, and it exists because a toll had to be refusable before the case opened, and what Mr. Vale has just done is take a rule that was built for one purpose and put it on a search party, and the reason it fits is that it was never about charters. It was about rooms. Record it exactly like that and let somebody argue with it in six weeks."

"Enter it," Nessa said, and entered it.

---

Then the yard wanted to know about the Fault, and Adrian gave them the only honest version he had.

"I asked Warden Marne to let a party go past the river wall. He said no." He put his hands flat on the crate. "He did not say no to be cruel and he did not say no because he thinks I am a fool, although he thinks the second one. He said no because the top of the service cut is a place where the ground has been arguing with itself for seventeen years, and a party of civilians going onto it in a group is a party of civilians going onto it in a group, and he has watched one such party come back off a wall in a basket, and that was a bus with one person in it and a Warden on a rope."

"He wants a road under it first," Tarin's wheel man said, from the back. "That is what he wants. He always wants a road under it first."

"Then he wants a road under it first, and I have not got one, and neither has anybody in this yard."

And then a woman near the rail said, "Then who do we ask."

Adrian stopped.

It was not a hostile question. That was the thing that went into him about it, afterwards, when he was sitting on the stool with his hands shaking from something that was not the echo. It was said the way a person asks where the office is.

"The board," Adrian said.

"The board cannot grant a use," Nessa Pell said, from the crate, not unkindly. "The board cannot grant a use and it cannot grant a search, and the four seats on that panel are a registrar, a steward with two days of term left, and two people out of this yard, and if the four of us walked out to the river wall and told nine more people they could go past it, then in three weeks there would be a queue at that wall every morning of people we sent ourselves, and there would be no officer in the city who could say no to any of them, because the officer would be us."

The yard made the noise it made.

"Then it is him," the woman said. "Then ask him, and if he says no, then he has said it out loud in a yard, like everything else, and we can all stand here and be angry at him instead of at each other."

---

Adrian went and asked him, which took him forty minutes and cost him more than the four hundred and ten coppers eventually came to, and he did it in the rain and on foot because the Wardens' cart was faster and using it would have made it a request from an office.

Selik Marne was at the near abutment with two men and a lamp and a folded load sheet, and he had heard about the bell post's arm from a Warden on the spur, and his face when Adrian came round the corner was the face of a man who has been handed the one thing he did not want before breakfast and is going to be professional about it.

"Mr. Vale."

"Your marshal stopped my party at the wall at one o'clock. I am not here to argue about it."

"You are not." Selik folded the sheet. "I sent two men down the spur at half past twelve to walk the gravel and put four men on the west side of the yard, because the yard was going to go to the wall on its own inside a quarter of an hour and I would rather they went with somebody who counted them. That was before the complaint went up. It is a different thing now."

"It is a different thing now," Adrian agreed.

"You have written terms."

"I have written terms. I am going to read them to you and you are going to tell me they are wrong, and then I am going to do what you say, and I want that understood between us now, because I have been the man who writes the terms in this city for eleven days and I am asking you to write half of one with me, and I would like the yard to be able to see me do it."

Selik looked at him for a long moment in the rain.

"Say that again in a yard, in daylight, in front of a clerk," he said. "Not to me. To them. Because if you say it to me it is two men in the rain, and in a month somebody will say the Warden administrator wrote the public lot's search terms, and I will be doing your work with your name on it and my coat on the wall, and I have enough coats on walls."

"So I say it in the yard."

"Say it in the yard," Selik said. "Now read your terms."

Adrian read them, and Selik stopped him twice with a hand up, and at the first one he said, "The route. You have written *to the top of the service cut*. Whose top?"

"Mine, as far as the wall. Yours past it."

"Mine past it, and there is no top you may stand on and call it ground." Selik took the slate. "Give me that pen."

He wrote four things on the back of Adrian's second draft, in a small hard hand, and gave it back.

"One: I go. Not you. A Warden and a marshal of mine, at the wall and past it, to the cut, and they stop if the lamp goes out or if the ground under the ballast moves, and they come back inside the gate when the call is made, and the call is still yours. Two: the party on this side of the wall is a party of walkers, and a walking party does not go onto the ballast past the second rail, ever, for any reason anybody gives me, including a bus. Three: your sixth term is mine, and I will say it out loud in your yard in my own words at three o'clock, and you will not say it for me, because a rule that you say for me is a rule that I have not got. Four: your party walks the ground in a line and it walks it once, and whatever is there when it walks is what was there before it, and the book says nine men found nothing in an hour and not one word about what they did to the place."

Adrian looked at the fourth line.

"Four is a blame," he said.

"Four is a record," Selik said. "In six weeks somebody is going to ask why a search of the spur found the arm turned and no print in the gravel. And if the answer is *because nine men walked the gravel in a line*, that is an answer. If the answer is *because we looked*, that is a hope. I have spent nine years moving from hopes to answers. Do not make me do it twice because a man I do not like is standing in my rain asking me nicely."

---

Selik came to Lot 17 at three o'clock in the afternoon with four Wardens and said the fourth term in his own words in front of two hundred and forty people, and Adrian stood on the rail and did not say it for him, and afterward told the yard that he had asked for the terms and been given them and that this was the first time in eleven days that he had wanted another man's hand in one of his documents.

The gates opened at ten past three.

Nineteen people walked out of the yard of Lot 17, and Selik's marshal walked with them, and they went down the tram apron and along the marked spur in one line, in the rain, at the pace of the slowest person in it, and they did not go onto the ballast past the second rail, and they were back inside the gate at twenty to five with nothing, and nobody in that yard had been destroyed by the shape of its own wanting.

And the coordinator with the handcart was still at the rail at half past four, and Adrian went down the two steps to her, because he had owed her an answer since one o'clock and had spent the afternoon owing it.

"You are taking your cart to the abutment," he said. "I want to say that first, so you are not waiting on me. The span is open to supervised cargo. Four small boxes a day, one vehicle, one marshal, and the load sheet is the manifest. Your three boxes will go on that if you take them this afternoon."

"And the draught."

"I do not know about the draught," Adrian said. "I want to be honest about the fact that I do not know. It is a cylinder and the load sheet has a clamp on it and a cylinder is not a box, and if the clamp on that sheet is not certified for a pressure vessel then the marshal will refuse it at the scale, and if it is certified then it goes, and I have not seen the sheet and I am not going to guess at a thing that decides whether your ward gets a draught tonight."

The coordinator looked at him for a moment, and then she said, "All right," in the flat voice of a woman who had been given an answer with a hole in it and had decided to spend the afternoon on the hole, and pushed her cart out of the gate toward the abutment.

Nessa Pell entered: **three boxes, one draught, per the ward cooperative's second clerk. The reader states the boxes will be accepted and states that he does not know the position of the draught, and the clerk took the cart to the abutment on the strength of the part that was known.**

---

The second party came in at twenty to six and brought a boot.

It was in a fold of oiled cloth, and it had been lifted off the gravel at the platform by a man who had not touched it with his fingers and had then walked six steps away and had waited, which was the fifth term being obeyed by a man who had been in the yard when it was read out, and who did not know that anybody was watching him do it.

Nessa opened the fold on the crate, under both lamps, and nobody in the first three rows could see anything and everybody in the first three rows leaned anyway.

"Size?" Adrian said. "And tread, and age, and what is under it."

"Small," said the man. "A nailed heel, worn flat on the outside of the left, three of the nails gone. It is not one of ours. I have nine men on that spur and I know all twenty feet and I would stake my place on it."

"How do you know all twenty feet?"

"Because I have walked this spur with the same nine men for six years," the man said, "and you do not forget a foot in six years, and I would like that written down as the reason and not as a feeling, because I know what a feeling is worth in a book."

Nessa wrote it down as the reason.

"Where," Adrian said.

And the man told them, and it was this: the print was in the gravel at the platform, four or five yards short of where the bell post's foot stands, and it was pointing away from the platform and up the spur toward the river wall, and it was not a print made by anybody who had come in over the last four hours, because it lay under the edge of one of the eleven o'clock party's own prints and had not been stamped into by them.

"So it is before noon," Adrian said. "Say it that way. It was made before the first party walked that gravel this morning, and it is a left foot, and it is not any of ours, and it is going toward the wall and not coming back."

"Going toward the wall," Nessa said, half to herself. "Nobody walks to the river wall. There is nothing at the river wall."

"There is a wall," Adrian said. "That is what is at the river wall. And somebody went and looked at it, and did not come back along this spur, and I have got no idea why, and I am not going to invent one, and whoever writes one in that book is going to be a person I will find."

Then Nell Sarr came in off the spur with a lamp in her hand and a look on her face that Adrian had seen twice before and had never once enjoyed.

"Mr. Vale," she said. "Come and look at the post."

---

The arm was still pointing along the spur. That was the first thing, and it was the thing everybody already knew, and the yard had made a small disappointed noise about it on the morning of the crossing and had been chewing on it ever since.

But Nell Sarr had not come for that.

She had come because she had put her hand on the post, and the post was warm, and that was nothing, and because she had crouched down to look at the foot of it in daylight with a lamp because it was her job, and what she had found was on the underside of the arm where it sits in its socket when it is swung across.

"You do not clean these," she said, to Adrian, and then to the yard, because Nell Sarr had never in her life said a thing to a room quietly. "There is no one in this city who cleans a bell post. It is a piece of municipal iron that is four years past its service and the only thing anybody does to it is put a hand on it and swing it. In seventeen years the rust on the underside of that arm has been building up, and it has built up evenly, the way rust does on a thing that nobody touches, and it has been building up on both sides of the socket, and it is coming loose in a continuous sheet, and it has come loose in a continuous sheet because a hand has gone in there and lifted it."

She put the lamp down so the yard could see, and Adrian could see, and the yard could see: a clean arc about the width of two fingers, running the depth of the socket, where the rust had been broken away and there was nothing but bright iron underneath.

"Somebody swung that arm," she said. "Somebody put their hand in the socket and took the weight and lifted it and swung it round, and they did it inside a day or two of the crossing, because the iron under there has started to go again and it has had one night of rain on it, and if that arm had been standing since Tuesday it would be wet all the way into the socket and there would be no clean arc at all."

The yard was very quiet.

"So write it down," Adrian said. "Both halves. Write down that a hand has been in the socket, in a day or two of the crossing, and write down that there is no print in the gravel but ours and one boot that is not ours, and write down that I do not know who put a hand in it and I am not going to put a reason on it."

"And the boot," Nessa said.

"The boot goes in the book exactly as it is, and the arm goes in the book exactly as it is, and the two of them are not joined up in the book, because I cannot join them." Adrian reached for the crate the way he had done all day and stopped with his hand in the air, and put it in his pocket instead, in front of a yard that noticed. "Nessa. Add one more line, and I want it in my own words so that I cannot be talked out of it tomorrow. Add: *the reader has looked for a person to blame since one o'clock this afternoon and has not found one, and the reason he has not found one is that nobody in this city has a reason to turn a bell post four yards, and that is not the same as nobody in this city being able to.*"

Nessa wrote it, and read it back, and the yard heard a sentence get put into a book that nobody had voted on, which was the entire distance between a public thing and a private certainty, and it had been travelled in one afternoon by a man standing on a crate with wet boots on.

At the rail, in the last of the light, the Road House wheel man said quietly to Tarin's second, "He asked Selik for the terms."

"I heard him," the second said.

"Nineteen years I have watched that man give a crowd something to do," the wheel man said. "I have never once seen him go and get it from somebody else. I do not know what to make of it and I am not going to pretend I do."

Outside, in the dark, three streets west of the depot, the maintenance spur was four streets away and empty and entirely unsearched, and the arm on the bell post was pointing along the spur toward a river wall with nothing at it, and there was a print in the gravel under a print in the gravel, and the rain came back.
