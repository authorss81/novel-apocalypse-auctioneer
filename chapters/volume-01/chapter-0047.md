# Chapter 47: The Term

Adrian Vale stood on the tram apron outside the rail from about ten past five in the afternoon until about forty minutes to six, and about nine hundred people stood in a yard in the rain watching him stand there, and he had nothing to do for the whole of it.

He had done this for four weeks inside his own head and he had got very good at it. A term with an end on it means that somebody has to be the person who watches the end arrive, and a man who watches an end arrive does not get to look busy. He stood with his hands at his sides and watched the empty gate and thought about salvage, and about eleven minutes after the vehicle went out of the gate somebody in the second row put a hand on the rail.

At about ten past five, with the bus about five minutes out and the yard already able to hear its engine under the river wall, a woman in the second row put her hand on the rail and said, in a voice that nine hundred people could hear because nine hundred people in a yard go completely silent for nothing:

"Mr. Vale. Keep it open."

---

He did not get to answer for about four seconds, and in those four seconds about six other people said things, and the six things are in the book because Nessa Pell wrote all of them down in about eleven seconds flat, and it is the only time in five weeks that six people in a yard have said the same thing and been entered as six and not as one.

"Keep it open."

"My boy is on that bank."

"Four minutes. Only four."

"Mr. Vale, the road is *there* — hold it there."

"Twenty-nine is a number and thirty-three is a number and it is open—"

"Mr. Vale. Please."

And the last one was not shouted. It was said at the volume of a person who has run out of shouting, by a woman of about thirty-four in a wet mender's apron about four feet from him, and it was the only one of the six that anybody has ever repeated back to him.

**Rosalind Hesk**, thirty-four, a mender at the ward market, eleven years on that counter, and a ratepayer since she was twenty, and mother of a girl of eight who is one of the fourteen off the school roll, and who is on the far bank of a river right now with a chalk number on a wall and a return party that does not contain her.

"Mr. Vale," she said. "Keep it open four minutes. My Tam is on the other side and there is no way across and I have been told there is."

"You have been told there is what?"

"That if you hold it four minutes it can go up and take more." She was not crying and her voice did not go up. "Somebody in this yard said it at about two o'clock this afternoon. I did not see who. There was a crowd at the gate and somebody said it and about nine of us heard it and we have been standing here since two waiting for you to be able to do it."

---

**So Adrian Vale did the thing that nobody in this district has done for him in five weeks and he did it in about four minutes, in the rain, in front of about nine hundred people, and it is in the book in his own words because he wrote it down himself afterwards and would not let a clerk do it for him.**

"I am going to tell you what is true and it is going to take four minutes and none of it is good.

"**If the road is held past the term, the case closes.** That is written on the twelfth of this month in five sentences and one of them is mine, and I wrote it, and I said in this yard at the time that it was the fourth of the five and that the fourth was going to be the one that got used, and I was right, and every person in this yard is to notice that I was right about my own sentence.

"**A person who says the word has the right to stop it.** Not the bearer. Not the board. Not me. A person. Any person standing in this yard. And that is in this book and I did not write it that way — Mrs. Bode wrote the sentence and I entered it.

"And **there is no second use this afternoon.** A second use is not a longer first use. A second use is a second closing, and a second closing is a proposal and two fresh witnesses and a board seated with four seats named, and it takes about six hours, and I have been doing it since ten o'clock this morning.

"So the road does not open again today. Not at six. Not at nine. Not at midnight. **The last time it opens is the time it is opening now, and it is open, and the count is twenty-four and the count is right, and the case closes when the case closes.**"

He looked down at the woman four feet away from him.

"**Ma'am. Whoever told you at two o'clock this afternoon has just cost you something they will never know they cost, and I am sorry, and I am not going to find them for you in four minutes, and I am not going to pretend I could.**

"And I am going to answer the other half of it, because you are owed it and nobody in this yard has ever given it to you and I have read a book out loud in three kitchens in five weeks and I have read that book and it does not have it in it.

"**Your daughter is on the far bank and she is somebody's. She is on a roll and there is a man at the end of a goods shed holding a number and the number is twenty-four, and she has a roof and there are eleven people on that floor and none of them is a stranger, and there is a light on the ramp and there will be a light on the ramp tonight and tomorrow night.**

"And nobody has told her why she went. She will not be able to tell you why she went. She will know the road and she will not know the reason and that is the toll and it is in a book and it is what I read to you at about half past three this afternoon and you were in this yard, and I read it twice.

"And I know it is a bad thing to be handed at eight years old. I want it in the record that I know it, and that I have known it since the ninth of last month, and that I have spent five weeks trying to build something that does not do it to a child, and that it does it anyway, and that this afternoon I held the term at twelve minutes anyway, because the alternative was a longer road and a longer road is a road that somebody's child gets carried out of at some later date in a way I cannot describe in a yard."

Then he said the sentence that Selik Marne told him afterwards was the only thing in the day worth having, and he said it out loud, to nine hundred people, without being asked:

"**The limit is the only thing in this city that anybody can check.** Not the toll. Not the count. Not the witness. Not the man. The limit. Twelve minutes. Twelve minutes is on a sheet in nine copies and it is on eleven boards and every person in this city can look at it tonight and say *that is what it was*, and about nine hundred people standing in this rain can say that nobody in Alder Reach made it longer. **If we cannot check the end, we have not got a road. We have got a man with a token.**"

---

**And then somebody at the back of the crowd, about two hundred feet away, said the thing that cost the shipment, and he said it in the voice of a man who has staked his own money and is not asking anybody for anything.**

"That is fair," said a chandler of about forty-four from the upper tram arch, standing beside a handcart. "And I am not going to argue with a word of it, and I am going to lose about two hundred coppers because of it, and it goes in the book that I said so and am not calling anybody a robber."

Adrian knew the cart. He had walked past it twice.

**Amos Treft**, forty-four, of the ward market, a chandler and a coal factor, eleven years a ratepayer and the man who had voted *against* the rate on the thirteenth in a room where he had said *we are not against the office, we are against two words in the second paragraph*, and who had stood in that room and listened to a Warden administrator ask for a public review of his own order.

The cart had been on the apron since about four o'clock in the afternoon. It carried **a hundred and seventy pounds of coal in a handcart and eleven sacks of seed**, and the coal was for eleven households on the far bank who have had none since the middle of the month, and the seed was for a household at the foot of the receiving ramp that had nothing planted and had asked a chandler on this side to find out what seed cost on the other side.

"**It goes on the road at about a quarter past five**," Treft said, "if the road is held four minutes, and the road is not going to be held, and so it is going to sit in my yard behind a coal shed until Monday, and coal is not seed, Mr. Vale, and the seed will be a fortnight late whatever I do with it, and eleven households on the far side are going to be cold on Monday night and they will not be cold because of anything anybody in this yard did today.

"And I am not going to stand here and be a martyr about it. I would have made a very good thing out of four minutes and I would have paid eleven coppers to Mr. Reed for a place and I did not, and that is the only reason I am standing here with a cart instead of sitting down, and you can all check it with him, and he will tell you the truth because it is printed on page two.

"**So put my two hundred coppers in your book.** On my own figure, and on the ward market's rate sheet, and on whatever the yard thinks it is, and put the difference in as the last one is uncomputed and not as nothing, because a nothing is a number somebody chose."

Nessa Pell entered it, and read the three figures out, and they were **two hundred and twelve on Mr. Treft's own figure, one hundred and eighty-six on the ward market's rate sheet, and two hundred and forty in the yard**, and she entered them the way she enters everything, in columns, with the holder's figure not the largest by default.

And then she read out the line underneath, in her own hand, and it is the only time in this book's history that a trader has asked for that and got it:

> **THE FIGURE THE SHIPMENT WAS WORTH: ENTERED IN THREE COLUMNS. THE FIGURE THE SHIPMENT WILL COST: ENTERED AS UNCOMPUTED AND NOT AS NIL, BECAUSE THE COST IS ELEVEN HOUSEHOLDS WITHOUT COAL ON MONDAY AND ONE HOUSEHOLD WITHOUT SEED FOR A FORTNIGHT, AND NOBODY IN THIS DISTRICT CAN PUT A NUMBER ON THAT, AND A NUMBER THAT CANNOT BE PUT ON IT MUST NOT BE ENTERED AS NOTHING.**
>
> **AND THE REFUSAL: MR. VALE ASKED THE YARD FOR IT BEFORE HE ASKED THE MOTHER AND HE WAS TOLD THE ROAD DOES NOT OPEN AGAIN TODAY BY A MAN WHO SAID IT IN FOUR MINUTES IN THE RAIN, AND THE SHIPMENT DID NOT GO, AND NOBODY IN THIS YARD ARGUED WITH HIM, AND MR. TREFT SAID HE WOULD HAVE MADE A VERY GOOD THING OUT OF IT AND WAS NOT CALLING ANYBODY A ROBBER.**

---

**And at about a quarter past five the object reported, and it reported to about nine hundred people, and it did not report to the yard, it reported to the open page of the public book on the crate, which is the record of a closing that four people have signed and about nine hundred people have heard read out.**

Nobody touched it. Adrian Vale read it standing up, and it cost him an hour, and about nine hundred people watched a man read a page and watched his hand stay open and watched him sit down on an upturned crate about four minutes later with his right hand still open on his knee, and this time about forty people brought him water and one of them was the woman whose child was on the other bank.

Nessa Pell entered it, without being asked, because she enters every reading and its cost: **a reading, entered at about a quarter past five in the afternoon of the eighteenth, one hour, off the open page of the book on the crate and not off the object, the object being inside the vehicle in the Fault; the fourth entered in this month; and the reader is not to read again before the twenty-fifth of this month, and the gap is the reason the next opening is on somebody else's word and not on his.**

---

**And then the bus came down.**

Tarin Kest had it at the second rail east of the bell post at about ten past five, under the lamp, where the ballast drops, and he stopped it there, on the stamped plate, with the arm of the bell post standing four yards further along pointing down the spur where a hand had turned it, and the second rail joint held, and the lamp was lit because a lamp is lit on a return point and Tarin had asked for it and the ward market had paid for the oil.

The vehicle came down out of St. Orra at an angle that no drawing in this city has ever got right, and it came down slowly, and it did not stop anywhere else, and it stopped on the stamped plate, which is the whole content of a named pair: **a place, an approach, and the person able to hold them.**

And the release condition was read at the closing by Bett Ruhl, who cannot read, and who said so before she started, and who had asked for it to be read a third time on the sixth of this month and had it read a third time.

> "**I am reading words off a slate and I am not saying that I have understood them, and Mr. Vale wrote the condition and I have read it once and I am going to read it twice, and if anybody in this yard thinks I have understood it, that is their error and not mine.**
>
> **The object leaves this yard in the vehicle and it comes back to this yard at the closing, whether the vehicle comes back or not. And a returned object is not a returned bus, and it is not a rescue, and it is not evidence that anybody is alive, and nobody in this yard is to be told otherwise by anybody who has seen it come back.**"

And then the count, read out by Nessa Pell, in the voice she uses for tolls, to about nine hundred people in the rain, and it is the count and the tolls, and they are not the same number, and the whole of five weeks is in the difference.

> **OUTWARD: TWENTY-FOUR TRAVELLERS. TWENTY-FOUR TOLLS. Each of the twenty-four paid once, on the way out, and every one of them knew before they got on what they were paying and none of them could stop it and every one of them could have said the word in a yard and two of them did.**
>
> **RETURNING: SEVEN. Seven travellers, seven tolls, and a returning traveller pays again, and there is a ninth line in this book that says a second payment is not a smaller thing and is not the same thing and is not stated by this charter to be either, and the object did not state it yesterday and did not state it today.**
>
> **TOTAL TOLDS ON ONE ACTIVATION: THIRTY-ONE. NOT THIRTY-ONE PEOPLE. SEVENTEEN PEOPLE AND THIRTY-ONE TOLLS, AND THE COUNT AND THE TOLLS ARE COUNTED SEPARATELY AND BY LEG, AND ANY RECORD THAT STATES A NUMBER OF CROSSINGS INSTEAD OF A NUMBER OF TOLLS IS WRONG.**
>
> **REMAINING ON THE FAR BANK: SEVENTEEN. Six clinic hands and eleven off the school roll. And the eleven is the school roll's own number from the twenty-sixth of last month, and that is a coincidence and the tenth hand of the signal crew has entered it as a coincidence and has not entered it as a meaning.**

---

**And the seven came off, and they were walked to the platform, and one of them could not say why she had set out, and said so in a yard, in front of about nine hundred people, and it was not a tragedy and it was permanent.**

**Hester Vane**, twenty-seven, a cooper's wife off the lower terrace, a traveller on the outward leg as one of the fourteen and a named member of the return party, and the reason she had been put on the return party was that Tarin Kest had asked for seven people who could be counted and could be told, and she had been one of them, and she had been asked, and she had said yes.

She came off the bus at about half past five carrying a canvas bag that had been in the seat beside her, and she put it down on the platform, and the yard came about thirty feet closer, and nobody touched her.

"I know the road," she said. "I know where the turn is by the wall and I know which stair and I could take you there. I have been driving that run for six years and I know it the way you know a room in the dark.

"And I cannot tell you why I got on that bus.

"I have been sitting on that platform for about an hour trying to find it. Because a bag is not nothing — a bag is a reason. Somebody puts a bag on a bus and that is a thing with a reason behind it, and I know the bag is mine and I know what is in it, and I cannot get at the sentence that connects them. **It is not gone in a hole. It is gone the way a word is gone when you have said it four hundred times and it stops meaning anything while you are still saying it.**"

"What is in the bag," said somebody, and she answered him.

"Seed," she said. "About nine pounds of it, and it is somebody else's, and I have carried it up a stair in this city in the last three years and I would know the house, and I do not know the house. **I know the seed and I do not know why I am the one carrying it**, and I want that in a book somewhere, and I am not going to pretend it is a small thing to be saying in front of nine hundred people, and I am also not going to have anybody be kind about it, because kind is the thing I have had all week from people who were being kind about me about a count."

---

**And then, at about forty minutes to six, the token came back to the public case of Lot 17, on its own, without the bus, and the wax was broken again, and nobody in that yard said anything for about a minute and a half.**

Nobody had any idea how. The bus was on the near apron with its engine running and Tarin Kest in the driver's seat and the route-control cover shut and the slot empty, and a brass token that is cracked through the plate came onto the crate in the middle of a yard of about nine hundred people, and Nessa Pell had it covered with a cloth before anybody had finished getting near it, which is what she did on the nineteenth of last month and is in the book from that day.

They weighed it. Because it is that kind of book.

**Two hundred and eleven grains before. Two hundred and four grains now.** And the crack, which had run through the plate from the lettered face and stopped short of the rim, went through the rim on the eighteenth of this month, and there is a chip of the plate about the width of a thumbnail missing out of the reverse, and the two pieces of the edge are parted far enough to get a fingernail into.

**And the entry of the lot has said since the ninth of last month that it may not open again, and that repeated use may strand the route permanently. Nobody in this district knows, and the object has not said, and yesterday it said that it does not know which.**

---

**And the last thing that happened on the eighteenth of this month at Lot 17 happened at about twenty to seven in the evening, in the rain, with about four hundred people left in a yard, and Selik Marne said it to Adrian Vale on the tram apron where nine hundred people had been standing four hours before, and about a hundred and forty of them heard it, and it is in the book because Adrian Vale entered it himself and would not let a clerk summarise it.**

"You kept the limit," Selik Marne said, "and you gave the reason in four minutes in the rain, and the reason was good, and it is the right reason, and I am going to say the other thing and then I am going to go home.

"**I lost a crossing in this district that I have had for nine years because I wrote a condition with an end on it and then a man in a room with a door on it put a permanent office in a draft, and I have spent a fortnight arguing that the answer to that is a limit anybody can check.** And you have just stood on an apron and said the same sentence I have been saying all month, and it is to be minuted that the best argument in this city for a public road is a sentence a Warden's office has been making for nine years and nobody listened to, because the man making it had a bridge and a certificate and a queue, and the man making it this afternoon had a bus and a token.

"And here is the other thing and it is the only thing I have come back for.

"**The limit you kept was checked by you.** You said the limit, and you ended the use, and the yard watched one man on an apron put his hand on a page and a road close. And that is what I have been building for nine years and cannot get, and I am not going to be dishonest about it at seven in the evening in the rain.

"You did it for the right reason. The reason you said out loud is the right one and it is in a book now and I have read it and it is the best sentence anybody in this district has written about a road.

"And I think the reason underneath it is that you cannot afford to be wrong twice. And I am not saying that to be unkind, Mr. Vale, and I would not have said it at all four years ago, when I would have said you were hiding a security. I am saying it because **a limit that is checked by one man is a man and not a limit**, and I have watched a very good man build a very good instrument four times in five weeks and the instrument is very good and the checking is him, and I have watched him stand on an apron today and lose two hundred and twelve coppers of a stranger's coal on the strength of his own character, and that is the most honourable thing I have seen in nine years, and the minutes of this district are to have one sentence in them about what happens in three years when the man on the apron is tired, or sick, or has been bought something by somebody we have never heard of, or is simply a different man with the same coat.

"Because that is the whole of my nine years and it is not a complicated thing. **The rule on my ballast is not good because I wrote it. It is good because a bicycle policeman of twenty-five is out on it at four in the morning and it costs him nothing to be there and he will complain about it.** Nobody has to be the man. That is the entire invention. And the reason the bridge review table wrote *the order is correct and the district does not want it* in a market yard in the afternoon is that this district cannot work out how to have a rule that is not a man, and it has spent five weeks watching a reader try, and I am not going to pretend he has not got further than I have.

"You should be replaced. Not today and not by me and not by anybody who wants a fight about it. **But somebody, in about a year, in a room with four seats in it and the reading board and the notice nailed up in eleven places, should take the standing you have had for five weeks and take it away from you in public, and you should let them, and if you cannot let them then everything anybody in this district has read for five weeks is a note from a man.**"

Adrian Vale did not say anything for about nine seconds, and Nessa Pell timed them, because she times everything, and entered them afterward, and never told him.

And then he said: "That is fair. I am not going to answer it. You are right that I kept the limit and I am not going to tell nine hundred people which of my two reasons it was, and the book is to record that I declined to say, because a man who says *the real reason is the right one* in a yard of nine hundred is buying something, and I know what it costs because I have watched somebody else do it."

Selik Marne said, "So it is still in there."

"It is still in there," Adrian said, "and it will be in there at the next one, and if I am honest with you, Warden, I do not know how to get it out, and that is the first thing I have said to you in five weeks that I have not had an answer ready for."

**And the yard made the noise a yard makes at the end of a thing, and about four hundred people went home in the rain, and the board sat for about forty minutes with all four seats named aloud and entered the closing, and the closing is in the book, and the case was locked, and the custody term of the object ran from the twelfth of this month for seven days and expires on the nineteenth, and nobody in that yard thought about it, and that is the whole of what the nineteenth of this month turned out to be about.**
