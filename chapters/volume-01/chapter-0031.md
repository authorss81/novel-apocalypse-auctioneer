# Chapter 31: What the Inspection Costs

The first thing Ivo Stenn did, on the morning the inspection board went up in the counting room of the ward market on the terraces, was price what the district was about to be asked for by a clerk who was not him, because the ward market had been paying for other people's sentences all year and Stenn had learned to read a levy the way you read a bad joint.

The levy was the fourth one that quarter. He wrote all four of them in a column and the column was short and it was the shortest column he had ever written.

"The bridge levy," he said, to nobody, in the counting room, at about a quarter to seven in the morning, with the gas turned low because gas was a thing the market charged for. "Ninety-one coppers a quarter, the first, in the year before the flood, when the span ran four boxes and a half and nobody wrote anything down. Forty-four, the second, when they cut the slots. Nineteen, the third, when the certificate came off the wall and the appointees argued about who paid for the stair. And now this one, and this one is not a number anybody has set yet, and everybody in this building knows what that means, which is that the number is going to be set by whichever of us is willing to do the arithmetic in public."

He put the pencil down and looked at the board he had screwed to the wall himself, on his own time, with his own screws, because the ward market had a carpenters' rate sheet and he was not going to charge the market for a thing that told the market the truth.

On the board, in his fair hand, in four columns:

**WHAT THE ORDER OF THE NINTH ASKS.** The inspection on stated days, in a public yard, at the ward market's expense. The days published on a board kept in the trading places. The reading of the board the duty of a clerk appointed by the review table and not by the Warden's office.

**WHAT THAT IS IN MONEY.** Two clerks for the days. A yard hired and put up and taken down. A fitter's fee and a water-office fee, because a water office does not come out for nothing twice. The fuel for the lamps, because a public yard at four o'clock in the dark is a public yard in the dark. A notice printed and the notice nailed to eleven boards and the eleven boards kept in repair. The ward market's own share of the reading of the order, at four o'clock, to people who are carrying things.

**WHAT IT IS NOT.** It is not a bridge. It is not a date. It is not an end.

And at the bottom, in bigger letters, because he wanted it legible from the back of the room and because he had learned that a levy nobody can see is a levy everybody resents:

**NOBODY HAS SAID IT HAS AN END.**

---

The ward market counted at seven, on market days, and Ivo Stenn had been its freight clerk for six years and its cooperative secretary for two, and in that time he had said no to more people than he could count and had been proud of it in a way he had never once examined, because a man who is good at saying no has a job and does not have to have an opinion.

He had been right, too. He had refused the fuel on the twenty-sixth, which was honest, and he had refused to write it as a refusal, which was more honest still. He had said to a room of sixty that he was suspending because he could not carry a market's fuel and lamp rights into a yard where the price of the last crossing was still being argued about, and he had meant it, and he had known while he was saying it that the room would hear *suspended* as a softer word than it was, and he had let them, because he was tired and because a market is a machine and machines are run by people who are not looking at themselves.

What he wanted, and had wanted since the sixteenth, and had been refused twice for, was a published three-day service. That was the whole of his ambition and he had never told anybody that, including his wife, on the grounds that a man who says out loud that his deepest wish is a three-day delivery schedule is a man who has run out of things to want.

And what happened in the counting room on that morning was that a man with a handcart of nothing in particular put a piece of paper on Stenn's table, and Stenn read it, and could not tell whether the ninth line was fair.

The paper was a claim. Nine flat lines, which meant the new trade, which meant a man had written his own lie out where anybody could see it, which was the thing the boy at the tram arch had told a yard would happen. It was for a carrier and load and a term of five days across the east reach of the cross cut. Eight of the nine lines were things Stenn could have said were true.

The ninth line was this:

*The carrier is not required to return, and no return is provided, and the holder is not answerable for the carrier's whereabouts after the term.*

And Stenn read it, and put it down, and picked it up, and read it again, and the thing he could not get past was that he could not tell whether it was fair.

He had spent nine years pricing goods. He knew a false weight when he saw one. He knew a short measure. He knew what a tallow factor's greed looked like on a docket, and he had refused four claims in three weeks at the market counter, and he had stood in a yard on the second of last month and said *that is a good claim* out loud about a man who was an hour later at the bottom of nine feet of water, and had been right about it, and had not been thanked then either.

He could not tell about this one.

There was no fold in it. There was no lie in it that he could find. The word *not* in the ninth line was doing real work and the real work was that it was a sentence a lawyer had written and a lawyer does not write a sentence unless somebody is going to pay for the trouble of it.

He put it down and looked at his own column on the board behind him, the one that said **NOBODY HAS SAID IT HAS AN END**, and he understood then that the two things on the table in front of him were the same thing.

---

The eleven came at half past nine, which was the wrong hour and which he had chosen, because the ward market's freight room is a room with one door and a man who wants to be listened to will take it.

The copper merchants were first, and the copper merchants sent a voice, and the voice was a man named Ferrin Usk who had a stall and eleven years of standing and had voted with the ward market on everything since the flood year.

"We have been asked to pay for a thing that has no end," Usk said. "That is the whole of my objection and I would like it taken in those words."

"Noted," Stenn said. "Do you want to say what you would pay for instead?"

"I would like somebody to tell me the month it stops."

"Nobody knows the month it stops. The order of the ninth says the inspection goes on until a replacement housing is certified by the water office, and the water office has not said when it will be able to certify one, and a man in a room with a door on it on the second floor of that office is drafting a thing with no expiry in it, and I am told the two are connected and I am not going to guess at it in this room."

The chandler spoke next, and the chandler had nine claims in the queue and had been in the queue since the seventeenth, and the chandler was not Oveta Kraus and would have been the first to tell you so.

"I have a queue nine deep of people who cannot get to a market with me because a bridge is shut," the chandler said. "Nine. And you are asking the six of us who are not in that queue to pay every month for a bridge that none of us can use, to find out whether it can be used, and I would like somebody to explain to me why that is called a cost and not a tithe."

"Because it is a cost," Stenn said, "and I would rather call it a cost."

"To me they are the same word with a coat on."

"Yes," Stenn said. "And I am not going to stand in this room and tell a chandler with nine claims in a queue that his nine claims are the district's business, because they are not. They are yours. And the inspection is ours. And I have spent three weeks telling this room that those are different things, and this morning the two of them have come due on the same morning, and I am going to be uncomfortable about it in front of witnesses and nobody is to stop me."

Nobody objected to that, and the discomfort sat in the room for a while.

Then the signal hand stood up, at the back, with his copied page, and he was the tenth hand and not one of the nine and said so, because he had learned to say it in the first four seconds now.

"I am not going to argue about the bridge," he said. "My nine came over the footbridge on the eleventh and the twelfth, five on the deck at a time, with a marshal and a lamp, and every one of them is standing in this city tonight, and the far bank is still cut off, and neither of those two facts has bought this district a road. I have read the Warden's order. It says one office is responsible for the crossing at St. Orra and it is closed-ended, and somebody in this room read me the part where it stops being the Warden's job, and there is not one."

"That is not in the order," Stenn said.

"Then it is not in the order," the tenth hand said, "and I am the tenth and not one of the nine, and I have had a page with every number on it since the nineteenth, and there is not one number on it anybody has ever let me check, and the thing the ward market is being asked to fund is a thing nobody has agreed will ever be somebody's, and a district should not pay a monthly bill for an unanswered question."

And that was the sentence the room turned on, and it was not a clever sentence, and it was the fourth sensible objection to arrive that morning, and it was the first one Stenn had not been able to answer, and he had gone to answer it and found nothing, and what he actually felt about that was relief.

---

They broke at eleven for the copper merchants, who wanted their figure, and Stenn gave them a number that he had built in twenty minutes and that he knew was wrong in the way every number he builds is wrong, which is that it assumed the inspection lasted a year.

He was going to have to say that out loud in four weeks, and he wrote it on the board in the third column instead, where it was visible, and somebody would see it and ask him about it, and that was the correct arrangement.

Then, in the middle of the afternoon, the rate clerk of the ward market came out of the back office with a piece of paper and a face, and Stenn knew the face, because it is the face of a clerk who has found the answer to something in a ledger she did not know was a ledger.

"You wanted to know what the market has been paying," she said.

"I know what the market has been paying."

"You know what the market has been paying *for*," she said. "I have gone back eleven years on the paper levy. Not the bridge levy. The **paper** levy. Four coppers a quarter, every household of two adults and above, since the year before the flood, collected at the market counter along with the water and the lamp, entered in the book as *public notice and printed matter*."

Stenn looked at the column on the wall with **NOBODY HAS SAID IT HAS AN END** on the bottom of it.

"How much has it taken in eleven years?"

The clerk said the number, and the number was not large, and it was large enough that eleven years of it would have paid for the inspection for a considerable time, and the room did the thing a room does when a figure arrives that has been in a book the whole time.

"It is a paper levy," the clerk said. "It is for paper. The ward market has been collecting four coppers a quarter off every household in this district since the year before the flood for *paper*, and half of it has gone into nothing that anybody can name, and a third of it went into the notices for the bridge queue, which is the only set of notices in this district anybody has ever paid for that anybody actually wanted."

"How much of it is still being collected?"

"All of it," the clerk said. "The levy has not stopped. It is charged at the counter at six o'clock this morning, and it has been charged every quarter for eleven years, and nobody can tell me what a household gets for it, and I have asked, and I was told that it is public notice and printed matter, and I would like to know where the printed matter is."

Nobody answered her.

And then Stenn said a sentence that he had not planned and would defend for the rest of his career, and it was: "Mrs Halder, where is the printed matter."

And the rate clerk said: "I do not know. I have looked. There is none. There is a board at the gate of the yard that says *public notice* and there is nothing on it since the flood year except a chalk notice about a horse."

---

The ward market did not fund the inspection that day, and the ward market did not refuse it either, and the difference between those two things is the difference between Stenn's life before the nineteenth and his life after it, and it cost him a day to get there and it will be in the book.

What he did was this. He asked for the eleven trades to stay in the room, and he asked Mrs Halder to bring the eleven-year paper column, and he read out the whole of it, column by column, quarter by quarter, for about forty minutes, with the door shut, which is a thing he had never done, and which the freight side of the market found out about by the end of the week.

And then he put two things on the table.

"First. The inspection. It is going to be paid. Not by me, on my own authority, out of the bridge fund, because the bridge fund is a quarter old and it is two hundred and six coppers and I cannot spend a year of it on a thing with no end without a vote. And not by the six of us on the bridge levy alone, because Oveta Kraus is right and has been right since the ninth that eleven trades are being closed for an instrument four people looked at and none of them could explain, and the district should not pay for somebody else's unfinished work on a levy nobody can see.

"What it will be paid out of is the paper levy. All of it. For as long as it goes."

The chandler said, "That is four coppers a quarter, and the inspection is not four coppers a quarter."

"No," Stenn said. "It is not. I have the figure in front of me and it is roughly eleven times that, and I am telling you the true figure, which is that the ward market has been collecting for eleven years for paper, and the district has received no paper, and the whole of that is a sum of money that has been taken from about four thousand households for something that does not exist.

"So the inspection is not a new levy. The inspection is the first honest use of an old dishonest one. And the rate on it goes up from four coppers a quarter to about forty-one, and every household in this district will feel it every quarter it runs, which is exactly what the Warden asked for when he said he wanted it to cost money the district felt every month, and I did not understand that sentence when he said it and I have thought about nothing else for four days."

"Forty-one coppers a quarter," the copper merchant said. "A household in the terraces does not see forty-one coppers a quarter."

"No," Stenn said. "A household in the terraces does not see it either, and that is the argument, and it is the argument I have been making to this market since the fourteenth, and I have been making it about a road.

**A man who cannot examine a claim cannot fund a public process either.** The same person, at the same counter, paying the same money, is asked to trust a thing on a piece of paper that says *carrier not required to return* and has no way in this building to find out whether that is honest. And we ask it of them once a quarter, silently, for nothing, and then we wonder why they buy a page off a man with a shaking hand at four in the morning."

He picked up the nine-line claim and put it on the table in the middle.

"Second. I cannot tell whether that ninth line is fair. I have been doing this nine years and I have priced eleven thousand lots by the pound and I cannot read a sentence, and Mr. Pike is seventeen and teaches it at four in the morning on a table, and the Warden's inspection is going to be a man with a clipboard writing things on a board, and neither of those is a thing I can stand behind with a customer's money.

"So the market is not going to fund a bridge we cannot see the end of. The market is going to fund **a room**. A public reader, at the counter, on market days, that any household can bring anything to — a claim, a docket, a notice, a lease, anything with a mark on it — and be told, out loud, in words they did not bring, what it says. Paid out of the paper levy, same as the inspection, same quarter. And the eleven trades on this levy are not funding a bridge. They are funding the thing that lets a market know whether a bridge is worth funding."

The room sat with it for a while.

"That is a new levy on a new thing for a new service," the copper merchant said, "and you have told us a figure and you have not asked us."

"I have told you a figure and I have not asked you," Stenn said, "because I have been asking for eleven days and I have never once been given a yes, and I have started to think that being able to say yes to something is a thing a market has to be taught, the same way it has to be taught that a bad thing can be refused in public. So I propose the experiment, and it is entered as an experiment, with an end, and the end is a date, because I have watched this district refuse a date for a month and I have learned that it is the one thing you do not do."

---

Selik Marne was in the counting room at half past four the next afternoon, because he had asked to come and Stenn had said yes, and because a Warden administrator who does not show up is a Warden administrator who is not asking.

He listened to all of it twice and did not interrupt, and at the end he asked one question, and it was not the question Stenn had prepared for.

"Whose ninth line is it?"

"I do not know."

"Then that is the answer to the question, and I want it written down that way and not the other way round." He put his hat on the table. "Mr. Stenn, I have been shut since the ninth and I have had a great deal of time to work out what I actually want, and what I want is a permanent office. I have not said that in public yet and I am saying it to you now because you are the only man in this district who is about to put a sum of money on a thing he cannot see the end of, and I would rather be argued with by you than by a man in a room with a door on it."

"I thought you were going to argue with the market about the inspection."

"I am going to argue with the market about the inspection, publicly, on the day, in a yard, with the days on a board, and I am going to lose, because your traders are right that I have not shown anybody the end of it, and I have not got one, and a man who has not got one and asks to be paid for it every month is asking a market to fund a prayer." He said it without bitterness. "I have thought about it for nine days and I have decided that you are not wrong and that I would rather be refused by a market that understood the reason than carried by a market that did not. So the permanent office is going to be argued in public, and I am going to be argued out of it or I am not, and either way it will be in your book and not in a drawer, and that is the only thing I have ever wanted out of a process."

"Will you say, in a yard, that you wanted a permanent office?"

"I will say it in whatever room you put me in, in the words I used, and I will let it be written down that I wanted it, because a man who hides the shape of what he wants is how this district got a closed-ended order in the first place."

Then Selik Marne said the other thing, and he said it slowly, and he looked at Stenn the whole time, and it is the reason Ivo Stenn slept badly for a week afterwards.

"Mr. Stenn, I have a permit in this case with a year-eight water mark on it, and the reason it is in my case is that a roll on the near abutment is the reason a man's body was found, and his sister has carried that permit to three reviews and to nobody else's, and she is the only person in this district who has been right about something for eight years. I have never once asked her to be reasonable about it, and I am not going to now.

"And I want you to understand that when I say *I need a permanent office*, what I mean is that I need a thing that cannot be ended by a clerk in a room with a door on it, and that I have wanted one since the twelfth of March, and that I have a certified instrument on my span that drew a line at twenty-three minutes to eight on a bridge that did not move, and that neither of those things has an explanation, and that they are three hundred yards apart, and I have refused for nine days to put them in the same sentence, and I have said it out loud for the first time to a man I met four days ago, and I want to know what it sounds like, and it is to be read back to me."

Stenn wrote it down. He wrote the two things on two separate lines and he did not join them, and he said so while he was writing it, and the Warden watched him do it and nodded once, and neither of them ever mentioned it again.

---

The room filled again at six, and there was no gate and no marshal and no order read out, and that was the first honest thing about it.

The four seats came in and were named aloud, because they always were now: **Mara Quill** as registrar, which is not a vote and not independence; **Nessa Pell** as the sitting steward, whose seven-day term from the twenty-first runs to the twenty-eighth and has not been an argument in this room yet; **Sabin Tull**, a barrow-hauler with a bad hip and four refusals in nine days; and **Marda Osk**, who sells tea at the tram arch and had no claim on any lot in the district.

"We are not doing a board sitting," Mara said, from the crate, with the wax tablet on her knee. "I want that said first because half of these people have been in a board sitting and the last one refused to let a line be struck off a notice board. This is a public room with a person from the ward market's counting house in it, and the man in the room is the one who wrote the column on the wall, and I am going to read his column, and I am going to read it in the order he wrote it, and the first column is called **what the order of the ninth asks**, and it is the only column in the room that is not an opinion."

Then the copper merchant stood up and said: "Mrs Quill, before you read it. Understand that the ward market is not an office. I am not a clerk. I sell copper. And what I need to understand is why a thing I have to pay for is going to be read to me by a person who cannot be asked whether it is true."

"Because you are right that you are not a clerk," Mara said, "and I am going to answer it in front of the room and I am not going to give you a good answer, because there is not one.

"The thing you are about to pay for is a reader and an inspection, and neither of them can be asked whether they are honest, and that is not going to change this year or next. What changes it is that this week somebody in this district can be asked to read the ninth line of a claim out loud, and did not know it, and now they will, because a clerk with a column on a wall put it in a book and the book is a thing we can all read. That is a worse instrument than certainty. It is the best one anybody in this district has been offered this year, and I am not going to pretend that is the same thing as being right."

The copper merchant thought about it, and then he said, "Read the column."

She read the column. It took eleven minutes. She read the first column twice, because Stenn had written the word *required* once and then struck it through and written *does not have to* above it, and nobody in that room was going to ask a trader what a word he could not read meant. The man with the handcart read the ninth line of the nine-line claim aloud, slowly, twice, and when he came to the word *not* the second time he stopped and said, "That is the same word twice in one line," and there were people in the room who could not read and people who could, and both of those groups made the same noise, and Stenn wrote it down as a finding, because he had begun writing down the things that happened in his room as if he were somebody else's clerk, which is the only way he knows to hold a thing still.

Then the trader from the lower terrace stood up, on the way out, and put a paper on Stenn's table on his way past, and said, "Mr. Stenn, I have had a bad month and you have not made it any better and I am not blaming you, and I would like you to look at the ninth line of this one as well, because you are the clerk and I am not, and I would like to know whether I have been sold a thing that is a lie or a thing that is just dangerous."

It was a claim. It was nine flat lines and there was no fold in it.

And Ivo Stenn, who had spent nine years pricing goods and six years saying no, and who had wanted a three-day service since the fourteenth and been refused twice for it, read the ninth line twice and could not tell whether it was fair, and put it in a drawer with the other one, and did not sleep well.

Because the thing he had said to the room at four o'clock — that a man who cannot examine a claim cannot fund a public process either, and that the ward market's money is better spent on a room where anybody can find out what a piece of paper says — had turned out in the four hours since he said it to be a true thing, and a true thing that he could not do either, and he had not been prepared for that, and the reason he had not been prepared for it was that for three weeks he had been telling himself that the ward market's problem was a road, and there was no road, and there had never been a road, and what had happened that afternoon was that a market found out it was a printing office, and the printing was bad.
