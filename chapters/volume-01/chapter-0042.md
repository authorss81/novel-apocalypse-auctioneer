# Chapter 42: What the Job Is

Fen Ambrose left Lot 17 at four in the morning on the fourteenth with a tally sheet, a pencil with a bit of soap in the end of it, the school roll folded inside her coat against her body, a strip-book of blank strips cut to the size of the ones in a kitchen under the Ward Nine tram line, and a cold chisel in the outside pocket of her coat, which she had put there on Friday for a reason she had not told anybody and which was the reason she had slept as badly as she had.

The river stairs are a stair and not a bridge. They take one person at a time in daylight and they take about three hours, and there is no rule anywhere in this district that stops eleven people from walking down them together, and doing it in company is how a person comes back with a chest that will not settle for a fortnight, and Fen had watched two people do it in the last two years.

She went down alone.

---

**The goods shed on the far receiving ramp had a fire in a drum at the north end and a kettle on a ring and a bedroll and a dog, and eleven people, and none of them stopped the woman with the roll, and that was not hospitality, it was that nobody on that floor thought a roll applied.**

Four of them were on the school roll, which is the gym's roll and is nine years old and carries the shelter group and about nine people who keep the gym. Two were on the clinic's list of hands, which is a list of people who carry and is not a list of people. One was in the exchange's count, because a steward writes it down for us. That is seven, and the seven are the seven anybody in Alder Reach can find, and on the second of this month they were seven, and Fen had come down on the second and gone back up and told a yard of three hundred that the count of the twenty-sixth was wrong by four.

She had the four in front of her at about half past seven in the morning and it took her until about eleven.

---

The man was under the rail at the end of the floor again, mending a length of chain with a cold hand, and the strip-book had a cross in it against a bearer entry in a public proposal at Alder Reach, and the cross was hers and the entry was not, and she had told three hundred people on the second of this month that she did not know who had put it there.

"You have not been told what the job is," Fen said.

"No."

"I am going to tell you what the job is, and it is going to take me a while, and I am going to say it in the words it is going to be written in, and then I am going to ask you a question and the question is whether you want it. Not whether you will do it. Whether you want it. There is a difference and I have watched four months of people confuse the two and I am not doing it to you on a stair."

He put the chain down. It took him a while to stand up, and she waited, because she had a roll and the roll is the only thing in this district that cannot be improved by anybody's opinion, and waiting is the only tool she has that the Warden's office does not have.

---

**"The job is this," Fen Ambrose said. "You stand at the ramp. You stand at the ramp for the whole of the time there is a vehicle on this side of the river, and for no longer than that, and not one minute before and not one minute after.

"**Every person who comes off that vehicle is somebody's, and not a stranger's.** That is the whole of what the job is. Not that you are the far-side holder. You are the man who decides, for as long as it lasts, that a person arriving on this floor at four minutes past seven in the morning is somebody's and not a stranger's. And you do not decide that they are safe. You decide that they are somebody's. Those are two different sentences and this district has spent a month confusing them.

"So — a person comes off that bus. You ask them one thing and one thing only, and the thing is *is there anybody on the other side who has been told you are coming*, and you do not take an answer, you take a word. And if they say a name, the name is not written down. If they say a household, the household is not written down. If they say nothing at all, then they are somebody's, because they are on a roll somewhere, and a person on a roll is somebody's even if they cannot say whose roll.

"**And if they say no.** If a person comes off that bus and says no to being counted, or says no to being asked, or says nothing and looks at you until you stop — then they are somebody's, and what they are is a **decline**, and the decline goes in the count as a decline, and it is not a refusal of service, and it is not a stranger, and it is not a hole. A decline is a thing the count carries. That is what a count is for."

She stopped, and drank about a mouthful of water out of a stoppered bottle, and went on.

"**And here is the part that is the job and not the description of it, and I am going to say it slowly and you are going to stop me if I get it wrong.**

"**You do not go and look for anybody.**

"Not on the stairs. Not down the cut. Not along the bank. You do not walk off that floor to find out where a person went, and you do not send anybody either, and you do not decide who is lost and you do not decide who is searched for. The man who does that is the driver and the man who does that is the marshal and both of them are on the other bank and both of them are named in a book.

"**And if the bus does not come back, you do not go and find out why.** You hold the number you have. You write it on the wall if you have to. And then you wait, and there is a book on the other side that says what a man is supposed to do at four minutes past seven in the morning when a thing has gone wrong, and you do not do that thing, because you are not that man, and on the nineteenth of this city a man with a good shoulder and a bad day did that thing and it cost eleven hours and a child.

"That is the job. **It is a job where the whole of the work is standing still and counting, and the whole of the danger is in a man who cannot stand still.**"

---

The man at the chain looked at the tally sheet for a long time, and then at the floor, and then at the woman by the kettle, who had been awake the whole time and had not come over, and whom Fen had not spoken to either.

"Ask me the question," he said.

"You have to have the count first," Fen said. "Before I ask you, I have to be able to tell you what you are being asked to hold, and I cannot do that until I have that count, and the count is the hard part, and I am going to go and get it now, and you are going to hear every number I get, because that is the deal."

"Then go and get it," he said, "and I will tell you the second thing while you are gone, because you would otherwise have asked me the question first and then I would have said it and it would have looked like a condition and it is not a condition, it is what I have got."

---

Fen got the count in an hour and a half and it is the whole of the second half of the morning, and she read every number out loud on the floor, to everybody, as she got it, because the rule is that a count is public while it is being made and not only after it is finished.

**THE FLOOR OF THE FAR RECEIVING RAMP, THE FOURTEENTH OF THIS MONTH, BY FEN AMBROSE, SHELTER STEWARD, IN THE PRESENCE OF ELEVEN PEOPLE AND ONE DOG.**

**Seven are in books and have been since before the plank went: four on a school roll that is nine years old, two on a clinic list of hands, one in a count a steward wrote on a wall.**

**Of the four who were in nothing: two men, who were asked, and who answered, and who are entered as two.**

**A woman who has not been in a household since she was nineteen, who was asked, and who said no.**

**And a child of about nine who is in nothing at all and has never been and could not have been asked, and who is entered as not asked, because a person the steward cannot get a word from is entered as not asked, and not asked is not a refusal and is not assent, and it is a word and it is in the book.**

**ELEVEN IS THE NUMBER. TEN OF THE ELEVEN WERE COUNTED. ONE DECLINED, AND THE DECLINE IS ENTERED. ONE WAS NOT ASKED, AND THE WORD NOT ASKED IS ENTERED. THE NUMBER A MARSHAL WITH A LAMP CAN CHECK AT THE OTHER END OF A STAIR IS ELEVEN AND IT IS NOT FIFTEEN AND IT IS NOT SEVEN, AND BOTH OF THE OTHER TWO NUMBERS ARE IN THIS BOOK AND NEITHER HAS BEEN REMOVED.**

---

**"Now the second thing," said the man at the chain, when she had read all of that out. "And I want it in the count, and I want it in the same line as the yes, not above it.

"**The count goes in the book with the four in it.** Not with the seven in it. With the eleven, and the decline in it, and the *not asked* in it, and my two in it. And when that count is published it is published with the names of nobody in it, and the four are in it, and there is no household named, and nobody is to be told later that the count was of households.

"And the second thing, which is the only one I am actually asking for.

"**Nobody asks her again. Ever.**"

He did not point at the kettle and neither did Fen.

"Not today and not at the rate meeting and not at the yard on the seventeenth and not by the counter reader and not by the signal hand with his page and not by the man who comes down the stair in six weeks because there are always six weeks. **Nobody asks her anything, about anything, ever again, and the reason she is on this floor in a count instead of a line is that she has been asked four times in three weeks and three of the four were by people who were being kind about it, and that is the worst way to be asked.**

"I am not asking for her to be protected. She does not want protecting and I have known her eleven days and she would have said so. I am asking that the answer she gave be the end of it. She gave an answer. It was no. It has been written down. **An answer is a thing that is finished.**"

---

**"And that is the price," Fen Ambrose said, out loud, on a goods-shed floor on the far side of a river, to eleven people and a dog, at about one in the afternoon. "I am going to say the price out loud in front of all of you because I have got a rule about that and I am not going to break my own rule on a floor like this one.

"**I got a yes today and it cost her the rest of her life being asked.**

"And here is what we bought with it, and it is a bad thing and I want everybody on this floor to hear it in the plain words.

"Every count in this district is checked by asking. That is what a count is. You ask people and you add them up and if the two numbers are different then somebody has been missed and you go and ask again. That is the whole method. It is worked on the roll and it is worked on the list of hands and it is worked in the exchange's count and it is worked on the rate books, and it is how this city has checked a number four thousand and one times in eleven years.

"And this one cannot be checked by asking. She has said no and there is no asking her again, and so the only way anybody in Alder Reach can ever check whether eleven is the right number on this floor is for a man with a lamp to stand at the top of that stair and count heads going past him. **And he counts heads. He does not count people. And it is the only count in this city with no way to be found wrong out loud.**

"I am not saying that is not worth it. I am saying it is what we paid, and I paid it, and I am not going to go up that stair on Friday and stand in a yard and let it be a good afternoon."

The woman by the kettle said, from about nine feet away, without getting up: "That is the first true sentence anybody has said about me in a year, and you did not get it by asking me anything, and I have decided not to be gracious about it, and you should tell the registrar I said that too, because she will want to know what I am and I would rather she had it from my own mouth and in those words."

"I will tell her exactly that," Fen said, "in those words, and I will not add anything to it."

---

Then she took the cold chisel out of her coat and showed it to him, and he looked at it and then at the pin in the rail and did not move.

"That is a mark," he said.

"Yes," Fen said. "It is yours, or it is your household's, and it is a mark and not a name, and it is the standing record of a refusal, and the refusal is not going anywhere and the mark does not go anywhere either. **A mark is a person's refusal of a name and it is the only thing about a mark that is permanent.** What comes off it today is the job.

"I put that mark in a public proposal on the twenty-ninth as a bearer. I did not put it there — I carried it. It came off a wall in a kitchen where a steward had written a count under a household, and I carried it into a proposal and I made it the fourth bearer of a road, and a man with a bad shoulder is in a room in the lower terrace because a second person put the same mark in a clause at two in the morning and never saw the first document, and neither of them did it to hurt anybody, and one of them put a man on a ballast.

"So the mark is finished carrying jobs. It does not stop being a mark. **A mark is a refusal and a refusal is a real thing and this one has been real for years and it is not a job and it never was.**"

She turned the head of the pin over on anvil-stone off the end of the rail, and she did it in about six minutes, and the cross came off as a bright ring of metal around the head, and she knocked the ring flat with the shoulder of the chisel and the pin went into her pocket.

"**Pin and ring both, and the ring is going in the strip-book, and the pin is going back on the rail with nothing on it, because the pin is a pin.** And the mark is retired from the fourth entry of the proposal of the twenty-ninth and it is retired today and not on the day the charter opens, because the entry has been standing for three weeks with a job on it and every week it stands is a week somebody could put a second one on it."

And she wrote the strip, and the strip is the whole of a month of pieces of paper, and this is it, and Nessa Pell read it out loud at Lot 17 on the morning of the fifteenth in a yard with about four hundred people at the rail, and read it again because Bett Ruhl asked her to:

> **INDEX OF THE USE OF A MARK. FOURTH ENTRY OF THE PROPOSAL OF THE TWENTY-NINTH. A CROSS, BEAREN BY MARK, FOURTH BEARER, FAR RECEIVING RAMP.**
>
> **RETIRED THE FOURTEENTH OF THIS MONTH BY FEN AMBROSE, STEWARD WHO CARRIED IT, IN THE PRESENCE OF ELEVEN PEOPLE. THE PERSON IT STOOD FOR WAS ASKED ON THE FOURTEENTH WHAT THE JOB IS AND WAS TOLD, IN WORDS, AT LENGTH, AND SAID YES.**
>
> **THE MARK IS NOT DESTROYED AND IS NOT A JOB AND WAS NEVER A PERSON. IT IS A REFUSAL OF A NAME AND IT REMAINS A REFUSAL OF A NAME. WHAT CAME OFF IT TODAY WAS THE FOURTH BEARER ENTRY AND NOTHING ELSE, AND THE ENTRY IS RETIRED AND NOT STRUCK, BECAUSE THE BOARD CANNOT STRIKE A LINE.**
>
> **AND THE SAME CROSS IS THE MARK IN THE FOURTH PLACE OF THE READ-OUT OF THE SEVENTH AND IT IS THE MARK ON THE PAPER THAT WAS NAILED TO A GATE IN THE SERVICE-CUT YARDS, AND NEITHER OF THOSE TWO IS THIS ONE, AND NOBODY HAS SAID THEY ARE THE SAME PERSON, AND THIS ENTRY DOES NOT SAY THEY ARE.**

---

**"Now the last thing," Fen said, "and it is the only reason I came down a stair for three hours, and it has taken me three weeks and a strip-book to work out, and I have got it in about four minutes now because I have been turning it over since the second.

"The proposal of the twenty-ninth has a return crew in it. It is the fifth entry, and it is a schedule, and it is not a bearer, and nobody has ever written down what those three people are for. **A crew of three on foot from this ramp to the near apron.**

"So I have worked out what three people on foot from this ramp to the near apron do.

"**They do nothing.**"

She said it in front of eleven people and a dog, and she said it flatly, because it was the only true thing she had.

"It is three hours down that stair and three hours up it, one at a time, in daylight, and this floor has no daylight to spare in the afternoon, and if a bus does not come back then three men walking to the near apron arrive at about nine at the earliest having crossed nothing, and what have they learned? That the stair is open. That is all. They have learned that a stair is open.

"And if the three set off *before* the bus is late, and the bus is not late, then the three have walked six hours to arrive somewhere they were already, and they are not on the floor when the vehicle comes, and **the job, which is standing still and counting, has been done by three men who went for a walk.**"

The man at the chain said, "So the clause is a walking clause."

"So the clause is a walking clause," Fen said, "and it is in a public proposal, and nobody had noticed for three weeks, and the only reason I have noticed is that I have been sitting on a floor for three weeks with nothing to do but add up."

---

"So here is what the charter has to say instead, and I have said it to the yard in my own words and I am saying it here first, and if the yard changes it then the yard's is the one that counts and not mine.

"**A returning party, or a party cut off, is held at the far receiving ramp by a named holder, and is counted, and is not sent anywhere. If the vehicle does not return, the holder does not leave the floor and does not send anybody, and holds the count where it is, and the count is published in the morning whatever the count is.**

"**And the holder is not the crew.** The holder is a man standing still. The crew of three on foot is not a rescue and it is not a search and it is not a fetch, and if it is written into a charter as any of those three things then somebody in about four weeks is going to rely on it, and there are eleven people on this floor who would rely on it, and one of them is nine years old and is mine.

"So the crew of three is either struck or it is given a job that is not walking. **And I cannot write the charter, and I have not been asked to, and I am a steward and I count and I do not close anything.** What I am doing is going up that stair on Friday and putting a hole in a document in front of a room, and I have wanted to do that for about a fortnight and I have not been able to, because I did not have the arithmetic."

She folded the tally sheet into the roll against her body, and put the strip-book in the outside pocket, and put the chisel in the other one, and stood up on a goods-shed floor with a bad knee.

"And the last of it, and then I am going, because it is about a quarter past one and it is three hours and I am not twenty-two any more.

"**There is going to be a far-side responsible party and it is not going to be him and it is not going to be me.**

"He is the holder. The holder holds a number. A responsible party answers. Those are two different jobs and this district has been running them as one for a month, and the reason it is going to come apart on the day is that the man who is going to be holding this floor in the dark is not going to be the man anybody can go and shout at when a count is wrong.

"So somebody on this floor has to be named as the party who answers, and be asked first, and be able to say no, and I do not know who that is, and I am not going to guess at it on a floor at one o'clock in the afternoon, and it goes up the stair as an open thing with my name on it as the person who did not solve it."

The dog got up. Nobody had counted the dog. The dog was not going into the count and had never been going into the count, and the eleven was eleven, and the dog was a fact about a floor and not a person and not a number, and Fen Ambrose wrote it on the tally sheet anyway, in the margin, because it is the only entry in the whole of this month's paperwork that has nothing to do with anything and she wanted there to be one.
