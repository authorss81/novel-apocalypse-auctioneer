# Chapter 12: The Manifest Question

The Brass Verdict Hall smelled of wet coats and hot paper, and by late morning both had become the smell of the room itself.

Mara Quill had the long table at the west end to herself, and she had covered it with duplicate forms. Not one stack. Nine. Every sheet a bridge permit printed in triplicate at the near abutment, a Warden dispatch, a fuel return, and a passenger manifest with its columns ruled in a hand so upright it looked like a threat. She had asked the ward market for the forms on the theory that a review conducted on the reviewer's own paper was a review conducted by one person, and the ward market had sent nine hundred of them, and the hall's clerk had asked her twice whether she had made a mistake.

The glass roof leaked in three places. The leaks had been arranged by the building.

The review had four members besides herself and the Warden who held the bridge. Mara said the names out loud before anybody spoke, because a body that had not been named was a body that could be argued with later.

"Registrar, municipal. I am not a safety vote and I am not independent of the complaint. Route keeper, Ansel Roak, tram line and outer apron, nineteen years. Ward market, Ewan Latch, contracted freight, no interest in the span. Ward market, Oveta Kraus, chandlery and two river boats, no interest in the span."

Roak was a heavy man with a bad knee who had brought his own rule and a length of blue chalk. Kraus had brought a lantern and a question. Latch had brought nothing, which Mara noted, because a man who brings nothing to a review has usually decided to be honest in it.

Selik Marne stood at the head of the table with his coat off and his sleeves cut above the elbow, as he had been at the bridge in every meeting Adrian could remember. Nell Sarr sat behind him with the gauge log and the sealed order that had moved the review up four days.

"The question before this table," Mara said, "is written. I will read it as written, and the Warden administration has already accepted that it is the question."

She put the sheet flat.

**Whether the named-manifest condition of the official St. Orra crossing is necessary for the safety of the people it carries, and if it is necessary in its present form, what narrower condition would carry the same safety.**

"Read it again," Kraus said.

Mara read it again.

Selik put his thumb on the table. "Necessary," he said. "In its present form, no. I will give you the narrower condition, because I have had eleven days to write it and I wrote it before your courier arrived." He drew a folded sheet out of his coat and set it down without ceremony. "The span is supervised, the counterweight is manned, and the load board is enforced. What I will not do is carry a person I cannot name to a place where a fault may put them in the water."

"Named to whom?" Roak said. "To you? To the sheet?"

"To the sheet, to the near abutment, and to a person on the far side who can be sent for if the sheet says who."

"And when the sheet says nobody?"

"Then nobody is carried," Selik said, "and I have kept a great many people out of a river for twenty-two years by being the man who says that."

Mara let that sit. It was the argument in its strongest form, and she was not going to improve it by touching it.

---

They went through the record first, because Selik had insisted on the record going first and Mara had agreed, and agreeing had been the only way to get the record into the room at all.

He had brought eleven bound volumes. He laid them out along the table like a man laying out a body.

"Year five. Harrow interchange, east span. It reported clearance and clearance and clearance while the ground under the second pier moved nine inches." He turned a page. "Eleven people were told the ground was fine by a structure that had no way of knowing. Four were in the water. Year nine, Orra service cut, the same fault in a different coat. Year fourteen, a footbridge at the salt wharf that a contractor's foreman certified twice a week and that a boy found soft in April."

"And the manifest in those cases?" Kraus said.

"Does nothing. The manifest is not a structure."

"It is a record of who was on it," Mara said.

"It is a record of who was owed an answer," Selik said. "That is not the same as keeping them dry, and I have watched people confuse the two because the second one is easier to put in a form."

He was right, and Mara wrote *the Warden administrator's stated position: the manifest does not bear structural load* without irony, because it was what he had said.

Delia Fenn came in at half past ten, and she was not on anybody's list.

She was sixty-one or near it, with lamp-trimmer's hands and a burn along the inside of one wrist that had gone the color of weak tea. She had come from the tram arcades with a cloth bundle and no coat, and she put the bundle on the table and unwrapped it.

Inside was a bridge permit, eight years old, water-stained, with a passenger column and a dispatch stamp.

"My brother," she said. "He went over on a Sunday in year eight. The span reported fine. The span reported fine at four in the afternoon and he was in the water at four in the afternoon, and the roll on the near abutment had his name on it, and a marshal ran the roll to the far-side households in under an hour, and they found him at my sister's door at supper."

Mara looked at the permit. "And they told you."

"They told me. That is the part I have not been able to put down for eight years." Delia Fenn's voice did not rise. "Everyone tells me that if the bridge had not had the roll, I would never have known where to look. You are all sitting here deciding whether the roll is a burden on the poor. I am the reason the roll exists. I came to say so, because somebody should have said so before you decided."

Kraus had the grace to look at the table.

"And who sent you?" Roak asked.

"A lamp-trimmer at the arcades named Ossie Prent sent me." She said it evenly. "Ossie sells trimmed wicks to Mr. Reed's warehouse. He also sells them to the clinic. He was paid a day for the walking, and I am here, and you may put both of those in your record."

Mara put both of them in her record.

---

Petra Sill had been at the hall since nine, with her daughter Bel on her hip and a ration chit folded into a square.

Bel was five and burning with the fever that came up out of the river-cut water, and she had been burning for two days, and there was a clinic on the far bank at Ward Nine with eleven cots and a matron who had told Petra at the river stairs on Tuesday that the child would not last the week without the fever draught.

Petra was not on the roll. Her sister's name was on the shelter list and her sister was in the Salt Verge and did not know Petra was in the city. Petra's aunt was the person who was supposed to meet them on the far side, and the aunt had a name, and the name was not the problem. The problem was that nobody on the far side could be shown to be expecting a child called Bel who belonged to a household that had never existed in any record the Wardens could read.

At half past ten a marshal came along the line and gave her the slip from the inspection: a provisional berth, one crossing, return marshal present, destination Ward Nine fever ward, confirmed by a public shelter.

Petra had gone forward with the child. She had got as far as the near ramp, in the mouth of the span, with the marshal's hand on the rail and Bel's face against her neck, and there she had stopped because the span made a sound.

---

It was not a large sound. That was the part that mattered afterward. It was a single knock from inside the middle section, the kind of sound a cooling plate makes, and then the gauge on the near abutment began to sound.

The gauge did not have a voice. It had a needle, and the needle went across, and a bell in the housing began to ring, and a red flag came up on the far abutment before the third stroke.

Selik did not raise his voice. Mara had heard three men in her life give an order in that volume, and one of them had been Selik Marne.

"Off the deck. Everybody. Counterweight locked, no exceptions, and I do not want a single pair of feet still standing out there when I count." He was already moving. "Nell, the housing. Roak, the near abutment. Nobody goes back for a bag."

Mara was on her feet before she had decided to be. The hall emptied toward the doors in under a minute, the four review members among them, the nine stacks of forms abandoned under their weights.

What she saw, in the two minutes after, was a hundred and sixty people standing in the wet on the near side, and a hundred and sixty people on the far side who had been in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday, and no way to tell anybody on either side that the instrument and not the bridge had made the noise.

The span did not sag. Mara watched it the whole time and it did not sag; the green lamp stayed green and the red lamp stayed up, and the only thing that had moved in the whole structure was the needle in a brass housing on the near abutment.

Petra Sill was on the near ramp.

She had got onto the ramp because the marshal had told her to, and she was four steps out from the abutment with Bel on her shoulder, and when the order came she went back the way she had come, and she could not run, and the crowd behind her had to make a channel for her or not make one at all.

Somebody did make one. Mara did not see who, and later nobody could name her either.

They got her back to the queue. Bel had gone from crying to the awful stillness of a small child who is too hot and has decided that the safest thing is to stop. Petra sat down on the wet cobbles of the approach with the child on her lap and the provisional slip crushed in her fist, and the line closed over her, and after a while she stood up and took her place in it again without being asked, because if she lost her place in the line then the day was over, and she was not going to let a bridge make the day over.

The whole business took eleven minutes.

---

Nell Sarr came up out of the housing with her sleeves rolled and her face the color of the wet stone.

"Float," she said.

"Explain it to the table," Selik said.

"The float is a sealed brass cylinder on a wire in the sump. It rides up when the chamber fills." She held something up between two fingers. It was bent. The bend was not dramatic; it was the kind of bend a thing gets when a spanner is dropped on it in a hurry and nobody says anything about it. "Somebody carried fuel across at seven this morning and set the can down on the housing lid. The float took the edge. It has been reporting the chamber a finger lower than it is since about nine."

"Since before the alarm?" Roak said.

"Since before the alarm. It reported a change today because somebody on the far side knocked the wire with a spanner and the chamber moved for a second and a half." She set the float on the table. "The span did not move. The chamber moved. The float lied about it."

"Then why was it called in as a structural alarm?" Kraus said. "If your needle reports a chamber, a man reading it should know the difference."

Nell looked at the gauge log and then at her own boots, which was an answer about a person.

"The first call was not from the needle," she said. "A Warden on the far side saw a line on the second pier and called it movement. He was not wrong about what he had seen. He was wrong about what it was."

Mara stood up. "Show me."

They went out onto the approach, in the rain, in a file, with Nell leading and a Warden holding a lamp over her shoulder. The second pier had a maintenance gang's chalk mark on it at knee height, a depth figure for a winter draw-down, and in lamplight from below, at the angle a person standing on the deck would see it, it read as a fine vertical crack in the concrete that opened toward the water.

Mara looked at it for a while.

"That mark is older than the gauge fault," Roak said. "That is months."

"Yes," Mara said. "So we have had a sound span with a lying instrument standing behind a mark that looks exactly like the thing we are most afraid of, and a Warden on the deck who did the only thing a Warden on the deck could do with what he had."

"And who pays for the eleven minutes?" Selik said. He was standing at the end of the pier with his arms folded, wet through. "Not the fault. I want to be exact. I am not asking anybody to pay for a fault I made. The fault was a dropped can and a bent float and a chalk mark in the dark. I made the call. I made it in a second and a half, and I would make it again, and here is why."

He turned to face the tables and the forms under the leaky roof.

"Eleven minutes ago that span reported a movement it could not explain. If I had waited to explain it, I would have been explaining it with a hundred and sixty people standing on my deck, and the person standing furthest from the ladder would have been the person who paid for my wanting to look careful in front of witnesses." He nodded at the near ramp, where the chalk mark still was. "A structure that can report a fault it cannot account for is not a structure I carry passengers on until I know why. Not for a week. Not for a child with a fever. So the line is closed until the housing is rebuilt and certified, and that is not caution, it is arithmetic."

Somebody in the hall said, "That is caution."

"It is arithmetic performed by a cautious man," Selik said. "You are free to write down which of the two you think it was."

Mara wrote down both. She wrote the false alarm in the same entry as the evacuation, on the same line of the same page, and she made sure the two stayed in one paragraph, because the thing she had learned in eight years of disputes was that a precaution separated from its cost reads like prudence, and prudence does not have to answer anybody.

---

When they were back inside, Oveta Kraus had gone out to the approach and come back with Petra Sill.

"Her provisional berth is void," Kraus said, "because the line is closed."

"She has a feverish child and a void berth," Mara said.

"She has a feverish child, a void berth, and the fact that the last time somebody told her she could cross, the child was carried back down a ramp in front of a crowd." Kraus put her hands flat on the table. "I am not the registrar. I am the chandlery. But I will say that the record today contains a woman who was moved forward and then moved back, and if the record does not say who she was and what it cost, then the review is a review of a bridge and not of the crossing."

Mara looked at the manifest stacks.

"Registrar's note," she said, and wrote it out. "Petra Sill, adult, one child, five years, fever, destination Ward Nine fever ward on the far bank, provisional berth granted at ten thirty and voided at ten thirty-one by the evacuation. She was moved back along the approach. She returned to the queue without being asked. The berth is not reissued and the child is not treated. Recorded."

She read it back to Petra Sill, who stood at the end of the table with the crushed slip in her hand.

"You do not have to agree with any of it," Mara said. "If you want your name off it I will write *a guardian and a child* and no more, and the appeal still works."

"My name is not the part that is wrong with it," Petra said. "Go on. Write it down. He asked to be the one who says what things cost." She tipped her head toward the door.

Adrian was standing in the doorway with rain on his coat, and he had been there some time.

---

The break for the false alarm cost them forty minutes and cost more than that on the near side, and when the table reconvened Selik went straight to the record, because he wanted the tightening on the table while the room still had the sound of the bell in it.

"Narrower condition," he said. "Four parts, and I will accept them into the review record without argument."

Mara had her pencil ready.

"One. A named responsible party, not necessarily a passenger, must be physically present at the near abutment for the whole crossing and must be answerable there. Two. Any child under twelve requires a second adult name, and the second adult is on the deck, not on the far side. Three. The gauge housing is rebuilt, sealed, and certified by two people who are not mine before any passenger crossing of any kind, including a supervised small-cargo exception. Four. Supervised slots are halved until the housing is certified, and the halving is posted at the abutment, not at the hall."

"And the group designation?" Roak said.

"Gone."

"Altogether?"

"For the duration of the rebuild. A group designation without a person on the abutment is a group of strangers to my marshals." Selik looked at Mara. "That is the part you came to argue for and I am refusing it, and I would like the refusal in the record in my own words, because in six weeks somebody will say I never considered it."

Mara wrote: **Group designation refused for the duration of the gauge-housing certification, on the Warden's stated ground that a designation without a person at the abutment leaves the marshals with strangers.**

"I am going to disagree with that publicly," she said.

"I would think less of you if you did not."

"And I am not going to pretend your history is worthless. I have been in your hall. I have read Year Five and Year Nine and I have a brother whose file contains the phrase *accepted risk* in a sentence where it does not belong." She set the pencil down. "The question before this table is still the question. Is the named manifest necessary *in its present form*. You have shown me that a structure does not need a roll. You have not shown me that the roll in its present form is what is dangerous. Those are different arguments and the room has been treating them as one because one of them is easier to win."

Selik's mouth tightened. "And your remedy."

"Supervised group designation, with four conditions." She turned the sheet around so the table could see it. "The group is named by a public shelter or a ward market officer, not by the crossing. The group carries a count and a destination. One named responsible party travels, or stands at the abutment, and the marshals have a person to send for. A sealable page held by the registrar names whoever must be identified and is opened only if the remedy is called. And any affected person may challenge the designation in public, in this room, without giving a reason and without being crossed first."

"That is a registry with a lid on it," Selik said.

"It is a record that does not become a map of a household," Mara said. "I am not offering you a way to carry strangers. I am offering you a way to carry a person you cannot name and still know whom to send for."

"You are offering me a registrar's private page on my span."

"Yes. That is the whole cost of my proposal and I am not going to pretend it is free. The cost is that the person who can open the page is me. If I am bought, coerced, or simply wrong, then the people on that page have no remedy. If you use my page, you are trusting a person instead of a form, and you have spent twenty-two years learning not to do that."

The room was extremely quiet.

Selik picked up Nell Sarr's gauge log, squared it to the table's edge, and put it back down.

"I will accept the question," he said. "The question survives. I will not accept the remedy, and I will not accept the lid, and I will say in the record that a registrar's sealed page on a working span is a single point of failure dressed as a protection."

"Write it," Mara said.

"And I will say this." He looked at her without any heat at all, which was the worst way he could look at anybody. "I have had a fault I could explain and I have moved a hundred and sixty people off a bridge in eleven minutes for it. In six weeks, if a fault comes that I cannot explain, the people I move off will be the people who are hardest to move: a child on a back, a woman with a bundle, an old man who will not let go of the rail. That is not a reason to keep the bridge open. It is the reason your page has to exist, and I am not putting it on my span until I know who answers when the answer is my own fault."

He put two fingers on the ninth form in the nearest stack.

"Halve the slots. Post it at the abutment. Certify the housing. And get the clinic's fourteen boxes off that convoy list, because they are not a small-cargo exception and they will not be treated as one while I hold this bridge."

"That is your answer to the clinic."

"That is my answer to everybody. The review continues on the ninth." He gathered his coats. "Registrar. You were right about one thing, and I will give you it in front of the table because I do not intend to be a man who takes a concession back to his office. I did not consider the narrower condition, because in twenty-two years nobody has ever stood in front of me and asked for one. That is not a credit to you. It is a fact about how long I have been doing this alone."

---

They cleared the table after midday. The nine stacks of forms went back into the hall's stores in four crates, and Kraus carried two of them herself, and Nell Sarr took one to the abutment to be posted, and the rain stopped while they were doing it, in the way rain in Alder Reach does when it has been asked to make a point.

Adrian caught Mara at the foot of the hall steps and did not touch her.

"A week ago the bridge would have taken the clinic's boxes," he said. "Today it will not take them, and it will not take the shelter group, and it will not take the fourteen people on the far bank who are waiting to be collected, and the tightened line runs until your ninth, and the coolant on those boxes does not care when your review sits."

"I know the calendar."

"Then we do not wait for the ninth. The token is cracked. It may be the only road it ever opens again. I have a board that cannot authorize a single thing, a driver who forgets why he drives, and a memory toll that takes the reason out of everybody who uses it." He was talking faster than he had meant to. "The question is whether the market waits for the safer road or accepts a bounded public one before the safer one exists. And if it is the second, then the second is going to have to be tonight or tomorrow, and the terms will be written in a hurry, and I—"

"And you will write them well," Mara said. "That is the part I am afraid of. Not that you will write them badly. That they will be very good, and late, and shaped by the only person in the city who can read what the thing is going to do."

"That is a fair way to put it."

"It is the fairest way I have." She rolled her waterproofed paper tighter. "The clinic's matron came to the hall at six this morning to find out whether the boxes could cross. I sent her back with a slip that says nothing. I would like to be able to tell her something by dark, and I do not currently have anything."

"Then find something."

"I intend to." She looked at him. "Adrian. Whatever you do next, do not open that road because I asked you to and do not close it because I told you to. If the next crossing is a public one, it has to be a public one on a day the market would have chosen it anyway. Otherwise we have not built anything at all. We have built a road that runs when one frightened man decides to build a road."

Petra Sill was waiting at the bottom of the river stairs with Bel asleep against her shoulder and the crushed provisional slip flattened out and folded into her pocket, which was a thing a person did when they intended to keep a thing.

Alma Dren came down the last four steps to meet her, and Mara watched the shelter steward take the child without asking a single question, and watched her turn and go back up with the whole weight of a small hot body in the grey canvas coat.

"They will sleep in the school gym tonight," Alma said, when Mara asked. "Nine children, one adult, and now a mother and a child whose names are not in anything, which is two more mouths and no paper. I am telling you that because somebody has to be able to say it out loud, and you are the only person in this city with a book."

"Then I will say it out loud in the book," Mara said, "and I will not write the names."

"Write the two extra mouths. The names you can keep." Alma started up the stairs again, and turned. "The next time the board sits, one of the things you are going to have to decide is whether a remedy can work at all for a person the record cannot point at. I have been thinking about that for two days and I have got as far as *it had better*, and that is where I have got to."
