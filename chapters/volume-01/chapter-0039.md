# Chapter 39: Four Streets and a Wrong Count

No bus was going anywhere on the seventh of this month, and Adrian Vale understood that about eleven seconds after he got to the end of the marked spur with a lamp in his hand and four people behind him and one person in front of him, which was: the published terms of the sixth had a line in them that said the fourth bearer holds the marked line at the top of the service cut, and there was no fourth bearer, and the line was still up there.

The line was not a rope. It was six feet of tarred twine between two iron pins, put in by a Warden bicycle party four years ago on the strength of a surveyor's chain, and it marked the last piece of ground a person not being careful should stand on. Above it, the ballast of the old maintenance approach went up to the crown of the service cut. Below it, four streets west of the depot, the ground went down into a place where the earth had been arguing with itself for seventeen years and had never once settled on a side.

The lamp was Adrian's, because Adrian had come, and that was the first wrong thing about the morning and nobody had noticed it yet.

---

The party was six. Adrian, and Nessa Pell with a slate and a lamp of her own, and a Warden of about twenty-five with a bicycle and a rope, and a signal hand who had come because the line was a signal line, and two men out of the repair crew who had not been asked to be useful and had come anyway.

And Selik Marne, who came out to the spur at about eight in the morning on the seventh because a walking party was going onto the ballast of his approach and the fourth term of his own search of the nineteenth said that a walking party does not go onto the ballast past the second rail, ever, and which the book of Lot 17 had carried ever since because Adrian had asked him for it in public.

He did not bring anybody. He came with his own lamp and a roll of chalk and he was in a wet coat, and the first thing he did on the spur was chalk a mark on the second rail and say nothing about it, and the second thing he did was stop the party at it.

"You are not going past that," he said.

"We are not past it," Adrian said. "That is the whole of what we are doing. We have come to look at the top of the cut and we are going as far as the line and not one inch into the ballast."

"You are going to be at the line in about four minutes and then you are going to be arguing with me about the four inches, and I have had that argument with a Witness in a yard nine times and I always lose it, and this time I am not going to lose it, because the fourth term is the only piece of my own work that has never been wrong. The rule is the fourth term of my search of the nineteenth, the flag is out of the flood year, the twine is four years old, and the three of them are the same rule and I have never written a different one. It is on the wall of the abutment house with the date on it." He tapped the rail. "And I will tell you what it is for, Mr. Vale, since you have come out with a schedule in your head and I can see it on you. It is for a person who is doing exactly what you are about to do, which is stand on a thing that looks like ground because the alternative is to turn round."

They argued for about nine minutes, which Nessa Pell timed, because she times everything, and then Adrian gave it up, which is not a thing he has done often and which he did not enjoy.

Selik chalked a second mark on the ballast six feet above the first, on the crown, at the place where the ground was worst, and said, "That is as far as the rule lets anybody go, and I have put it there myself and I will take it away myself and it will not be in the book. You may look from there. You may not put a foot past the twine. And if the twine goes, you come back, and I will not argue with you about it in the moment, because in the moment there will not be time."

---

What Adrian Vale actually wanted to know was not on the ground. It was in four documents, and he had the four documents on a slate, and it turned out that the walk was the least useful part of the morning, exactly as it had been on the afternoon of the nineteenth, and exactly as Selik Marne had said it would be when he put the flag on the spur with a piece of broken glass in his hand.

*Why is a mark doing two jobs?*

The mark was a small cross, struck with a cold chisel into the head of an iron pin, a thing you could do in forty seconds with a cold hand and a bad night's sleep. It was not a name. It was not a house. It was what a person gets instead of a name.

The first place it appeared was the fourth entry of the proposal of the twenty-ninth, which Nessa Pell had read out on the crate in the gate yard of this lot on the twenty-ninth, at about eleven in the morning, before two hundred people, and which said, under BEARER, FOUR: *entered by mark and not by name, the household having refused names and the steward named in the public book as the holder of the count.*

The second place was a strip, and the strip was in a book in a kitchen under a street, and the strip was dated the twenty-ninth, and the strip was Fen Ambrose's, from the far receiving ramp, and the mark on the strip was the same cross.

The third place was the largest. *The published terms of the sixth, sheet four, item three.* And there it was not merely a mark. It was a mark with a job attached to it. Item three said: *THE FOURTH ENTRY, being the far receiving ramp holder, on the far side, at the ramp, for the whole of the time the vehicle is on the far side, and the person who decides that a person arriving is somebody's and not a stranger's. AND THE MARKED LINE AT THE TOP OF THE SERVICE CUT IS HELD BY THE SAME PARTY FOR THE WHOLE OF THE SAME PERIOD, ON THIS BANK, AND NOBODY GOES PAST IT.*

Nobody had written that. Adrian had written it, on the second, at about two in the morning, in the back office, in the middle of a night in which he had also written a clause about the lamp oil.

The fifth place, which Nessa Pell found at about eleven o'clock, standing on the crown of the service cut in the rain with a lamp in her teeth, was a piece of paper that had been nailed to a gate on the fourth street, and which had been there since the second, and which said, in a hand that was not Adrian's and not Nessa's and was small and careful and pressed very hard:

**HELD. ASK AT THE YARDS.**

Three words and an instruction, and the yard that it referred to was not a market yard. It was a row of six low houses, and a washhouse, and a stone trough, and about a dozen chimneys, standing in a piece of ground that the ward market had never had a rate sheet for, four streets west of a depot.

Those eleven chimneys were the service-cut yards. The Warden's men had put a lantern up over a bus in the flood year. Rell Bode had stood in a yard on the third of this month and named the two blocks the ward market had never been able to count, and the woman at the far receiving ramp had said *nobody was ever going to say my name* and had been right, and Mara Quill had said it out loud in a kitchen on the fourth, and here it was, four streets west, in a dozen chimneys, with a piece of paper on a gate saying HELD.

Somebody had gone round on the second, at about the same hour Adrian had been writing the clause, and had nailed up a paper that said *held, ask at the yards*, and had then gone to six houses and had said, in words that Adrian was now quite sure had been careful and had meant well and had not once been the sentence *may I put your name in a book* — something like *there's a mark in a book and it'd do the yards good to be in it* — and had got six yeses, or five, or four.

And the mark in a book was a household's mark. And the household was a household in the service-cut yards. And the mark had been used, in eleven days, for the far receiving ramp, for the top of the service cut, and possibly for a third thing.

---

Selik Marne had gone back down at ten, and had said one thing at the gate, and had said it to nobody in particular.

"You have found a piece of paper with three words on it," he said. "I have found eleven of those in nine years and every one of them was a man doing his best in a room where nobody could give him a form. Nobody in this district has ever built a form for *held*. I am not going to help you with your paper, Mr. Vale. I am going to tell you one thing about the rule on this spur, and then I am going to go and open the sluice at the low end at eleven, which I do every day of my life and which nobody thanks me for.

"The rule is that nobody goes past the second rail. It is the only rule in this district that has never cost anybody anything. It is not clever, and it is not a manifest, and it does not know who anybody is, and it has been right every single time for nine years and it is going to be right tomorrow, and in nine years it has never once had to be argued with anybody who was on the ballast at night, because nobody has ever been on the ballast at night, because the twine is there and the rail is there and Culler Bray is a bicycle policeman who is twenty-five and sleeps.

"So you go up there and find out whose foot is going to be on the wrong side of a rule in order to write a document, and when you know, Mr. Vale, you had better not come down here and ask me to move the twine. I have moved it once in nine years, on the night of a flood, and I did not move it back for eleven months, and there is a reason and the reason is a name I am not going to say to you and have not said to anybody since the sixth year."

---

They found him at about twenty past ten in the morning, and the finding was nothing like a discovery and everything like an audit, and it took forty minutes, and Adrian Vale spent the whole of it reading aloud out of his own hand to five people on a wet slope.

The man who had been holding the line was a man of about fifty-five, of the service-cut yards, and he was the fourth child of a household of six that had been struck off the rolls in the ninth year for a thing nobody in the record is entitled to know, and which had lived in two blocks the ward market has never had a rate sheet for, and which had, in the flood year, put a lantern up over a bus.

And he was of the household the mark belonged to. So was the man at the chain on the goods-shed floor on the far bank, who had been asked one question by a steward on the Saturday and had said he did not know what the job was, and who is the fourth entry in the proposal of the twenty-ninth, and the two of them did not know it and had never met, and one mark had been given two jobs six days apart by two different people in two different rooms, and that is the whole of what went wrong and it took an hour and ten minutes to read out.

He had not been named. He had not been asked. He had been sent, and he had come, and he had stood in Adrian's yard for twenty minutes on the sixth of this month with a hood up, and he had not said one word, because in twenty minutes in a yard of five hundred people, if a man has not been asked a question, there is nothing for him to say, and the reason nobody asked him a question is that everybody in that yard believed the terms were complete.

He had said one thing. He had said it to Orren Pike, at the rail, at about six o'clock, in a voice that carried about nine feet, and Orren Pike had written it down, and Orren had put it in the book on the seventh, which was either the single most useful thing he has ever done or the single most damaging, and the argument about which is going to run for two years.

**"I was sent to hold a line."**

---

Nessa Pell did not ask Adrian what he intended to do. She asked him what the document said, and she made him say it out loud, standing on the crown of a service cut in the rain with a slate, which is the first time in seventeen years that anybody in that city has needed Adrian Vale for something other than a reading.

"Read item three," she said. "All of it. And then read the clause about the line."

Adrian read it.

"**AND THE MARKED LINE AT THE TOP OF THE SERVICE CUT IS HELD BY THE SAME PARTY FOR THE WHOLE OF THE SAME PERIOD, ON THIS BANK, AND NOBODY GOES PAST IT.**"

"Now say the last four words again, slowly, and look at the ground while you do it."

Adrian looked at the ground. The twine was six feet up the slope from the rail. Beyond the twine, the ballast. Beyond the ballast, the crown, and the crown dropped away to the west into the cut, and the cut went down nine feet to water and a long way along under a road that used to be a maintenance approach and was now a line in a book that a district had stopped being able to maintain.

"**Nobody goes past it**," he said.

"Yes," Nessa Pell said. "And the *it* is eleven feet above the rail. That is the Warden administrator's rule, Mr. Vale, and I am the clerk who holds the book that says so, and the rule is not yours and it is not mine, and the thing you have written into a public book on the sixth of this month **puts the fourth bearer of a charter on the wrong side of it.**"

Adrian stood in the rain and did not say anything for about eleven seconds, and Nessa Pell timed them, because she times everything, and entered them afterward as eleven seconds, and never told him.

Then the Warden of twenty-five said, "And I was going to tell him, and I was not going to, and I have been up here for two hours and I did not tell him." He took his bicycle by the bars. "Because the rule is the rule, and if I say *come off the ballast* to a man standing on the ballast, then the rule has been argued with by me, and then in nine years somebody will say the Warden moves the twine for a friend of the auctioneer. And because I am twenty-five and I did not want to be the man who told the man who wrote it."

"You should have told him," Selik Marne said, from the gate, where he had been standing for about ten minutes and where none of them had noticed, because he is very good at that and it is nine years of practice.

"Your honour—"

"Sergeant. I am not your honour and if you call me that in front of Mr. Vale I shall have to resign in public." He came up the slope. "You should have told him, and you did not, and the reason you did not is the reason the rule has been worth keeping for nine years, which is that it does not have to be explained to anybody, and the day it has to be explained is the day a man stands on the ballast and argues, and I have been waiting nine years for that day and I did not expect it to be Mr. Vale's own handwriting."

He looked at Adrian.

"Mr. Vale. I am going to say this once and I am going to say it in front of six people and I would like it written down. I have read your book. I have read the whole of the nineteenth and the list of the nine things it did not contain, and I have read that a man published his own security, and I have shaken your hand in a yard and said that your disaster and my closure are the same event. I mean all of it and I have never once said that you were careless, because you were not careless on the nineteenth, you were the opposite of careless, you were so careful that you wrote a promise instead of a term.

"And I have been waiting three weeks for you to put a man on my ballast, and I did not think it would happen, and it has happened, and I am not going to enjoy it and I am not going to pretend to."

---

The foot went at about four minutes past eleven, and it went the way a plank goes.

There was no noise. That is the thing about ground that has been arguing with itself for seventeen years: when it gives, it does not shout. There was a sound like a man clearing his throat, four streets away, and then a foot of the crown of the service cut went down, and it went down as a single piece about eleven yards long and nine feet wide, and the ballast rode it, and the ballast is loose, and the loose ballast rolled into the gap like water finding a hole, and it was over in about four seconds.

He was standing on the crown, nine feet from the drop, with a lamp in one hand and his back to it, because he had been walking up the slope with his face to the light, and Adrian had said *mind the crown, it is soft under there*, and he had said *I know where I am going*, and he had been on the ballast for about a minute and a half.

The lamp went in first and did not break. He went in after it on his side, and the ballast came down on the leg, and it was not a fall from a great height. It was a foot. It was a man on his side in a foot of gravel with a lamp burning next to his head.

What saved him, and Selik Marne said so out loud in the yard that afternoon, and again in the entry, and it is the sentence this district is going to be arguing from for a year:

**The lamp was still burning, so somebody could see where he had gone.**

What stopped him is the other half of the same sentence. **He had a lamp because a man on this spur had given him one, and the man who gave him the lamp was the Warden of twenty-five, and the reason the Warden of twenty-five was at the top of that cut at eleven o'clock in the morning instead of asleep on a cot is that he had a bicycle and it needed doing and nobody had told him not to.** And the reason nobody had told him not to is that Selik Marne had said *you may look from there, and you may not put a foot past the twine*, and everyone in that party had agreed to it, including the man who wrote the clause that made the place necessary.

Selik got him out in about eleven minutes, with a rope and a bicycle chain and two men and a signal hand, and the leg was not broken and it was not whole, and the ankle was one of the two that everybody in Alder Reach who has ever worked a barge has, which is to say it turns, and it will turn again, and it will turn on wet ground for the rest of his life.

The lamp was still burning when they got him out and it had to be put out, and the pin the twine was tied to on the crown side went into the ground with the crown, and the twine itself held, and Selik Marne walked back down to the rail and looked at his own rule for a while and then went and opened the sluice at the low end, which he does every day of his life at eleven, and which was half an hour late that day.

---

He was not named in the public book. That is the first fact about him and it is the reason the chapter ends the way it does.

He is a member of a household that refused names in the ninth year. There is no form in any book in this city by which such a person can be entered, and there is not supposed to be, and the whole of Lina Vale's eleven years has been spent on that, and the whole of the no-name remedy has been spent on it, and the remedy of the fourth of this month was written *on the explicit grounds that it would fail* and the grounds it gives are *the protected category*.

So the public book of Lot 17 carries, of the accident at the top of the service cut on the seventh of this month: a place, a time, an injury, a cause, a man. It does not carry the man.

It carries:

**A PROTECTED HOUSEHOLD, OF THE SERVICE-CUT YARDS, OF WHICH THE MEMBER INJURED IS ONE, AND OF WHICH ANOTHER MEMBER IS THE FOURTH ENTRY OF THE PROPOSAL OF THE TWENTY-NINTH, AND WHICH WAS COUNTED ON THE SECOND OF THIS MONTH BY A STEWARD, UNDER A MARK, WITHOUT BEING ASKED, AND WHICH IS IN NO COUNT OF THE BENCHES.**

And the same entry carries, in Adrian Vale's own hand, because he wrote it himself and would not let anybody else have it:

**The schedule is mine. The clause that put a man on the ballast is mine. It is not a mistake in the terms and it is not sabotage and there is nobody to blame but me, and I am not going to be permitted to have it entered as a piece of bad luck.**

And then, because the yard would have got there in about nine minutes if he had not said it first, and because a man who waits to be blamed is a man who is still managing the room:

**I would like the yard to know what the fourth term of the nineteenth cost the search party and what it cost me. It cost the search party a place I could not go and it cost me the only sentence anybody in this district has ever written that was read out by two hundred people and made the world smaller. It is in the book. I would like people to read it, because they are going to need to. Nobody past the second rail. That rule has never cost anybody anything until this morning, and it cost somebody something this morning, and the somebody is not on this rail because the somebody is protected, and I would like the yard to hold both of those.**

---

The board was seated at about half past four in the yard, all four seats named aloud, and it was the review the board had called for itself on the sixth, and it sat, and Adrian Vale was the answerable man, and the room used him as such, correctly, for the first time in seventeen years.

"Mr. Vale," Nessa Pell said. "You are the author of item three. I am not going to ask you how you feel. I am going to give you the fourth sheet and the strip-book and the gate paper, and I am going to ask you to read out every place a mark has been used in any document in this district since the fourteenth, in order, with the date, and I am going to time you, and it will take as long as it takes, and if your hand goes I am going to stop you and you are going to sit down."

"How long does it take," Adrian said.

"About an hour, and I am not going to do better than that at this hour," said Nessa Pell. "I do it in six, because I keep the index, and I have kept an index since the fourteenth because Registrar Quill asked me for one, and it is the only piece of administration anybody in this district has ever asked for, and every one of us now depends on it."

It took him an hour and ten minutes. Twenty-three places. Four of them were a household and not a person. Two of them were the same household doing two different jobs in two different documents four days apart, and one of them had a line on it, and *nobody goes past it*.

And at number twenty-three, at about twenty to six in the evening, with his hand going and the sound going flat at the edge, Adrian Vale read out a strip reference that was not a strip he had ever seen, in a document he had never seen, in a book kept by a woman in a kitchen under a street, and said:

"That is a match. That is the same cross. That is the same hand with a chisel in it. And it is dated the second of this month, and it is a group of four out of the service-cut yards, and it has a job on it, and the job says *held*."

Nobody in that yard had put a strip in a book. The strip-chain's writer's half is kept under a barrel in a kitchen and does not go in Lot 17's book. That is the whole of Lina Vale's design, and it is the reason the strip chain is safe.

So a strip had been put in a public book, with a mark on it, and a job, and the job had come off a gate in the service-cut yards.

---

Nobody slept in Lot 17 that night, and about nine people in it did not go home.

The board sat again at nine in the morning on the eighth, and it sat for two days, and its business was not Adrian's document, and the only reason anybody in that room knew that was that Mara Quill said it at the first sitting.

"Before we take anything else, I am going to say the thing that is going to be said about this for the rest of the year and I would like it said by me first, in this room, before anybody can make it into a story about a reader.

"**A mark is a person's refusal of a name, and it has been given two different jobs, and the second job was handed to it by somebody who had never seen the first document, and three people in this district each believed they were helping.** Fen Ambrose did it and wrote the reason down and asked him one question. A clerk did it and put a paper on a gate. And Mr. Vale did it in a schedule at two in the morning and put a man on a ballast with a Warden's rule in his back, and the ballast went a foot and a man is lying in a room in the lower terrace with an ankle that will turn on wet ground for the rest of his life. **None of those three people is a villain, and that is in the first line of this entry and not the last.**

"Now. The remedy of the fourth of this month was entered on the ground that it would fail on the day it was called, and the reason it gives is the protected category, and I said in a kitchen on the fourth that the reason was going to be true and incomplete, and that in a year somebody would read that entry and think the district did not know.

"It is the eighth of the month. The hearing is called for the twelfth. There is a man in a room who cannot be named, and the remedy reaches people by counting them, and he is not in any count, and the pool will be published on the twelfth and it will be four short, and the difference will be entered as a finding about the counting, and the finding will be that a mark was used for a job and a job was written on a gate, and the hearing will not discharge, and it will be entered as not discharged, and it will be the second time."

She put the wax tablet down.

"Registrar Quill will hold the hearing on the twelfth at nine in the morning in this yard, the board seated, all four seats named aloud, and the remedy will be called, and it will not be discharged, and the reason will be entered in the book's own words, and I am entering now, on the eighth, in advance, in my own hand, so that nobody can stand up on the twelfth and say they did not know.

"**Remedy: the split-use remedy, Lot 17, entered the fourth. To be called: the twelfth, nine in the morning, this yard, before the board. Expectation of the registrar, entered in advance: called, and not discharged. Reason: the protected category; a mark used as a person; a household counted by its steward; a schedule that put a named party on the wrong side of a Warden's rule. Cost of calling: one hundred and ninety-four coppers, of which the reader is entered at nine for the day, with the lie in it, in the reader's line.**"

---

And at about ten o'clock on the evening of the eighth, in a kitchen under the tram line, three people looked at a strip that did not match.

Bett Ruhl had the kitchen's half. It is a book, and it is not the public book, and it has never been and cannot be.

Mara Quill had the registrar's copy, because the registrar holds a duplicate of every count that enters a public record, and the count of the service-cut yards entered Lot 17's book on the second of the month as *household, four, giving and receiving, west of the service cut, and no names*, which is four words short of the six people in it and does not say a word about which two are missing.

Lina Vale had the third thing, and the third thing was the strip itself, and it was one of her own, and it had a cross on it, and a date, and a count of four, and a job.

"They are the same cross," Lina said.

"They are the same cross," Bett Ruhl said. "That is what I have been saying for an hour. That is the entire finding. It is not complicated. Two strips, six days apart, two jobs, one household, and the writer's half of one of them is in my book and I did not write it and I have never seen the hand before this evening in my life, and the other one I wrote, and I wrote it in a hurry, and I did not ask."

"What was the count on yours," Mara said.

"Four," said Lina Vale. "And the registrar's copy of the public entry says four. And the group that came to the gate on the second to ask about the line was six, and there were six of them standing in the street, and one of them had a bad ankle before the seventh and it is not the same ankle and I have been careful."

They looked at the strip for a while, in a kitchen, under a street, with a stove going and a lamp on a barrel and a floor that had four coppers of flour on a wall somewhere above the door.

"So the count is wrong by two," Bett Ruhl said, "and the wrongness is in a book that nobody can check and in a book that everybody can, and they are two different books about the same six people, and the six people are the ones nobody counted on the second and the four are the ones that got counted, and I do not know which of the two is right, and I have got a bad hip and a cold and I am going to sit down."

"Sit down," Mara Quill said.

And Lina Vale, who had been standing at the wall with a strip in her hand and thirty-one keys in a coat and her own name in a public book for a month, said the thing that the rest of this city is going to be arguing about, in a kitchen, to a registrar and a cook, at about ten o'clock at night:

"**Write both numbers in the same book.** Not one of them. Both. And put the reason they differ underneath, and the reason is that one of them was written by a man who counted a household because asking six people how many of them there are takes an evening he did not have, and the other one was written by a woman who counted in a hurry and did not ask, and neither of them did a crime and one of them put a man in a hospital.

"And Registrar. When you read the whole of the seventh out in this room tomorrow morning with two people in it who are not clerks — which you have promised, and I have been holding you to it for a month, and you are going to do it or I will say so in a yard — read that part twice. And when you get to it, do not stop to explain it. Stop, and ask me what the two books are, and let me answer in my own words in front of Bett Ruhl, because that is the only part of your instrument that I have ever believed, and it is a quarter of an inch of it."
