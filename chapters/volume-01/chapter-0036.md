# Chapter 36: The Four Who Have Not Been Asked

Fen Ambrose had kept a roll for nine years and she had never once been thanked for it, and the reason nobody thanked her was that a roll is the only thing in the district that cannot be improved by anybody's opinion.

She had said in a yard on the seventeenth: *do not write the school shelter group and mean everyone under a roof.* She had said it about a charter. It had turned out, on the twenty-sixth, to be a thing about counts, and about the fact that a count in Alder Reach is a count of households, and that there is no category in any book in this city for a person who is not in a household.

She got up at four. The river stairs are a stair and a stair takes one person at a time in daylight and is a three-hour thing each way, and she had made the round trip twice in three weeks and both times for a worse reason than the one before.

She took a tally sheet, a pencil with a bit of soap in it so the rain would not take it, the school roll folded inside her coat against her body, and a strip-book of blank strips cut to the size of the ones in a kitchen under the Ward Nine tram line, which she had made herself on Sunday from four pieces of the same stock, with no writing on them, because the design requires that the person inside a group never touches the paper, and if you bring your own strip you have already touched it.

---

The far receiving ramp is a loading floor of a goods shed with a rail at the end of it and a board across, and it is where the charter of the nineteenth said the riggers and the shelter group would be put down, and the riggers walked a mile and a half up to their cut and the shelter four hundred yards to the school house, and the charter had said so in those words.

The four children of the second shelter group had come home on the eleventh. Their two stewards had come home on the twelfth. **All nineteen came home, and the middle plank went into the cut on the last crossing, and the far bank has had no way across it since that afternoon.**

So the ramp was empty of them. And then it was not.

"Three weeks," Fen said, out loud, to herself, at the top. "That shed was cleared out on the thirteenth and there has been somebody on this floor every night since, because the far side has had no crossing since the plank went, and the river stairs are three hours each way and take one person at a time, and a goods shed with a draught in it is where you end up."

There were a fire in a drum at the north end, and a bedroll, and a kettle on a ring, and eleven people and a dog, and about half of them were people she had counted at the gym on the twenty-sixth, and half of them she had not.

Nobody stopped her. That was the first thing she wrote down, later, and it is the sentence she would have given a hearing: **nobody on that ramp stopped the woman with the roll, and I would like the record to show that that was not hospitality, it was that nobody there thought a roll applied.**

---

The man on the rail at the end of the floor was the one she had come for, and it took her an hour to get to him, because he was not at the rail, he was *under* the rail, on the gravel behind it, mending a length of chain with a cold hand, and there was a mark in the strip-book that said he was one.

"How do I put your name against this," Fen said, without preamble, because preamble wasted about forty minutes in a yard and she had learned better in nine years.

The man looked at the strip, and at the roll, and then he stood up, which took him a while.

"You do not," he said.

"You are in a proposal in a public book in Alder Reach as a mark, on a schedule, on the twenty-ninth, and there is no name on you anywhere in it, and I have come three hours down a stair to ask you a question and the question is whether you were asked."

"No."

"Was anybody in a room asked on your behalf?"

"No."

"Was anybody told that there was a room?"

"No." He set the chain down. "I have been on this floor since the fourteenth, which is the last of it, when the last of the nineteen went over the footbridge and the plank went after them." There is a fire and a kettle and a bedroll and eleven people and a dog, and the fire is because the goods shed has a draught in it you could sail a boat through, and the kettle is because the shed has no water on it and you carry it up from the tail. That is what I do. And the dog is because a dog is not a person and a dog does not have to be in a roll."

Fen wrote on the tally sheet, and wrote it in full, and read it back to him, which is the whole of her method and the reason nine people had signed things in her presence that they had refused to sign for a registrar.

"Fen Ambrose, shelter steward, on the floor of the far receiving ramp, the second day of this month. I have a mark in my strip-book against a bearer entry in a public proposal at Alder Reach, and the mark is mine and the entry is not, and I did not put it there, and I do not know who did, and I am going to find out in front of you and not afterwards."

"You are not going to know," the man said. "Whoever it was did it the way you would do it. They counted a household and you were in it."

"Then I would like to ask you a different question, and it is the only one I have. If the road opens and there is a bus on this floor, and the far side is not the road the driver left on, and the people on this floor have to be told what to do, and the man who tells them has a sheet in his hand that says he is the far-side holder — do you want to be the far-side holder?"

He was quiet for a long time, and the fire went over, and the dog moved.

"I do not know what the far-side holder is," he said at last.

"That is the correct answer," Fen said, "and it is the answer of a man who has not been asked, and I have to write it down as exactly that, and then I have to go back up a stair for three hours and tell a room that the fourth name in a public schedule is a man who does not know what the job is."

"Then say I will not," he said.

"You may say it yourself, and you may say it to whoever put me on this floor, and you may say it to a yard, and nobody in this city will argue with you about it." She put the tally sheet flat on the crate. "What I am not going to do is write *he will not* on a piece of paper and carry it up the stair and tell a room that a bearer has refused, because you have not refused anything. **You have not been asked.** Those are two different things and I have spent nine years learning the difference and there are about four people in this district who can tell it."

And Fen Ambrose, who had counted seventeen children twice in front of witnesses, wrote on the tally sheet in her own hand, and read it back, and the words were these:

**THE FOURTH ENTRY IS A MAN WHO WAS NOT ASKED. HE HAS NOW BEEN ASKED ONE QUESTION, AT THE FAR RECEIVING RAMP, BY THE STEWARD WHO CARRIED THE MARK, AND HE HAS SAID HE DOES NOT KNOW WHAT THE JOB IS. THIS IS NOT A REFUSAL. NO REFUSAL HAS BEEN GIVEN. THE ENTRY STANDS UNTIL HE IS TOLD WHAT IT IS, IN WORDS, AND DECIDES.**

---

She got eleven out of them in an hour and a half, and eleven was wrong, and she knew it was wrong before she had finished, because the school roll had four of them on it and the roll is nine years old and Fen Ambrose had written it herself, and the other seven came out of three different books and none of the three books had ever met the other two.

Then the woman by the kettle asked her a question and Fen sat down on the bedroll, which she had not expected to do.

"You have got a sheet," the woman said. "You have been going round the room with it for an hour and a half and you have not asked me a single thing, and I have been waiting for you to, because I have been counted four times this month by four different people and three of them asked me and one of them wrote it down and I did not say anything, and that is the only difference there is, and I would like it written down that I said nothing."

"Your name is not on my roll," Fen said.

"No."

"Who is on your roll?"

"Nobody. I have not got a household. I had one and it is not one." She did not make it smaller than that. "There are eleven people on this floor and four of them are on the school roll, which is the gym's roll and carries the shelter group and about nine people who keep the gym, and two of them are on the clinic's list and one of them is in the exchange's count because a steward writes it down for us, and that leaves four, and the four are me, and two men who have been on this bank since the fourteenth and cannot tell you what they did before it, and a woman with a child, and that child is not in anything at all and has never been."

"How long have you been here."

"Since the fourteenth. The last of them went over the footbridge that morning and the plank went after them and this is the first week anybody has been stuck on this bank with no way across at all."

"Fourteen days." She put her hand flat on the kettle lid, which is a thing Fen has never done and could not have explained. "I have not been on a roll since I was nineteen. And I want to say the next part out loud because you have got a sheet and because you came three hours down a stair, and if I do not say it here I am going to be counted again next week by somebody who is kind about it.

"It is not that nobody leaves me out. It is that **every count in this district is a count of households, and I am not in a household, and I have not agreed to be in one, and so I am counted by nobody and I am also not missed.** Those are the same sentence. That is why the four is wrong and not two. You are not four missing. You are four *uncountable*, and I am one of them, and there are three others, and one of them has a child."

Fen Ambrose sat on a bedroll on a goods-shed floor on the far side of a river and discovered that the argument she had been making for nine years had a hole in it the shape of a person, and that the hole had been there the whole time, and that she had spent nine years saying *do not write the school shelter group and mean everyone under a roof* to a district in which **the school shelter group means households and everybody knew it and it was the only vocabulary there was.**

"Well," she said. "Then I have to write that down and I have to write it in a yard, and it is going to be the most unpopular four lines anybody has put in front of this district in a month, and I am going to do it in front of the man who wrote the schedule, because he is the man it belongs to."

---

She did it on the second of the month, in the yard, at Lot 17, in daylight, with the sheet on the crate, and about three hundred people at the rail, and Adrian Vale standing eleven feet from the crate with his hands in his pockets, which is where he put them when he had decided to be a thing in a room rather than a man in one.

The tenth hand of the signal crew had his copied page out before she started, because he had heard about a count coming down a stair and had done the only thing he knew how to do, which was to bring a page with every number on it.

**What the session of the twenty-sixth was.** School gym. Ninety-one people in the room. The clinic: seven named, and the boxes list of ninety-four lines. The shelter: seventeen. The signal crew: **one**, being himself, and the note in his own hand: *I am the tenth and not one of the nine and I have not been one of the nine at any point and nobody is to put me in it.* The exchange: household, four, giving and receiving. The ward market: eleven traders and two clerks. Visitors, counted at the door: forty-nine, of whom nine were affected persons with no claim on anything, including a child of eleven who had refused a crossing in the nineteenth and had asked for a place at the rate meeting and been given one.

**Sum of the named, at the twenty-sixth, excluding visitors: forty-two.**

**Bearers named in the proposal of the twenty-ninth: four.** *One of whom is entered over a refusal given in the same room; one of whom is a mark; one of whom is not a bearer and is entered as one; and a fifth that is a schedule.*

And then Fen read the four lines, and she read them in the plainest sentence anybody had put in front of that yard in a month, and she had written it on the stair coming up and had crossed out two versions.

"**The count of the twenty-sixth was wrong by four. The four were not in the room and they are not on this floor. They are four streets west of a depot on the other bank of a river, in a household of six that a steward counted as four, and nobody missed them, because the count of the twenty-sixth was a count of households and a person who is not in a household is not on any sheet there is to be missed from. The reason the count was wrong is that every count in this district is a count of households, and there is no category in any book in this city for a person who is not in a household, and therefore a person who has not chosen to be in a household is not missed by anybody, because there is no line on any sheet in this city for them to be missed from.**

"**And I have been saying for nine years: do not write the school shelter group and mean everyone under a roof. And I have been saying it to a district in which the shelter group means households, and everybody has known that, including me, and the reason I knew it is that I have been the one writing *household, four, giving and receiving* on a wall in a kitchen since the flood year, and I have never once put a mark on a sheet for a person who was not in one of my four.**

"So the correction is not four names. **The correction is a category, and it does not exist, and I am not the person to write it, and I have spent nine years being certain that I was.**"

The yard was extremely quiet, and then the tenth hand said, from the back, with his page in his hand:

"Three and a half weeks I have been keeping a page with every number on it. I started it on the nineteenth because eight of my nine went over there and I had nothing else to do but write, and I have had all nine back since the fourteenth, and having them back has not made one number in this district checkable. I have got a copy of everything anybody has ever said about how many, and I have checked every number in this district's paperwork against that page, and I have found two wrong ones in three weeks and neither of them was Adrian's." He held the page up. "This is the third. The two I found before were both in documents written by wardens. This one is in a document written by a man who let me stand in a yard and say so on the ninth, and I am not making a speech about it. I am a counter. I count."

"Thank you," Fen said, and meant it, and did not soften it, because softening it would have been the same as the thing she was complaining about.

Then she turned round to Adrian Vale, and this is the part of the afternoon that four people in that yard would remember for a long time and that none of them would enjoy remembering.

"Mr. Vale. I am going to do something to you now and I have thought about it for fourteen hours on a stair and I have decided to do it in daylight."

"Go on."

"You named four bearers and one of them is a man on a floor on the far bank who does not know what the job is, and you did not know that either, and I do not think you would have left it if you had." She held up the tally sheet. "That is one. Now the second thing, and it is worse, and it is not about him.

"Eleven people are on that floor. Seven of them were counted somewhere. Two of those seven were counted by the clinic's list, which is a list of hands and not a list of people, and Renn Fisk wrote it, and it is a good list and I have no complaint about it, and it is a list of people who carry. Four were counted by the school roll, which is the gym's roll, and carries the shelter group and about nine people who keep the gym, and is nine years old. And one — the man at the chain, the fourth entry, the mark — was counted by a steward, in a kitchen, on a wall, in a hand I have been reading since the flood year. And I counted him on that floor on the Saturday and I did not know I was counting a household until he told me.

"And you wrote a schedule that says *a mark and a strip reference and a count of one*."

"I did."

"You wrote a **count of one.**" Fen let that sit for a second. "There are eleven people on that floor. You have one. And the one you have is a man who was in a household and whose household's count was written by a steward, and you did not ask the steward, and the steward did not ask the household, and I have been the steward and I know exactly why, and it is not dishonesty. **It is that when a household refuses names, somebody has to hold the number, and the person who holds the number is answering for a number and not for a person, and it is the easiest work in the world and it is the reason this district's counts are the way they are.**

"And you did not ask, because the argument in your schedule is that the terms are complete. That is the argument, is it not. *Everything that can be asked has been asked and the schedule holds all of it.* And the reason I want it said in daylight in front of three hundred people, Mr. Vale, is that the ninth thing that was not in the charter of the nineteenth was a statement of what happens if the marking at the return is not where the charter says it is, and the ninth thing that was not in yours is a person on a floor, and in both cases nobody was harmed by a lie. In both cases the paperwork was **true**, and complete, and unread."

Adrian did not say anything.

"I am not going to say you are a bad man," Fen said. "I want that on the record as not said. You are the only person in this district who has ever read another person's sentence out loud to a room, and that is a thing I could not do, and I have wanted to do since I was thirty. And I have also just told you in front of three hundred people that a schedule you wrote on the twenty-ninth has a count of one where there ought to be eleven, and I found it with a bit of soap in a pencil and three hours of a stair, and I have never used it against anybody in my life, and I am using it against you, and I would like you to understand that the reason I can is that you are the man who read my sentence out loud in a gym and made it mean something."

---

What the record carries is the thing that matters, and it is a thing the board argued about for forty minutes and got right.

The board has four seats and no owner and it was seated within the hour with all four named aloud — Mara Quill as registrar, which is not a vote; Nessa Pell as the sitting steward; Sabin Tull and Marda Osk in the two unclaimed places, one of whom had been at the far ramp that morning and one of whom had not slept.

The board cannot strike a line off a notice board. It can only record what happened.

So the wrong count stays. Beside it, in Fen Ambrose's own hand, and not struck: **THE COUNT OF THE TWENTY-SIXTH WAS WRONG BY FOUR, AND THE COUNT OF THE SECOND WAS WRONG BY ONE, AND BOTH COUNTS ARE IN THIS BOOK, AND NEITHER HAS BEEN REMOVED, AND THE REASON IS WRITTEN UNDER BOTH.**

And under the correction, which is the first genuinely new institutional thing anybody in Alder Reach has produced in a month, and which is four lines long and took fourteen hours on a stair:

**A PERSON IS ACCOUNTED FOR BY A COUNT AND A DESTINATION AND A REMEDY, AND IS NOT ACCOUNTED FOR BY A HOUSEHOLD.**

**A GROUP MAY BE A HOUSEHOLD, OR MAY NOT BE. WRITING THE GROUP MEANS EVERY HOUSEHOLD IN IT, AND WRITING THE GROUP ALSO MEANS EVERY PERSON IN IT WHO IS NOT IN ONE OF THE HOUSEHOLDS, AND THE SECOND HALF OF THAT IS THE HALF THAT HAS NEVER BEEN WRITTEN IN THIS CITY.**

**THE STEWARD OF A GROUP COUNTS THE GROUP. THE STEWARD DOES NOT COUNT THE PEOPLE IN THE GROUP WHO ARE NOT IN A HOUSEHOLD, AND MAY NOT, UNLESS THEY ASK.**

**ANYBODY MAY BE ASKED, AND MAY SAY NO, AND SAYING NO IS NOT A REFUSAL OF SERVICE, AND THE COUNT IS PUBLISHED WITH THE DECLINE IN IT AND NOT WITHOUT IT.**

And then, at the bottom, in Nessa Pell's hand, because the book requires that the reason a number is what it is stands in the same entry as the number:

**THE DECLINE OF THE WOMAN AT THE FAR RECEIVING RAMP IS ENTERED. IT IS ENTERED IN HER OWN WORDS. SHE SAID NOTHING ON ONE OCCASION WHEN SHE WAS WRITTEN DOWN WITHOUT BEING ASKED, AND SHE HAS ASKED THAT THIS BE THE ONLY THING IN THIS BOOK THAT IS ENTERED BECAUSE SHE SAID NOTHING.**
