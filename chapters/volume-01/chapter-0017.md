# Chapter 17: The Larger Shipment

By dawn the St. Orra staging yard had stopped being a rail yard and become a queue with opinions.

Carts came in on the tram apron through the whole grey morning and stopped wherever there was room, which was nowhere, so the yard had arranged itself by who had arrived first and by how badly each group needed to be at the front. Adrian came in at six with two hours of sleep in him and a slate under his arm, and by seven he had stopped pretending he was going to sort it.

The clinic held the far end. Fourteen cold boxes on four hand carts under a new tarpaulin, chained the way Tarin chained things, with a matron standing at the head of them counting lids and a porter at the tail with a cold-chain sheet he kept touching. Renn Fisk, back on the near bank since last night, with a face that had gone soft at the edges in a way Adrian had seen before on men who had been pulled out of something.

The Needle Tramway signal gang took the wall. Six of them, all men over fifty, all carrying their own tools, who had come from a job on the far bank and had not been able to get back across to do the second half of it. They wanted no consideration at all. They wanted the road.

Two ward-market riggers were with them, and a boy of nineteen who was the ninth, and Adrian counted three times because the boy counted himself twice.

And along the tram arch, in a line that had formed before the clinic, were the eleven.

Not the eight. He looked at the line for a long time, looking for a face he knew, and did not find one. Eleven children, and three women in grey canvas coats, and a woman in a grey coat who was not one of the three and who was not Alma Dren either, and who stood at the front of the line with a list in her hand and the expression of a person who has done everything correctly and been told that is not sufficient.

"Eleven," she said, before he had asked. "Not the eight. I want that said first, because people are going to hear *the children who came back* and I have had that said about my group twice already this week and it is not true. My eleven have never been on a bus. They saw the eight come home and they have been asking me for eleven days whether the road was real."

"Who are you?"

"Fen Ambrose. I am the school shelter's second steward. Alma is the first and Alma is at the clinic because half her children have fevers again, and I have a list and I have nine parents who want their names off it and two who want them on it, and I have been standing in this yard since half past five working out which of those two things I am going to be."

---

Selik Marne came at eight with two Wardens and a load sheet, and he came on foot, which he had never once done.

The housing was certified. He said that first, which Adrian had not expected and did not know was true. The gauge housing had been rebuilt, sealed, and signed by two people who were not his. The slots were still halved, and would stay halved for another four days by his own order, and the line was open to named passenger crossings and to supervised cargo exceptions, and he had the sheet in his hand to prove all of it.

"Four boxes a day," he said, laying it on the crate. "That is a supervised cargo exception and it is what the exception was written for. Four boxes, one vehicle, one marshal, and the manifest is the load sheet. On the fourth day the coolant is a rumour and your ward has what it has."

"Four days," Adrian said.

"Four days. And I will take the eight repair hands and the boy across as a named work crew this afternoon, if they will give me eight names, because I have no way to carry a crew of nine that is not on a roll, and the roll is the whole of my instrument and I am not going to pretend otherwise to a man in my own yard." He looked at the eleven. "And I cannot take the eleven. Not today, not in a week, not in a month if they refuse the roll. That is the second time I have said that sentence and I am not going to soften it for a better crowd."

"Can the eleven go on a public road you inspect?" Adrian asked.

"You are asking me to supervise a bus I do not hold the token for, on a span I do not staff, using marshals I did not train, and to put my name beside it." Selik said it without heat. "No. I will do something harder. I will put a Warden and a white flag at your marked spur for every minute the route is open, and I will stop your bus if it leaves the line, and if the route bends past the wall I will close my bridge until the thing is explained. That is the limit of what I will put my name to and I have put it in front of your board in writing."

"Read it in," Mara said from the crate, and Nessa Pell read it in, and the yard heard the boundary of it in the yard's own language, which is that the Warden would be standing on the platform and not inside the bus.

---

Halloway Reed came at ten with a wagon and a broad sheet of oiled paper and the particular good humor of a man who has been waiting for a shortage.

He did not come alone. He brought his own carriers, six of them, paid and fed and standing in a half circle in his yard with their coats on, which was a thing Adrian could not dismiss and had tried for two months to stop noticing.

"Fourteen boxes, nine hands, eleven children, three stewards, and one driver you have already lost the reason for twice," Halloway said, unrolling the sheet. "On my claim. Tonight. I have a labor term and I will read it out loud myself, in full, in front of your board and your crowd, and I will put the return point on the sheet in front of everybody, and it will be the old Ward Nine platform on the near apron with a rail joint under the lamp for the driver to trust, and my term for a fault is a repaired carriage and a road, which is what roads cost, and I will pay my own crew in coin the same night so that nobody is doing charity."

"And the memory toll," Mara said.

"Stated on the sheet. Every traveler loses the reason. Twice if they come back. I will read that too."

"And the labor term."

"Fifteen days, not seven, and it falls on me and not on the carrier, and I will put my name on it as the party the labor falls on, and if I do not perform, the public book can have my warehouse." He looked around the yard. "That is the offer. It is faster than yours, it is more complete than yours, and the reason it is more complete is that I am prepared to be the person who answers, because the person who answers has to have a warehouse."

"And the seven," Adrian said. "Where did the seven come from?"

Halloway did not pretend not to understand. "From a room I rent on the tram side to three men who set their own prices and use my wicks and my carts, and who stopped answering for their own forms about a year ago, and the plate got into four sets of hands, and one of those hands belongs to a man on a tea table who cannot write." He said it without embarrassment, which was the unnerving part. "I have not been able to shut it down and I have not been able to say it is not mine, because I sold the first one. Put that in your book in whatever words you like. I would rather it were in the book than that you found it in a month and told the yard I had been clever about it."

"Recorded," Mara said, "and the registrar is not going to soften it or sharpen it."

"Neither would I." Halloway turned back to Adrian. "My offer is on the table. It is not a secret and it is not clever and it does not require anybody to be caught out at four in the morning to work. That is the whole of my case and you may now go and lose to it properly."

Nobody in the yard said anything, because the offer was not dishonest, and the difficulty with an honest offer from a man you did not trust was that the yard did not need to trust him to want it.

"Thank you," Adrian said, and meant it more than he had meant anything in a week. "If there is no public session, I will not stop you. I will want it read in the book, by name, with the time, so that the public record says who carried them and on what paper."

"Read it," Halloway said. "I would not sell against a record. I would sell *with* one, if you would let me, and that is the entire difference between me and the thing you are frightened of."

---

Then Adrian went and read the token, and the reading went badly.

It was the third time in two days. The case was opened under the same three conditions each time, and Mara read the time aloud, and he touched the brass and let the writing come, and the first reading was the same four lines it had always been.

The second reading was thinner. Use, toll, condition, and then a gap where the standing had been, as though the object had decided that he already knew it.

The third reading he should not have attempted, and he attempted it because Halva Sen was standing at the crate with her hand flat on a cold box and the lid was warm at the corner.

It came in pieces, with two words missing from the toll line, and a standing line that named the driver and then stopped halfway through naming the passenger, and it did not finish, and the brass got hot enough to be unpleasant against two fingers.

Then the depot lamp went flat and came back, and Adrian could not remember the last eleven minutes. He knew from the position of the sheet on the crate that he had been writing, and Nessa Pell was standing in front of him with her hands on his shoulders, and Halva Sen had not moved, and there were four cold boxes in a different order than there had been.

He had lost an afternoon to a decision he had made because a lid was warm.

He put that in the private ledger, and then he wrote it in the public one, because the second part was the rule he had signed.

---

Adrian had spent three days doing arithmetic that nobody wanted, and the reason he was doing it was that he had a number in his head and the number was this: sixteen hours of daylight, and the yard's people going without the medicine, and a fever ward on the far bank with eleven cots and four hours of queue, and every hour he spent in a session was an hour of something.

The trouble was that the same hour, counted the other way, was an hour of eleven children standing in the rain, and nine signal hands who could not get back to their own job, and a matron whose lids were warm.

There was a number for waiting and there was a number for rushing and they were both real and neither of them was knowable in advance, and he had spent seventeen years believing that a named cost felt like control, and here was the shape of the thing he had never had to face: he could not find a number that was the number.

He went and found Mara.

"The honest answer is that we cannot carry it in one crossing," he said. "Fourteen boxes is fourteen boxes. Fourteen cold boxes and nine hands off the signal line and the riggers, and eleven children, and three stewards, and a driver, is a bus full to the roof and a load that route has never held. And on top of that, the token is cracked through the plate and I cannot tell you whether it opens at all."

"Then say that in the session and let the market decide," Mara said.

"The market will decide the medicine. The market always decides the medicine, because the medicine has a number attached to it and the children have a word, and the word loses." He put the slate down on the crate. "That is not a complaint. That is a prediction, and I have watched it happen twice, and I am telling you that if we open a public session with fourteen boxes in it, I already know how the afternoon goes, and I would like it to go otherwise, and I have no mechanism."

"You have a mechanism. It is ugly and it is slow and it is the only thing you have." Mara did not soften it. "You go into that room and you want to be the one who ranks. You gave that away in front of nine people, and it was the best thing you have done since the token appeared. Do not tell me you cannot rank, because you can. I have watched you do it to a loading ramp. You are better at it than the four seats on that board and you know it, which is why you gave it away."

"And you would like it back."

"No." She took the slate and turned it round so the arithmetic faced her. "I would like the board to be good enough that the ranking it produces is better than yours, and I have not managed that in the three days I have had this board, and I have been the registrar for eight years, so you may draw whatever comfort you want from that sentence."

---

He drafted the terms in the back office at midnight, and the drafting took until three, and at three he did the thing he had been avoiding since dawn.

He made a list.

The list was headed **WAYS THESE TERMS CAN BE MISUSED**, and he wrote it for the session, and it was the most useful thing he did that week, and he put it in front of six people in a room over a repair shop and asked them to be unkind to it.

Tarin came first, and gave him two, and both were about the road rather than the paper: *a returning group is a different thing from an outbound group, and if you write one set of terms you have written the wrong terms for one of the two legs*; and *if the return point is the joint under the lamp, then anybody who can move a lamp can move the return, and the charter has to say what a driver does when the return is not there.*

Mara gave him one and it was a knife. *A charter can be complete and still be a lie, because the parts nobody in the yard is standing in to check are the parts that get left out. Write the list of what is not in it and read that too.*

Orren gave him one and it was the worst of the set, and he gave it late, from the doorway, with his satchel still on. *The return. Say what it costs and who pays it separately, or you have not written a term. You have written an outward term and an opinion about the way back.* And then he had said, before Adrian could answer, "You already know that. I am writing it down so that it is written down and not because I think you will do it."

Lina gave him one from outside the process entirely, which is to say she gave him the one nobody inside the process would think of. *Your beneficiaries are groups and your remedy is a person. Somewhere in these terms a person who refused a name is going to be inside your charter and outside your remedy, and on the day it happens it will be because the terms were written for people who have a file. Write the line for the ones who do not, and put it in the charter, where they can hear it.*

Selik gave him one and refused to soften it, and it was about Adrian. *You are the man who reads it, and the man who reads it sets the price, and a public market that turns on one reader is not public, it is published. Find the second person who can check you, and if you cannot find one, write down that there is only one, and let the room decide what to do about that.*

And Fen Ambrose, the second school steward, with eleven children in a list and nine parents who wanted their names off it, gave him the one that made him sit down.

"You have written *the school shelter group* in your beneficiaries," she said. "That is a name I can be responsible for. If you write *the school shelter group* and you mean *all children under a roof the school holds*, then the count is a lie the day somebody brings a child from a house the school does not hold, and the road will carry her, and I will have answered for a number that was never mine." She tapped the list. "Write the count. Write who counted. And put a line in that a child may be added to that count by the steward, in the yard, out loud, up until the case is opened, and the added child is told the toll herself. Otherwise the shelter's own discretion becomes the manifest, and I would rather it did not."

Adrian wrote all six down. He wrote them in the order they had been given, and he did not correct them, and at the bottom he wrote **these six are the objections and they are in the record, and if a term I have written answers none of them, say so in the session.**

---

Which was why, at six the next morning, in a yard with fourteen cold boxes and nine riggers and eleven children in it, Adrian Vale did the thing he had spent eleven days learning not to do.

He ranked them.

He did it out loud, in the yard, at the rail, with the board behind him and the slate on the crate, and he did it so fast and so well that the yard came with him: the fourteen boxes first, because a lid was warm and eleven people are on cots; the nine riggers with them, because they have a job on the far bank that costs the city a rate every day it waits; the eleven children third, because they are the most legible need in the yard and legible needs are the ones that get talked over; and the Ward Nine cooperative and the Unlisted households in a shared fourth, because neither has a number anybody in the yard can read.

It was a good ranking. He knew it was a good ranking, and he watched it work, and about nine seconds later he understood that nobody had stopped him, and that the reason nobody had stopped him was that it was four in the morning in a yard where a matron was touching a box lid, and the room needed a man to say what the order was, and it had him, and he had said it, and the saying had been easy.

Mara had not stopped him. That was the part he could not put down afterward. She had stood at the crate with the book open and had let him rank three hundred people in front of the yard, because the alternative was that the yard guessed.

"It is the right order," she said, when he came over.

"It is the right order and I am the only one who did it."

"Yes."

"So say the second half."

"The second half is that you stood on that rail and told nine people you wanted to be the one who decides who gets the road, and that wanting is why there is a board." She put her hand flat on the book. "And I am not going to use it against you this morning, because you are right, and because the yard is right, and because somebody has to say the order out loud at four in the morning. But write this in the book in my hand, and read it in the session: *the order was proposed by the reader, accepted by nobody, and carried out because the room had no other.* And if you do that, then the next time the order is wrong, the room will know that it was the reader's order and not the market's, and it will be able to refuse it."

Adrian took the slate and read what she had written, and then he made a version of it with his own name in the right place, and signed underneath.

He was still standing there, with the echo sitting in his jaw, when the last light came off the river wall and the yard's attention moved, and Halloway Reed stepped out of the shadow of the wagon with a folded sheet under his arm and the look of a man who has been waiting four hours for a specific man to be alone.

"Vale."

"Halloway."

"I am going to say one thing to you and I am not going to say it in front of your board." He held up the sheet and did not unroll it. "I have watched you do that. In the yard, just now. You ranked four hundred people in ninety seconds and every one of them came with you, and half of them had never seen a person do it before and they thought it was a kind of goodness."

"It was a good order."

"It was a *fast* order. There is no difference between those things in a yard at four in the morning, and that is the whole of my trade and the whole of your danger." He put the sheet down on the wagon bed. "Here is what happens next. You go into that session today with fourteen boxes and eleven children and nine signal hands and a whole city watching, and you will write beautiful terms, because you can. And your beautiful terms will be a great deal better than mine, and that is the danger, because mine is a piece of paper with a wax stick on it and yours will be a *market*. And a market does not run on fairness. A market runs on who can afford to be certain. So the ward market will take its three days back to sixty people who will decide they cannot afford to be certain, and the Unlisted will decide they cannot afford to be certain, and the eleven will be carried because they are children and the clinic will be carried because it has a number, and the fourth thing on your list, the thing with no number anybody can read, will get what the fourth thing always gets, which is a good word in a book from a person with a good hand."

"Who buys it," Adrian said.

"Everybody with a reason they cannot check. That is what certainty is, when you strip it: a reason you do not have to look at." Halloway picked his sheet back up. "You are going to sell the public board to the people who can afford certainty, Vale, and you are going to do it in one afternoon, with a fair process, in full daylight, and nobody will be able to point at a single dishonest act. I have been doing this for six years. I know what the shape of it looks like from the inside, because I have been the man who says *come and get certainty cheaply* and I have never once had to lie to do it."

Adrian had no answer. He had a counter-argument somewhere behind his teeth and it was about disclosure, and disclosure was the answer to a man selling certainty at a price, and the counter-argument was worth about eleven words and would not come out.

"Sell your market," Halloway said, and got on the wagon. "But sell it knowing that the four things on your list are not going to be in the room when the price is called, and decide now, in daylight, which of them you are going to go and get yourself."
