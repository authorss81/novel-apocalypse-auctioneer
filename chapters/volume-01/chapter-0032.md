# Chapter 32: A Table Is Not a Church

The trouble with a table, Tove Alard had worked out somewhere in the third week, is that a table is a place and a person is a place, and a person who cannot get to the place cannot be served by the place no matter how honest the person at it is.

She had said something like it out loud on the ninth of the month and Orren Pike had said no, which was a thing he did well and which she had learned to wait for, and the waiting was the part of it she was actually good at.

The service had run for three weeks. The fourth page was on the wall of the tram arch in four taped pieces, and there was a table under it with a lamp on it at four in the morning, and Marda Osk and a cooper's apprentice and Tove herself, and the number had stopped being the interesting part. A hundred and forty-one claims since the first of the month. Ninety of them matched. Fifty-one did not, and thirty-two were refused at the table and nineteen walked off before anybody got to the ninth line.

It had worked. In the sense that a thing which is supposed to stop somebody from drowning and does not drown anybody has worked, and Tove had been careful, on purpose, over nine nights, not to let that be the good outcome, because a table that reports a good outcome is a table that has learned to feel fine.

The problem arrived in the middle of the third week, in the shape of a woman who came to the table.

She was about fifty. She had a claim in her hand and a lamp beside her and she had clearly not intended to stop, because she was still walking, and Tove said, without looking up, "You can have it read here, it takes a minute, nobody buys anything."

She stopped.

She held the claim out and Tove took it, and it was a good claim, and that was the first thing that went wrong, because Tove had got fast at this: she saw the first line and knew it was Hask Vell's work, not the plate — Vell wrote claims himself at a tea table on the upper terraces, in a shaking hand, with a wax stick, and he did not fold, and he had never once been in a yard about it.

The first line said a route across the east reach of the cross cut, carrier and load, term of five days.

"That is Vell's," Tove said. "Third broker. Written out longhand, nine lines, no fold. Sit down if you want it read."

"I have read it," the woman said.

Tove put it down on the table and looked up, which she did not do, because the rule of the table is that you read the paper and not the face.

"I cannot get to this table," the woman said. "That is what I have come to say and I would like to say it quickly because I have to be at the market at seven. I cannot get to this table at four in the morning because I am on the terraces and I am fifty-one years old and I am on my feet from half past two and I have done it since the year before the flood, and I have walked to the river stairs twice a week for eleven years and I cannot walk up the tram apron at four in the morning, not once a month, not in this weather. That is all. That is the whole of what I came to say, and I would like to go."

Tove looked at the claim on the table.

"He will read it to me," the woman said, pointing up the terrace with her chin. "At the tea table. He reads it out, and he asks me if I understand the ninth line, and I said yes. I did understand the ninth line. The ninth line is that I am not required to come back and nobody is answerable for where I am afterwards. And I understood it, and I have three days' wages in a barrow-load of candle stubs going up to the clinic on Thursday, and if I do not go on Thursday I lose the Thursday."

"Then it is a good claim," Tove said, which was a thing she had learned to say before she said anything else, "and it is Vell's, and it is nine lines and there is no fold in it."

"Yes," the woman said.

"And you cannot come back down here to have it read properly."

"No."

"And you cannot ask him to read it twice."

"He read it twice," the woman said. "He is not a liar. That is the trouble. Everybody keeps telling me that man is a liar and he is not, he is a careful man and he reads it twice, and he read it to me and I understood it and I have understood it every time for four years, and I am not going to stand in the middle of a tram apron at four in the morning and explain to you that I do not have a way to get down here, because you have a table and I have a barrow."

She was already going.

Tove Alard, who was sixteen years old and had set the type of every page on that wall with her own hands, stood behind a table on a street at half past four in the morning and found that she had nothing to put on the wall that would have helped that woman, and that the page was on the wall four hundred yards behind her, in the rain.

---

They had it out for two hours after, in the exchange, and the exchange's floor and its stove and about nine hours a week of Bett Ruhl's life were what the conversation cost, which Bett said out loud at the end, as she always did, in a list.

What Bett Ruhl would not have is a discussion.

"Two," she said. "Two hours on my floor for two people. Say it."

"Say what," Orren said.

"Say what the discussion cost, in my own currency, in front of the people it was for, so that nobody can come out of this room tomorrow and say that the table is free and the room is free and the reading is free." She put the kettle on. "It is not free. It has never been free. The whole reason this service works is that it is made out of four people who are willing to be somewhere at four in the morning, and one of them is me, and I am sixty-one and I do not sleep."

Then she said the thing that settled it, and she said it to Tove, because Tove was the one who had said the true thing two hours earlier and had not known she had said it.

"You said a table is a place and a person is a place, and you are right, and you are sixteen, and the sentence you have just handed me is the reason this floor has a service on it and the reason the tram arch does not."

"How is that."

"Because a church is a place too." Bett filled the kettle. "I have watched nine people get through a bad winter because somebody read a thing out loud to them once and that was enough for them. And I have watched what happens when the reading is in the building and the reading was not for them, and the building did nothing. It is the same mistake in both directions and you cannot cure it with a bigger building. The only thing that has ever worked in this city is somebody going out on foot to the person, and the reason nobody does it is that it costs a night, and a night is a whole night."

There was nothing in that room to argue with, and Orren Pike, who had been arguing for four days, sat down.

---

What they built in the eleven days after that was worse than a table and they all knew it before they built it, and they built it anyway, because there was nothing else, and the reason it was worse is that everything about it was a person.

It could not be a notice. A notice is at an address.

It could not be the ward market. The ward market charged at a counter from half past six, and the woman on the terraces had said the thing herself: she was on her feet from half past two, and the market at seven was the only hour she had.

It could not be the service, because the service had no address, by Bett Ruhl's own rule, given on the night the service was built and agreed out loud in words both of them had said back to each other so that they were saying the same sentence.

So it had to go to people who already walked. And there were, in the end, eleven of them, and every one of them walked for a living, and not one of them was available at four in the morning, and all of them were free between six and seven in the morning while they waited for the market to open, which meant the courier went out at ten at night and came back at seven.

"Lina's," said Orren.

"No," said Bett Ruhl.

"It is the only network in this city that can get a paper to a place in a night—"

"It is the only network in this city that can get a paper to a place in a night *and be protected for doing it*," Bett Ruhl said, "and those are the same reason, and if I enrol those people in my problem then a woman on the upper terraces who has never heard of the exchange is one step closer to a room with a door on it, and I have spent nine years making sure that the exchange has a table and a barrel and a description, and a courier network is a *list*, and a list is a thing you can be given by somebody with a warrant in the morning."

"So the couriers don't carry the paper."

"The couriers do not carry the paper and are not to know there is a paper," Bett Ruhl said. "That is the whole of the arrangement and I am not being dramatic about it. A courier carries a strip to a steward's hand, out of habit, on a route they have walked for years, and the steward opens it, and there is a reader in the room by the time the strip is read, and no courier has ever been in a room where a reading happened, and no courier can be asked what a reading was about, because there was nothing to ask about."

"That is a secret route built out of innocent people."

"That is the only way a secret route stays a secret route," Bett Ruhl said, "and I have known that for nine years and I have never once said it out loud to anybody who was seventeen, and I am saying it now, and you may put it on your wall if you like, and if you do I will put the press in the river."

"The steward does not know either?"

"The steward knows that somebody is reading something to a person in their house, and the steward decides, and that is a duty and I will not cheapen it by pretending it is a mystery." She turned round. "In eleven years I have never asked the couriers' names out loud. Not once. Not because it would be brave to ask, and not because I think asking is wrong. Because the first time I ask a name, that name exists somewhere in a head that has a reason to remember it, and I will not do it for a book."

So it was not Lina's.

It was built out of the people who were already going to that street, for their own reasons, and who had no idea what they were carrying, and it was built in a week, and it was the worst institutional idea anybody in that room had ever had, and by the end of the second week it had read out loud, in eleven households that could not come to the table, one hundred and forty documents.

---

Bett Ruhl made the reader go.

Not Orren. Not Marda, who could not be spared from the arch at four in the morning and had said so at the length of a sentence. Not Tove, who was the only person who could read nine flat lines fast enough to be worth sending, and who was sixteen, and who said so herself, four times, in three different arguments, over nine days.

"You are the fastest reader I have ever stood next to," Bett said, on the eighth day, at the table, with a lamp and a claim in front of her and Tove across from her. "You are sixteen and you read a line the way I used to lift a barrow. And you cannot go."

"I can go—"

"You are going to a house on a terrace in the dark with a stranger's paper in your coat and you are a child with a cooper's apron on, and every Warden in this district knows what that looks like, and so do the three men on the terraces, and the whole of the point of this is that a person reading to a person has to be somebody the person already trusts." Bett put the claim down. "I will not do it. I am sixty-one and I have a bad hip and my kitchen is a public address that I have spent nine years pretending is not an address. And I have done two already, because I said I would, and I am not going to do a third until somebody who can does four."

It was a stockinger's wife, and she was the first of the eleven. She had walked down the ward market's own three delivery rounds for nine years and she did not know there was an exchange, and her name does not go in this book because nobody asked it, and the strip went back in her own hand and was matched out loud in a kitchen where two people who were not clerks were present, and the numbers were the same.

"Wait," Tove said.

She had it in her hand. She had asked for it, because the whole of the design was that the reader asked, and it was three lines long and it was a rent receipt and it was the ninth of the month.

"This is a rent receipt for the second room over the shop on Chandler's Row," she said. "It is nine coppers a month, and it says *in arrears for the month*, and the arrears are the months of the flood year, which is nine months of it, and that is eighty-one coppers." She turned the paper over. "And the landlord's name is Hask Vell."

Bett Ruhl put both hands flat on the flour crates.

"Well," she said. "That is not a road claim."

"No," Tove said.

"No," Bett said. "It is a rent receipt."

"Nine months," Tove said. "Second room over the shop on Chandler's Row, which is on the upper terraces, and the man who reads claims to women on the upper terraces at four in the morning has a nine months' hole in the rent of the room directly above one of them, in his own hand, on his own paper, and it is nine coppers a month, which is the price of a lamp shell, and it has been going in for nine months."

"And he reads her claims to her," Bett said, "and she understands the ninth line, and she has understood it every time for four years."

"It is an excellent claim," Tove said, and her voice went, and she had to stop, and she started again. "It is an excellent claim. It is the best-shaped paper I have read in three weeks. There is no fold in it because he is not hiding a labour term, the labour term is in the fold on the plate and the plate is Prent and Slade and not him, and everything he writes himself is *true*."

"That is the whole of him," Bett Ruhl said. "That is the whole man and it has taken you three weeks and a rent receipt to find it and I could have told you on the first night, and I did not, because I did not want it to be him."

Tove looked up.

"You knew."

"I suspected a shape," Bett said. "Not a name. And I have suspected a shape at four in the morning for about four years and I have never once been able to turn it into a sentence that I could put in front of a person, and that is what my hip is. I have a hip and no sentence."

---

"Orren," Tove said, at about five that morning, at the table, under the arch, in the rain.

"I know."

"He is a real person and he is doing a real thing and I have got a page on this wall that helps about a third of the people who bring something to it, and I cannot put his name on it, and I have known him for three weeks."

"You have known him for four years," Orren said.

"Do not do that."

"I am not doing anything. You have known him for four years, and you have taken tea at his table with your mother twice, and you set his type for him twice when you were fifteen because he said he could not get a boy who could hold a forme steady, and you have never once in nine nights said his name on this wall, and I want you to know that I noticed, on the third night, and that I have said nothing about it, and that I was waiting for you to say it, and you are not going to, and I am not going to make you." He put his satchel down. "That is the trade, Tove. That is the whole of the trade. In nine years the only thing I have ever wanted is to put my mother's name back in a book, and I know exactly what it costs to do that to somebody, because I have been the person it is done to."

She was quiet for a while.

"His hand shakes," she said. "He holds a wax stick, not a die. Orren, he holds a wax stick."

"I know that. I have known that for six years and I never once put it in a page, because it is not evidence of anything except that he is sixty-four and it does not make the ninth line false." He shifted the satchel. "What you have learned this week is bigger than his name and it is going to be the only thing you ever learn that matters, so I am going to say it now while we are both tired and I have the advantage.

"You can set type. I cannot. I have known that since you were fourteen and I have done everything I could this month to keep it a fact and not a promotion, and I have been good at it, and it has cost me a job I am better at than you are, and I would like that written down somewhere, because in about four years somebody is going to come along who is better at both of those things than I am and I would like it on record that I gave this up on purpose and not because I was too busy being important."

Tove looked at the wall, where four taped pieces of her own setting hung in the wet.

"What does that have to do with Vell."

"Nothing, yet. But here it is, and it is the whole of the lesson and I have been trying to teach it to you for three weeks by handing you paper: **the moment you can set the type, you are the person who decides what a claim says**, and every one of the hundred and forty-one claims that have been to that table was decided by a girl of sixteen, and about eighty of them were right and about nine were not and we do not know which nine, and no one in this city can check us, and one day somebody will print something on that stock with a name on it that is not mine, and it will be my name because I am the one who knows where the type is kept, and that is what happened to my mother and she did not even have a page."

"You have not put your name on the page either," Tove said.

"Orren Pike is on the page. It is under *set by* in eleven places, and I hated it, and I checked that it was on the first three and then I stopped checking, and that is the exact size of what you are becoming, and you should know the number, because the number is small and that is the only good news in it."

---

They did it because the only thing worse than a bad system is a person who has understood a problem and gone home.

The eleven rounds were walked by people who were already walking them: the second clerk of the ward market with her handcart, who had been turning up at gates and abutments since the nineteenth asking which of two things she was doing; two lamp-trimmers; a signal hand; four market traders who had agreed for four coppers each and thought they were being hired; the fever ward's own laundry woman, who was the only person on the list who went into that district regularly and was therefore the only person who was ever going to be near a household in it; and Bett Ruhl, twice.

The reader was Wenna Keel, on a nine-day term with a seven-day term if anybody wanted to pay for the other two, because the alternative was a seventeen-year-old and a sixty-one-year-old, and the reason that term existed at all is that Bett Ruhl refused to have it any other way.

Tove Alard set the whole thing in type on the eighteenth of the month, four hundred and eighty copies on the back of ward market claim stock, and the two things she printed on the front were the address a courier could be given and the words **READ TWICE OUT LOUD. THE NINTH LINE IS THE ONE.** and the third thing she printed, which she argued for for two days and which she is going to be asked about in a yard in a year:

**WHAT THE NINTH LINE ACTUALLY SAYS, IN PLAIN WORDS, AND WHO IS ABEARING IT.**

She told Orren Pike what it was going to cost before she printed it, on the evening of the eighteenth, at the table, and she was sixteen and she did it in the voice of a woman who had read a rent receipt at five in the morning.

"From tomorrow, every one of those sheets is a book of nine lines with a translation under it," she said. "And a man like Vell reads it in a minute, because he is careful and he is honest and he wrote most of it. And he will read the translation and he will know that we know, and he will not stop, because stopping is not in his business model, he will take the tea-table price and he will take it higher, because now he has to be better than a printed page, and he can be, for a year. And then in a year it is not him. It is anybody with a wax stick and a terrace and no fold, and there will be three of them by the middle of next month, because the third page is now the market's own stock and the market's own readers are reading it, and the fourth page has taught the whole of Alder Reach what an honest-looking claim looks like."

"That is the argument *against* printing it," Orren said.

"Yes."

"And you are going to print it anyway."

"I am going to print it anyway," Tove said, "because for a month this service has been a thing that helps the people who can get to a table, and the woman on the terraces is more of them than the people who can get to the arch, and I am sixteen and I do not have time for the version where I am right in a room and useless in a city. You can put the argument against it in front of a yard in eight months when it has not worked, and I will not argue with you, because you will be right, and I would rather be wrong in public at eighteen than right in a kitchen at sixteen."

He looked at her for a while.

"I will set it beside the argument," he said.

---

It reached a household on the twenty-second of the month, past midnight, and it cost eleven people their night and a reader's term and nine coppers, and nobody in that household can say who it was.

The steward's hand is not in this book and was not asked for. What can be said is that the strip went back matched, in a kitchen, out loud, in front of two people who were not clerks, in front of a woman who had not asked any of them to be there and who told them afterwards that she had not been asked and was glad.

The claim was a fair claim. It had no fold in it and there was nothing wrong with the ninth line and the ninth line was read out twice, and the woman who carried it home said afterwards, to nobody, that she had not known the ninth line was the one, and that the paper had said there were two, and that she had gone down the terraces and the stairs eleven years in the belief that the ninth line was a formality at the end like the word *sincerely* at the end of a letter.

And the thing she said next is the thing that is the argument against the market's money being better spent on a room, and the thing that made the ward market's money better spent on a room, and it is why the paper levy in this district went up from four coppers a quarter to forty-one:

"That is the third time this year they have taken something off us for a thing I cannot see. The market took it in the spring. The Warden's office took it after the bridge shut. And now you have come up my stairs at midnight with a lamp and told me the ninth line is the one, and you were right, and I would like to know what the money was for, and I want to know before the next quarter, and I am not going to hold the next quarter against you, because I have never held a quarter against anybody in my life, and I am going to pay it, and I would like somebody to say, out loud, in this room, what it is for."

And Wenna Keel, on the twenty-second of the month, in a kitchen under a street, with a lamp, to a woman she had been introduced to eleven minutes earlier, said the only thing anybody said that week that was really true, and it was not in a book and it did not go in the book and it is set down here because a thing that is not in a book has to go somewhere:

"It is for a room where anybody can find out what a piece of paper says."

And the woman said, "Then the room ought to be on this terrace," and Wenna Keel said, "It is going to be, and it is going to cost you forty-one coppers a quarter, and I am not going to pretend to you that it is going to be enough, and I have a nine-day term and after that anybody can do it, and I would like you to be the one who asks for it back if we are not doing it properly, because you have just asked the only question anybody in this district has asked about a public process this year, and you are not a clerk, and you are not a trader, and you are a woman who cannot get to a table."
