# Chapter 34: The People the Road Touches

Mara Quill had run a process exactly once in eight years, and it had been a hearing against a chandler who had sold the same tallow twice, and she had won it in nineteen minutes and had felt nothing about it since.

She had audited processes. That was the work. She arrived at a thing somebody else had built, and she read it, and she wrote down the parts of it that could not be defended, and she said so in a room, and the room either fixed them or did not, and she went home.

On the morning of the twenty-sixth she stood in the school gym on the terraces with a slate and forty-one names written on it, and she discovered that she did not know how to start, and that the reason she did not know was that she had spent eight years believing that knowing how to start was a thing a registrar learned in order to audit other people's starting.

"Rule one," she said, to about ninety people in a gym that smelled of floor polish and forty years of boys, "and I have put it on the wall in letters two feet high because I know myself. **Nothing is written in this room that is not read back out loud to the people who will pay it.** Rule two: anybody may leave at any point and nobody will ask them why, and anybody who leaves has not agreed to anything. Rule three: I am going to keep time and I am going to be worse at it than usual today, because I have a habit of letting a room find its own pace and this room does not yet have one. Somebody shout at me."

Somebody shouted at her inside eleven minutes.

---

The first four people who spoke were the clinic, and the clinic turned out to be the thing she had been most afraid of, because the clinic was not one thing.

"A store is not a clinic," Renn Fisk said, in the second row, on his feet, in a coat that was not clean. "I want that said first and by me, because I am the one who has carried for them and I am the one who has been carried back for them, and I have got three tolls on me, and I have crossed three times, and I am not a clinic. I am a man who moves boxes when a doctor says go."

"You are the responsible party for the boxes."

"I was, on the nineteenth, and I said so out loud, and there were five of us and I was the one who wrote the roll, and it is not the same as being the one who says go." He put his hand flat on his knee. "So if you are going to write *the clinic* on a piece of paper, you are going to write down a storeroom on the near bank and a receiving station on the far one, and the far one is nine feet of water and a stair away, and it has got a matron in it and a third of its cots empty and a draught coming, and none of that is in this room. Somebody is to say what we are doing about the none of that."

"What did you bring to the room," Mara said.

"I brought the boxes list," Renn Fisk said, "and I have not put it down because it is a piece of paper with ninety-four lines on it and it is the only paper in this room that anybody is going to lose sleep over, and it is to be read out before the day is out."

So they read the boxes list. It took a quarter of an hour. It is not in this book, all ninety-four lines of it, and it should be in the ward market's book, and it is, and it is the single most useful document any of them produced in three weeks.

The second clinic person was Dessa Rill, and she had not wanted to come and had come, and she had nothing to say for about twenty minutes, and then she said the only thing anybody in that room could have given them about the far bank and it was not about boxes.

"I can drive it," she said. "I can drive the far ramp in the dark and I know where the boards are stacked and I know the lamp goes on the left of the door and I know green is the water and red is lamps and you go on red. That is all of it. That is everything I have got and I have got it all and there is not one reason in me for any of it, and I have looked for a reason every day since the morning I woke up on a depot floor." She stopped. "I am telling you because you are going to write down a person who carries, and there is going to be a name under *carrier* in whatever this becomes, and if it is me then the paper is going to say a woman who drives a bus she cannot remember the purpose of. Write *the hands of the Line Six crew, of whom this one is one* and put the rest on a strip. I would rather be a count than a sentence."

Nobody argued with her about it. That was noticed by four people and entered later.

---

The second failure came at about the third hour, and it was the one the room had been assembling for since the nineteenth without knowing it.

The shelter's second steward, Fen Ambrose, had the roll.

She put it on the crate in front of the registrar and she did it the way she had done it on a far receiving ramp in the rain on the morning of the nineteenth, hours before the bus went, which was to put the whole thing down at once and then count it twice in front of anybody who wanted to watch.

"Seventeen," she said. "That is what is in the gym tonight. Seventeen, and the name of the shelter group is nineteen, and it has been nineteen since the fourteenth, and I have been going round the wards saying the shelter group and meaning seventeen, and every person in this room who has written the shelter group this month has written nineteen, and the difference is two children who went to a sister on the far side of the river on the second and whose mother signed for them and whose names are on a strip under a flour barrel somewhere in Ward Nine."

Nineteen people looked at a crate.

"Write *the school shelter group*," Fen said, "and mean everyone under a roof, and you will get the number wrong and the error will always fall on the household that counted honestly. I said that on the seventeenth in a yard and I was talking about a charter, and it turns out it is a thing about everything. I am not here to be difficult. I am here because I have counted seventeen twice in front of witnesses and the count in the book is to be seventeen, and because the mother who signed for those two is in this room and has not been asked whether she wants to be."

The mother was asked whether she wanted to be, and said no, and was thanked, and it was entered, and it is the only line in the public lot book of Lot 17 that records a refusal of the act of being recorded, and it took nine words.

---

The third failure was the one the room had come for, and it was engineered by the ward market's traders without any of them meaning to engineer anything.

"The count is the only thing anybody is arguing about," Sabin Tull said, from the back, "and the count is a problem because the people on the far bank are not in the room. So put them in the room. Not their names — their count. That is what the strip thing is for. There is a category, the woman Vale runs, a steward in a public book and a count and a destination and a remedy, and it went for nineteen over a footbridge in five crossings, and the whole of it is that the count travels and the names stay under a barrel. So the far bank travels in and everybody has got their number and the count closes and we can write terms."

Every head in the gym turned round, and it took about two seconds for the room to understand what had just been proposed, and about another two for Lina Vale to stand up.

"No," she said.

"It is your own—"

"It know what it is my own," Lina said. "That is why I said no in half a second. Mr. Tull, you have just described the exact thing I have spent eleven years and a night's sleep and a plank in a river making impossible, and I am going to explain it to the room, and then Mr. Vale can tell the room whether he agrees with me, because he is the reason it is going to be difficult."

She did not have a slate and she did not go to the crate. She stood in the fourth row of a school gym and talked for about nine minutes without notes, which is a thing that is on the public page about her in Lot 17's book and had never been tested before, and she was tested and she did it.

"The category is for people who have refused a name. That is the entire membership rule. It is not a bucket and it is not a convenience. If you can put any person in it who has not refused a name, then in a year this room will have a count of nine hundred and the count will be made of everybody who was easier that way, and the count will be right, and it will be right in the way a number is right when nobody is in it, and the day that happens the whole thing is finished and there will be no way on this earth to tell it from the truth.

"And that is not a theory. That is what happened to me. I was removed from the municipal rolls in a sweep in Adrian's first year and I was not brave about it, I was eleven and there was a man with a list. For six years the only people in this city who could find me were people I had met face to face. Six years. And on the night of the nineteenth there were nine people on a bus that did not come in and there was nobody in any book in this city who could be sent to look for them, and that is what I paid eleven years for."

"Which you would pay again," Adrian said, from the wall, and did not say it kindly.

"I would pay it again in a *different room*," Lina said, "which is the entire point and which nobody in this room has understood yet, including me, until about a fortnight ago. The category is the price of being findable. The moment you can put people in it who have not chosen it, you have made a room where being findable is cheap, and then the people who chose it are carrying the cost of the people who did not, and there is a name for that and it is not a category, it is a levy."

"You have heard the registrar on this," Marda Osk said. "The remedy that is reached by naming a person is not a remedy for a person who refused a name."

"Yes," Lina said. "And I am saying the harder half, which nobody says, which is that the *count* is not a remedy either, and everybody in this room has been treating a count like it settles the question, and it does not. The count is how a room knows it got everybody across. It is not how a room knows who is owed anything. Those are two different numbers and this city has been using one of them for both for three weeks, and the one it has been using is the one that is easy to carry."

She sat down, and then she stood up again, and said the part she had come to say.

"And I want it read out in a room where the man who wrote the nine lines is sitting, so that he hears it from a person and not out of a book. Adrian."

He looked at her.

"You have been in this room for three hours trying to be useful," Lina said. "You have offered the room a schedule twice and a list of things you cannot do once and you have not written a single word down, and all four of those were right, and you have been the wrong man in the room for all three hours, and I want you to hear that from somebody who loves you before somebody who does not."

"I have been the wrong man in a room for a month," Adrian said.

"You have been the *right* man in the wrong room for a month," his sister said, "and this room is the first one where there is nothing for you to do, and I am not going to be the one who gives you something to do about it."

---

The room did not produce terms.

That is the part of the day that went into the book as a finding and that the finding is worth more than any term, and when the board was told about it the board asked Mara Quill whether she was going to enter it, and she said yes, and Nessa Pell wrote it in the form the book has used since the twenty-first:

**A SESSION OF THE CONVENING HELD ON THE TWENTY-SIXTH, AT THE SCHOOL GYM, PRODUCED NO NEW TERM. THE SESSION PRODUCED A LIST.**

"It is not a session that failed," Mara said, when a trader asked her directly whether the market was wasting its money, "and the reason is worth saying in the plainest terms I have, because you have paid for the afternoon.

"I will say first who the board is and where it is sitting, because it asked me a question in this room and a board that asks a question has seats and the seats are named: **Mara Quill** as registrar, which is not a vote; **Nessa Pell** as the sitting steward; **Sabin Tull** and **Marda Osk** in the two unclaimed places. The board cannot grant a use and it cannot waive a toll and it cannot strike a line. It can publish a result, call a review, uphold a challenge in part, and set the short custody term, and all it is going to do with this session is the first of those."

"Every person in this room came with a term. Nobody came with a *process*. They came with the thing they already have, which is what happens to people who have been in rooms about a road for six weeks. We spent three hours trying to invent and we produced nothing, and what we produced instead was the discovery that this district has already written more of a road than it has ever read, and the only reason nobody knew that was that every clause of it lives in a different room and in a different hand and was written after a disaster by somebody who was frightened.

"So here is what I am going to do, and it is the whole of the afternoon's work, and Adrian is going to do it because he is the fastest reader in this city and because the man who reads other people's words to a room has nothing to sell anybody."

She stood up and gave him the list, and it is worth setting down because eight terms is the entire constitution of Alder Reach at the end of this month, and everything that happens in this district for the next month is going to be four people arguing about what to do with it.

"**One.** The named-pair return and the far-side condition. The second rail joint east of the bell post, under the lamp, where the ballast drops and the plate is stamped, in the bus of Tarin Kest, coming down from the far bank — and *if the far side is not the road you left on, stop the vehicle, get out, and walk back toward the wall.* Written by a mechanic, on a bench, for the person who drives. Tarin Kest is not in this room and he asked for it to be read and he is not the only person it works for.

"**Two.** Nine lines, offered for a future use and not attached to any use, and the ninth of them says that a second payment of the toll is not a smaller thing and is not the same thing and is not stated to be either, and that if the marking is not where the pair says it is, the joint is the return and the arm is not. Mr. Vale. In your words, not mine.

"**Three.** Four manifest terms of a bridge: a named responsible party physically present for the whole crossing, a second adult name for any child under twelve, a certified gauge housing before any passenger crossing including a cargo exception, and halved slots. Warden Marne's, of the twelfth of March, out of a real building that did not move.

"**Four.** Seven items entered in a public book in advance of any activation, and the seven of them are a request with a use and a destination and a count, the toll in the words the travellers will hear, a bearer and a remembering passenger, a term with an end, a remedy that works for a person the record cannot point at, two fresh witnesses not travelling on it, and a public way to refuse. Registrar Quill's, from a hearing in which she was right and I was not.

"**Five.** A strip chain. One strip per group of four, count and place and date and a mark instead of a name, torn in half, the writer's half kept by people the house chooses, the traveller's half carried and matched out loud in a room, and the person inside the group never touches the paper at all. Bett Ruhl's, and she built the last part of it around a refusal and not after it.

"**Six.** The no-name remedy: a public hearing, and then the record states that the remedy was called and not discharged and why, with the protected category named as the reason and no household named. Registrar Quill's, again, and the room is to notice that the two best things anybody in this district has written in six weeks were written by the same woman and neither of them is a road.

"**Seven.** A payment on a claim that has never been stated, entered as a payment and never as a security, at a figure the yard is invited to check, with the difference between it and the claim entered as uncomputed and not as nil, because a nil would be a number the payer chose. Mr. Vale's, and it is a *payment*, and the difference between a payment and a security is that a payment is spent and a security is lost on a breach, and there is no instrument in this district that anybody can hold over him in six weeks.

"**And eight.** Three lines that have been on a wall in Ward Nine since the fourteenth and are not going anywhere: **WHO CAN SAY NO. WHO PAYS IF IT FAILS. WHO ANSWERS WHEN NOBODY CAN BE FOUND.**"

She sat down, and there was a silence, and Adrian Vale took a slate and read the whole of it out loud, in the order it was given, in the flat voice he used for tolls, and it took him twenty-two minutes, and by the eleventh minute the room had stopped being a room and become about ninety people hearing their own arrangements read back to them in a stranger's mouth, which is the only thing Adrian Vale has ever done in public that was worth the whole of his trade.

When he got to number one he read the far-side condition and Tarin Kest was not in the room and his name was read out in a school gym and two people who had been in a cut with him looked at the floor.

When he got to number five he read *and the person inside the group never touches the paper at all*, and Bett Ruhl, who was at the back by the door and had not come in until the fourth hour, made a noise and then did not make it again.

When he got to the ninth line of number two he stopped for half a beat, because it is his own sentence, and then he read it, and his voice did the thing it does at the edge, and Nessa Pell, at the side, wrote down the time and did not interrupt.

And when he finished, the thing that happened in the room was not a discussion. It was a silence of about a minute and a half, and then a woman in the fourth row said, "That is a road," and a man at the back said, "That is eight things somebody else already decided," and about forty people said nothing at all, which for a Tuesday afternoon in a school gym on the terraces is the sound of ninety people finding out what they are.

Mara Quill wrote on the slate in her own hand, and read it back, and put it in the book:

**The terms of a Hearth Line are not to be negotiated in a session. They are to be collected, read aloud to the people who will pay them, and then argued about one at a time.**

And underneath, because she was tired and it was four o'clock and the rule was that things got read back:

**This is the first process in this district that anybody has run for the purpose of being refused.**

---

The last twenty minutes were the part that went in the book without anybody being able to improve it.

A trader wanted a schedule. The clinic wanted a date. Both of those were reasonable and Mara entered both and neither was answered.

Then the shelter's steward asked whether a person who paid the memory toll on the outward leg and then could not remember the reason would be asked to pay again on the way home, and Adrian said yes, and read the ninth line, and the room went very quiet, and Fen Ambrose said, "Then the return is a separate sale, and we have never sold it separately, and every single document in this room treats it as the same ticket."

"No," Mara said. "It is not a separate sale. It is the same use and a second toll on the same lot, and a toll that is charged per traveller per leg does not care what anybody has read, and the only way it is not the same ticket is if somebody stands at the far end and says the words out loud again before the case opens for the way home. That is what the ninth line is for. That is what Mr. Vale's sentence is actually *for*."

And then Teo stood up at the back, and he had not spoken all afternoon, and he had written a complaint on a notice board three weeks ago that was still up, and he was the youngest person in that gym who had ever refused a crossing and the only one who had ever written one down.

"I have got a question," he said, "and it is not about the terms, and I have been sitting here for three hours waiting to see whether anybody was going to ask it, and you are not going to, so I am going to."

"Go on," Mara said.

"Mr. Vale." He turned round. "You said in this yard a fortnight ago that the token is cracked through the plate and you do not know if it opens again. Say it here."

"It is cracked through the plate. I do not know if it opens again. Repeated use may strand the route permanently. That is what the book says and that is what I said."

"Right." Teo nodded. "Then here is the question and nobody in this book has ever asked it, and I have been in three hearings about it and none of them asked it, and I have asked it of the man who reads things for a living and he did not have an answer.

"If it never opens. Not if it opens next month, not if it opens at the spring. **If it never opens.** Then every term on that slate is a thing we made for a road that is not going to be there, and the people who are going to be told that are not the ninety people in this gym, they are the four thousand households who have just been charged forty-one coppers a quarter by Mr. Stenn for the inspection, and the traders on the terraces who have turned up with a list of eleven claims, and the woman at the market counter who does not know what the four coppers is for.

"And a man on the upper terraces is going to stand there every single day and tell them it is coming. That is his trade. He is not lying. He has never lied. So if the road never opens, then the only man in this city who can tell a household that a road is coming is the man who wants to sell them one, and he will have been right to be selling it, and every one of us in this room will have built a very careful machine whose only remaining use is to help him.

"So: **who tells them it is not coming?** Not the day it does not open. The day after. And how long do they keep being told? And who is standing there when they ask, six months later, in a market, what happened to my four coppers? Because that is the question, and it is not a question about the road. It is a question about whether there is anything in this district that is *only* a road."

Nobody in the school gym answered it for about a minute, and the minute was not silence; it was ninety people turning a question over, and it is the only four minutes of that afternoon that are in the book twice.

Adrian Vale, at the wall, with a slate in his hand and a full afternoon of other people's sentences read into it, said: "I do not know."

"Then write down that the man who reads things for a living did not have an answer," Teo said, "and write it down in a book, and not in a yard."

"It has to go in the book," Mara Quill said. "Nessa, take it in the book's words."

Nessa Pell took it, and she took it in the form the book had used since the twenty-first, and the form was not the form anybody expected.

**A QUESTION ENTERED BY AN AFFECTED PERSON, ON THE TWENTY-SIXTH, AT THE SCHOOL GYM, TO WHICH NO TERM IN THIS BOOK IS AN ANSWER: WHAT IS TOLD TO THE HOUSEHOLDS OF THIS DISTRICT, AND BY WHOM, AND FOR HOW LONG, IF THE CROSSING NEVER OPENS.**

**PUBLISHED. NOT ANSWERED. NO TERM HAS BEEN WRITTEN IN RESPONSE TO IT. NONE WILL BE WRITTEN BY ANYONE WHO HAS NOT BEEN ASKED.**

Then the board sat, with all four of its seats named aloud because they always were, and the board had exactly one thing it could do with a question, which was that it could publish a result, and a question with no answer in it was not a result, and so the board did the other thing it could do.

**CALL A REVIEW**, said Sabin Tull, and Marda Osk seconded, and Nessa Pell proposed it and Mara Quill did not vote because the registrar is not a vote.

The review was set for the day the ward market voted on the paper levy, which was the day Selik Marne's own order would be reviewed, which was the day a hundred and forty people were going to be in a yard for a rate, and nobody in that school gym had any idea what they had just done, and Teo did, and went home and told his cousin, who was the child who had refused a crossing on the nineteenth and was eleven years old and had said nobody had explained the toll to her, and who said, "Then somebody should be there," and went and asked for a place at the rate meeting and was given one because it was a public meeting and she was an affected person and nobody had ever yet thought of a reason to refuse her.
