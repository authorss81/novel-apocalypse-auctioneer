# Chapter 22: What the Span Recorded

Selik Marne had a way of doing paperwork that his junior staff found unnerving, which was that he began at the bottom of the day and worked up, and that he did not permit himself to read the top of the day first. Nine years of it. The reason, which he had never explained to anybody, was that in the ninth year of a municipal service a man can arrive at his desk at four in the afternoon and be handed two hundred pages of a morning he was not in, and if he reads them top to bottom he is reading somebody else's day, and if he works up from the bottom he is at least reading his own.

So he put the top of the nineteenth in the drawer and started with the tray of refused load sheets, and there were nine of them, and they were all the same refusal, and the ninth was the one that mattered.

Three boxes of clinic stores and a draught. The scale clerk had weighed it at five o'clock and had written **CYLINDER. NOT A BOX. CLAMP CERTIFIED TO EIGHTY KILLO OF FLAT LOAD** on the bottom of the load sheet in a hand that had plainly enjoyed itself, and had sent the sheet up, and Selik read it and understood in about four seconds that the clamp was certified to eighty kilos of flat load and that a sealed draught cylinder at nineteen kilos was neither flat nor a box, and that the load sheet had been drawn up in the ninth year of a municipal service by a clerk doing his job.

The ward had eleven cots open and a third of them empty. It was nineteen degrees out and falling. Halva Sen had been buying a lamp shell off Adrian Vale for three weeks and had a weeping joint on it and had crossed the water three times on a token that takes the reason out of you.

He wrote on the bottom of the sheet: **REFUSED. Cylinder is not within the clamp's certified load and the manifest requires boxes. No exception exists for a vessel. Take the three boxes and take the cylinder by the river stair by hand, or wait for a re-rated clamp, which is a fortnight.**

Then he stopped, because that was the sentence a man writes to be fair, and fairness is the word he had been accused of once, by a review table, in front of a woman with a feverish child.

He took a fresh sheet and wrote the second version.

**REFUSED, AND HERE IS WHY, AND I AM NOT ASHAMED OF IT.** The clamp on the near abutment is certified to eighty kilos of flat load. A sealed cylinder is not flat load and is not within the certificate, and the certificate is the only thing standing between a deck and a deck that has been asked to carry something it was not built for. I will not bend the manifest for a draught, and if I bend it once for a draught I will bend it once for a truss and once for a passenger and in a year this crossing will be a thing I have exceptions for. Take the three boxes. The cylinder goes down the river stair by hand with two of my people and a lamp, and it will take four hours, and it will arrive.

He read it twice and signed it and sent it down, and then he sat with his hands flat on the desk for slightly longer than a man sits when he has finished a piece of work.

The second half of it was the expensive half and he knew it before he wrote it. The three boxes would go over the span at ten past five with a marshal and the load sheet, and the cylinder would have to go by the river stair, hand-carried, up and across, with two of his people and a lamp, and it would take four hours, and it would arrive at about ten at night. And the two people were two people off the abutment for four hours on a day when the queue on the near side was eleven deep and every hour of an abortment is an hour of the queue not moving.

That was the second time in nine days that being right had cost somebody something. He had got very good at it. In the flood year of his fourth winter on this bridge he had refused a load of loose sand because the deck had been iced, and eleven people had gone to the upper yard and eaten for a week. In the last month he had evacuated a sound deck because a float was bent, and a woman had been turned back down a ramp with a feverish child in her arms, and he had been right about the span, and being right about the span had been a small part of a bad afternoon for that woman and the whole of the argument for everything he did.

He was not going to be told again this year that he was careful. He had decided that some time in the winter, without a meeting.

---

The far-bank trades came at half past five and there were eleven of them in the abutment office, which was a room built for four.

That was the other cost and it was the one nobody saw, because it did not have anybody dead attached to it yet. The crossing moved four small cargo boxes a day. Four. Before the tightening it had been eight for cargo and had run names for passengers, and it had run a shed of freight. Now it was four boxes a day, and the four went to whichever trade had been in the queue longest, and the queue was eleven deep, and the eleven were a chandler, a cooper, a rope-maker's widow, two riggers, a water-office clerk, a lamp-trimmer, a signal hand, a woman with seed, a man with a cart of roof slate, and the district's only miller.

Behind them, in the same file, was the fact that nineteen people were on the other bank of the river and there was no announced way to fetch them, and that one of the eleven in the room was a signal hand who was the tenth of his own crew of nine, who had not gone, who had stood on this bank through the whole of the term with a copied page, and who asked at the door that it be entered that he was the tenth and not one of the nine, because his crew counts itself twice and the yard had been told otherwise.

Selik put both facts on the table at once, because a man who puts them on the table one at a time has already decided which one the room will be looking at.

"Four boxes a day," he said. "Eleven claims. Nineteen people over there who are not on this list and cannot be put on it, because a roll is the only instrument I have and a roll of names is what I have just spent a fortnight rebuilding, and I am not going to put a bus-load of strangers on a manifest to make my own instrument look generous."

The signal hand said, "Then move a fifth box."

"No."

"Then move a fifth box today," the man said. "Once. Not a rule. A day."

"No," Selik said, "and I am going to tell you exactly why in the same breath, because you have been in this room before and you know I do that. In the winter of my fourth year I moved a fifth box for a man with a bad load of sand, and I moved it because I could see the shape of his week and the shape of his mouth, and eleven people went hungry on the upper yard for a week because of it, and I have not moved a fifth box since and I am not moving one for you, and I would like it entered that the man asked and the answer was no and the answer did not depend on who was asking."

"That is not fair."

"It is extremely fair," Selik said. "It is fair in the direction that costs me. You will be here in six weeks asking again and the answer will be the same and you will be able to point at the entry and say I never moved for you, and that entry will be worth more to you than a box, and on the day it is worth money to you I will still not move it."

He put the night's log on the desk and made them a copy, all eleven, at his own cost, because the paper is his and the copying is his, and then he sent them out into the rain one at a time with a Warden each, because the lamp on the spur had been burning since Thursday and there were eleven people going to walk past it.

---

He did the day's tapes at eight.

The housing sat in its box on the abutment wall in a glass-fronted case with a brass lock, and it had been rebuilt and resealed and certified in March by two people who were not his: a gauge fitter out of the water office called Dermot Vasey, and beside him a woman from the water office whose name was on the certificate and whose job title Selik had never entirely understood. The certificate was nailed to the underside of the case and it said, in the language of the water office, that the instrument and its three traces were complete and were reporting the movement of the span, its load, and the sump, and it said the date and it said nothing else at all.

He unlocked the case at eight and took out the day's tape.

Span trace: flat. He had the span's own chalk marks to check it against and they agreed, the way a bridge agrees with itself all evening.

Load trace: flat, because there had been no load on the deck at all today. The crossing had been shut since the previous evening. That was not a matter of caution. Selik had shut it on the seventeenth because the halved manifest had produced a queue he could not supervise and a queue he could not supervise was a queue with nobody in it responsible for anything.

Sump trace: flat, and flat for the correct reason, which was that the sump had been drained and refilled on the fourteenth and the float was new and true.

And the pilot line.

The pilot line was not a measurement. It was the thing the water office put on every housing they certified, because a paper trace that has not been touched is a paper trace that might be blank because nothing happened rather than because the pen did not move, and the water office had solved that in the ninth year before the Fracture and had never thought about it again. The pilot is a fourth trace with no instrument on it at all. It is supposed to be a straight line. Its whole function is to prove that the tape was running and the ink was flowing on the day, so that the three real traces can be believed when they are flat.

Selik put the tape on the light table and looked at the pilot line for a while.

It was a straight line for eleven hours and fifty-three minutes.

At 7:37 it was not.

There was a mark at 7:37 — a displacement of the pilot of about a third of a division, up and back down inside four minutes, the shape of something engaging and letting go. It was four-tenths of a line's width. On the span trace a mark that small would be nothing at all. Selik had spent nine years learning what a third of a division means on each of the four lines in that case, and on the span line it means a cold joint, and on the load line it means one man, and on the sump line it means a bird.

He put his thumb on the tape at 7:37 and did not say anything for a long time.

The bus had gone out of Lot 17 at seven twenty-five. The route had been open for twelve minutes. It closed at seven thirty-seven, and at seven thirty-seven the brass token had come up out of nothing onto a table four hundred yards away in a public lot with the wax already broken off it.

And at seven thirty-seven the pilot line on a bridge three streets from that table, on a span that had been shut and empty and had not moved, made a mark.

He wrote the time in his own book first, in ink, with the date, before he said anything to anybody, because he had a rule about that too and nobody had ever asked him for it. Then he went out into the corridor and sent a man on a cart to the water office for Dermot Vasey with two words on the slip, which were *pilot line*, and had sent him back out twice to add a third word to the slip, and the third word was *tonight*.

---

Vasey came at ten with a bad coat and no apology.

They did the thing properly, which meant the housing stayed in the case and the tape went under glass and neither of them touched the span. Vasey read the four traces the way a man reads a thing he has built, which is from the load line outward, so that the trivial explanation gets looked at first, and he spent twenty minutes on the sump and the load and the span before he went to the pilot.

Then he stood with his hands in his pockets for a long time.

"No instrument," he said.

"No instrument."

"That is the water office's line. It is there so the tape can prove it ran. It has never moved in nine years. I have certified four hundred of these housings and I have never seen one of them deviate." He put his finger near the tape and stopped short of touching it, which Selik noted. "Either somebody has put a pen on that channel since I sealed it, or the machine that draws the line has a fault, or the fault is in the clock, or the paper moved, or — "

"Or the thing it is standing in for has changed and there is no instrument on it to say so," Selik said.

Vasey looked at him. "Say that again."

"You built four channels," Selik said. "Three of them answer a question about the structure. The fourth one does not answer anything. It answers the question *is this machine working*, and the only way that question is answered is by the tape moving, and if the tape moves when nothing has engaged the pen then the machine is working, and the machine being working is all it was ever evidence of." He straightened up. "Your pilot line cannot be wrong. It can only be unaccounted for. And that is a different sentence, and it is a worse one for me, because a wrong instrument I can pull out of the case and throw in the river and this one I cannot pull out, because half of what it certifies is that my three real traces are worth reading."

The two of them looked at a piece of paper for a while.

"I did that on the floor of this abutment in March," Vasey said, "with a float bent and a hundred and sixty people on my deck, and the reason it worked was that I did not have to work out what the mark meant. It was a float. I found the float. Do you know how rare that is?"

"How rare?"

"It is the only time in nine years I have ever had a fault I could name."

Selik sent for two cups of tea and neither of them drank them, and at about eleven he said the thing he had known he was going to have to say and had spent three hours arranging to say it in a room where it could not be softened.

"Detach the pilot channel," he said.

"That will break the certificate."

"Yes."

"Then you have no certified housing, and your own order says no passenger crossing and no cargo exception without a certified housing, and I can get you a re-certified one with a live pilot in ten days, and I will do it, and I will do it for nothing, and it will take ten days." Vasey put his cup down untouched. "Or I can leave the pilot attached, in writing, with a mark on it that says *unaccounted, under examination*, and you can carry supervised cargo and no passengers, and you can have a live record of the thing that is doing it."

Selik thought about it for a while, and what he thought about was a woman on a ramp with a feverish child, and how quickly the sentence *no exceptions* had come out of him that morning and how completely correct it had been.

"Leave it attached," he said. "In writing, with the mark, and with the date and my name against it, and copy it to the public board at Lot 17 tonight, because in about four days somebody is going to say I kept a strange reading in a drawer, and I have been on the wrong side of that once already this month and I did not enjoy it."

---

Nell Sarr came in at half past eleven with the post report, and it was, as always, honest, and as always, useless, and Selik read it twice before he said so.

"The arm has been moved by a hand," she said. "Inside a day or two of the crossing. I do not know whose hand. There is one nailed-heel print in the gravel under one of ours and it goes toward the river wall and it is not any of the nine of us and I cannot tell you how old it is beyond the fact that it was under our print. There is no print in the gravel but ours and that one. I have walked it twice."

"Twice is not three times."

"Twice is not three times," she agreed, "and the third time will be tomorrow and it will be the same, and the reason I have written *I do not know* instead of writing a man is that I have watched a thing like this get solved in a review by a clerk who wanted the sentence to end, and I have written the sentence that ends and I am not going to write one that finishes early."

"Good," Selik said, and meant it, which surprised him.

"Mr. Marne." She hesitated, which she almost never did. "Do you want me to keep the post under a Warden hand from tonight?"

He thought about the fourth term he had put on the back of Adrian Vale's slate, the one about a party walking a line once and the book recording what they did to the place.

"No," he said. "A Warden hand on a bell post turns a piece of municipal iron into evidence, and in four months there will be a man in a yard saying the Wardens moved the marker, and he will be believed by about half of them, and I have spent this month being believed by half of a city about things I did not do, and I do not want another one." He pulled the log toward him. "Write it down that the post is unguarded, that I declined to guard it, and why. If it is moved again, that is a second time and the second time is a fact about a person."

"And if it is not moved again?"

"Then in a year it will be a post with an arm the wrong way, and somebody's boy will have swung it to look at the lamp, and the whole of this week will turn out to have been about a piece of iron and a country with nothing else to do." Selik's mouth did a thing that was not quite a smile. "I have wanted that sentence since Tuesday. I have not earned it. I am not going to take it tonight."

---

The copy went out at midnight on a cart, in a sealed tube, with two Wardens and a fair copy in the water office's own hand, and it went to three places: the Brass Verdict Hall, for the review on the ninth; the water office; and the public board at Lot 17, where it arrived in a yard that had two hundred people asleep or nearly and one man sitting on a stool by a crate who came out to take it off the cart himself.

Selik had written the covering note himself, at the bottom, in the small hard hand, and had read it three times, because he had learned in the last eleven days that the sentence at the bottom is the one that gets quoted.

*Copied to the public lot at Lot 17, in advance of any request, because a reading that no one can account for is a thing that is about to be shaped by whoever talks about it first, and I would rather it arrived in a yard in my own words at midnight than in a review in somebody else's in six days.*

*The span is shut. It is shut because I say it is shut and no other instrument in this city shuts it. The housing is certified and the certificate is now qualified in a way I dislike, and here is the qualification in a sentence anybody can read aloud: the instrument has drawn a line on its pilot channel at twenty to eight this morning, on an empty bridge, and no instrument is attached to that channel, and nobody in this city can tell me why.*

*I do not intend to carry passengers on it and I would have said so at any time in nine years. I am saying it at half past midnight on the day of a bus that did not come back, three streets from a public lot, and I would like the yard to understand that those are two separate reasons and that the second one has nothing to do with the first.*

*Selik Marne, Civic Warden, administrator of the St. Orra service approach.*

Nessa Pell signed for it, and put it on the crate, and put a lamp over it, because it was the only thing in the yard worth a lamp, and the yard did not come out, and Adrian Vale read it standing up in the dark, twice, and then went and sat down on the stool and did not sleep, and at some point in the night the pilot line on a strip of taped paper in a locked glass case three streets away lay in a depot office under a coat, in a drawer with a chalk mark on it, where it had been for nine years without moving, and where it had now moved.

That was the whole of the day. It was also the first document in Alder Reach that said out loud, in a hand anybody could check, that the city's most careful man had a piece of paper he could not explain, and it was on a public board before breakfast, which was either the safest thing Selik Marne had ever done or the most expensive, and he had known which one it was when he wrote it, and had done it anyway, which was a habit he had picked up somewhere in the last eleven days and had not examined and was not going to.
