# Chapter 7: The Price of a Name

Mara Quill opened the Brass Verdict Hall before the rain had finished.

The hall was a long room with a high glass roof gone opaque with grime. Three long tables had been pushed together at the near end. A clerk had placed a public complaint book beside them and weighted its pages with a brass ruler. The book had no cover of its own. Someone had bound it in the back of an old sanitation register.

This was intentional. A private notebook could be lost. A public book made losing look like an event.

Mara stood at the head of the tables with her red pencil and four blank forms. She had read the Last Fare’s failure account four times. The account did not become less painful with repetition. It only became more specific.

The first account said: *Route opened without valid closing.*

The second said: *Tarin Kest drove outward and returned with the memory of purpose absent.*

The third said: *Dessa Rill and one convoy escort were found at an unauthorized maintenance destination.*

The fourth said: *A replacement medicine injector was placed in escrow against the claim. Memory loss and route damage remain.*

Mara wrote that last sentence in full. She did not shorten it because the people who had to read it would prefer a smaller line.

Adrian stood near the side entrance, still in his auction coat. He had not shaved. A line of red thread was tied around his left wrist, where he had kept the red cloth after the inspection. The gesture made his sleeve look like a warning.

Selik Marne entered with two Wardens behind him. He had brought a bridge inspection sheet and a folded map marked with red circles. He set both on the table.

“The official bridge is safe for a single controlled crossing,” he said. “It is not safe for an unlisted group. If the public route is to be tried, every traveler must be named.”

“Every traveler must be accounted for,” Mara said. “That is not the same sentence.”

“It is the same sentence when a person disappears into a fault.”

A murmur moved through the hall. Ward Nine families had filled the public benches. Beyond the glass, the river wall shone with rain. People watched the room through the doors as if waiting for a person to be named.

Halloway Reed arrived with a folded coat over one arm and a small brass counter in his hand. He set it beside Selik’s map.

“Your bridge is a good bridge,” he said. “It is also a queue with a reputation. My crews can move the medicine in one hour.”

“By hiding the return condition,” Mara said.

“By making the price legible to the people who can pay it.”

“Your claim is not legible. It uses *ordinary transit memory* for a memory toll and *maintenance* for a labor toll. A buyer can read those words and still be sold a different obligation.”

“Then publish mine and watch the poor ones go without care.”

The room quieted. Halloway did not raise his voice. He had a way of making disagreement sound like a description of a bad road.

Adrian stepped forward. “The Last Fare is not available for a private bargain.”

“Your audit has already made that clear,” Halloway said. “That is why I am offering a different bargain.”

“We do not need your different bargain.”

“You need a different bargain. The official bridge is slow. The public board is new. Fear is making choices for you.”

Mara opened the first form. “Name the lot.”

Halloway glanced at the counter. “Last Fare.”

“Use?”

“One route across St. Orra for twelve minutes.”

“Bearer?”

“A named driver.”

“Beneficiaries?”

“Those who can show a need and a price.”

Mara’s pencil moved. “Toll?”

“Memory of purpose.”

“Term?”

“One crossing.”

“Witnesses?”

“They are witnesses by standing in the room.”

“Remedy?”

Halloway’s expression did not change. “A refund.”

“Refund of what? A memory is not a coin.”

“The claim.”

“The claim is not the memory. It is a record of damage. Naming it does not make the damage disappear.”

Halloway set a brass token on the table. It was not the Last Fare. It was a flat, convincing copy stamped with a municipal wheel and a number from a route that no longer existed.

“Your public process is a beautiful box,” he said. “It can hold a true token or a false one. It can hold a child’s name or a group number. It can hold a fair toll or a fair-sounding lie. The people outside it will buy the lie because it is the only one moving.”

Mara looked at the copy. “Is that a public offer?”

“It is an example.”

“Remove it.”

Halloway did not move.

Mara reached across and slid the copy into a paper sleeve. “The example is now evidence. Do not call it a sale.”

A door at the back opened. Lina Vale came in carrying a bundle of oilcloth parcels and wearing more keys than any ordinary person should need on one belt. She had cropped her hair close to her skull. Mud darkened the cuffs of her coat. She did not look at Adrian first. She looked at the complaint book, the names in the benches, and the clerk who held the list of witnesses.

Adrian stepped toward her. “Lina.”

“Keep your voice down.” Her eyes moved to the brass copy in Mara’s sleeve. “You put that in the public record?”

“It is evidence of a counterfeit claim.”

“Evidence of what?”

“Someone is selling a route that does not exist.”

Lina’s mouth tightened. “That is not the whole problem. Someone is selling a route that exists badly.”

Mara placed a blank form in front of her. “You do not have to give your name.”

“That is not why I came.”

Mara’s pencil stopped.

“The shelter group is nine children, a steward, and two escorts,” Lina said. “They can be described. Their names can stay with the people who care for them. If the board prints the shelter, someone can find the household and report it to the Quiet Registry. The children will have a route, and by morning they will have no shelter.”

Selik said, “A provisional group is not enough for a public charter.”

“Why not?” Lina asked.

“Because a group can be substituted.”

“Then list the number, the steward, and the destination. Let each person accept or refuse without a name in your book. You can hold the steward responsible for the crossing without making every child legible.”

Mara looked at her. The clerk had begun writing, then stopped when Lina’s gaze fell on the page.

“Protected absence cannot become a convenient excuse,” Mara said. “If the shelter is not named, it cannot hide a person from the remedy.”

“Protected absence is not secrecy,” Lina said. “It is the right to receive care without giving the institution a map of your home. The remedy can be reached through the steward, the kitchen exchange, and a witness who is not from the shelter.”

“The kitchen exchange is not a public registry.”

“It is where people find out whether the public registry has lied.”

The complaint book lay open between them. Its blank pages had become less blank by the presence of the argument.

Mara looked at the Red pencil in her hand. “The last time I accepted a phrase like *accepted risk*, it ended with my brother’s name on a bridge form and a statement that he had agreed to the outcome. He agreed to a shift. He did not agree to the outcome. If a protected name is omitted, I will not use that omission to remove the bearer’s obligation.”

“I am not asking you to remove it,” Lina said. “I am asking you not to call visibility the same as responsibility.”

Adrian had been silent. Mara looked at him. “You want the process because you fear your own improvisation.”

He met her gaze. “I want it because I trust the road more than I trust what I will do when the room gets frightened.”

The admission did not make him trustworthy. It made him present. Mara wrote it in the margin of the form.

“Fine,” she said. “Then use the process before the road. We will list the lot, the use, the bearer, the beneficiaries, the toll, the term, the witnesses, and the remedy. We will describe the shelter without publishing its household roll. Each person who understands the memory toll can refuse. A witness can challenge the bearer. A bearer cannot call refusal sabotage.”

“And if the official bridge demands names?” Adrian asked.

“Then we record that the official bridge has made visibility a condition of access and open the review. We do not pretend the demand is neutral.”

Selik’s jaw tightened. “You are asking me to surrender a control that has saved lives.”

“I am asking you to let the people who bear the risk inspect the control.”

“They inspect the bridge, not the danger.”

“The bridge is the danger they can see. The road is the danger they must trust.”

A clerk came to the table with a wet sheet. “Message from St. Orra. A private buyer has already used a copied Fare claim at a warehouse beneath the Ward Nine tram line. The carriers have been told to wait for a return driver. The paper has a folded page, and no one has been allowed to read it aloud.”

Mara’s red pencil snapped.

Lina took the message. Her face changed before she finished reading it. “They are at the warehouse now.”

Orren Pike appeared in the doorway behind the clerk, soaked through and carrying a roll of papers under his arm. He looked at the complaint book, at the public forms, and at the counterfeit copy in Mara’s sleeve.

“I found the source claim,” he said. “It is copied from the failed terms. There is a page under the fold, and the people using it have already begun moving carriers.”

Adrian crossed the room toward him. “Where is the source?”

“Halloway’s warehouse. I have the trail. I do not have the master.”

Orren lifted the roll. “And the people who bought it are about to find out that a copied road is still a road once someone gets afraid.”

The hall doors opened onto the rain. Beyond them, the St. Orra staging yard waited, along with a medicine shipment that could not survive another day and a counterfeit claim that had already found carriers to put under a folded page.

Mara closed the complaint book halfway.

“Public process,” she said. “We open it now.”
