# Chapter 20: The Late Return

By half past eight the rain had stopped and the yard had filled again, and the two lamps over the public board were the only thing in Lot 17 that was doing what it had been built to do, which was being a fact in public.

Fifty minutes had passed since the route closed. In that time Adrian had done three things and had understood, in the order he had done them, that he was the problem.

The first thing he had done was go to Nessa Pell and ask her to open the case.

The second thing he had done was say the sentence out loud, twice, to an empty yard, before there was anybody in it, because he had wanted to know how it sounded before he used it on people. *I can open the route again. I can extend it. I can put the token back in the slot and open the same road for the next cart of clinic stores and the six children who are standing on a ramp on the far side right now, and it would cost nothing that has not already been cost.* It sounded, in an empty yard at half past eight in the morning, exactly like a man repricing his own house.

The third thing he had done was sit down on the stool by the crate, where he had sat on the first morning of all of this, and read the charter back from his own handwriting, because there was one field in it he had never read since he had written it, at three in the morning, with the stove going.

**RETURN.** *The return follows the outward term as an administrative extension of the same use. No separate toll is negotiated. Any traveler who returns pays the memory toll again.*

He read it four times.

It was folded inside the administrative term, exactly where he had put it. That was the whole of the trick of it, and the trick was that it was not a trick: there was nothing to find, because he had not hidden a condition, he had written a sentence that described the absence of one and had put it in the place where a condition goes so that a reader moving their eye down the page would keep going.

He had said out loud, in front of two hundred and forty people, in a yard, in the rain: *anybody who comes back pays the memory toll again.* That had been true. He had said it in the words the travelers would hear and Nessa Pell had read it twice.

What he had never said, anywhere, to anybody, was what it cost, and who was going to be asked to pay it separately from the person who agreed to the sheet.

That was the breach. It was not a silence. A silence is a place where a person could have spoken and did not, and a silence is at least the same shape as the thing that was left out, and a person can argue with a silence. This was a sentence, in his own hand, in a field headed RETURN, that said the return was part of the thing they had already agreed to. He had not withheld the fact that the return would cost. He had pre-consented them to it in a clause that did not require anybody to be told what they were agreeing to, and he had done it in the same breath as promising, in front of two hundred people, that the return condition would be written in the yard within the hour.

He had been in front of this yard since six o'clock. He had not written it. The hour he had promised in front of two hundred and forty people had run out three quarters of an hour ago, and everybody in that yard had a clock in their head except his own.

He went to the case.

Nessa Pell had the token back in the case an hour after the route closed, under the same three conditions as ever, and the wax was broken and re-broken and recorded twice, and Adrian had watched all of it from the rail and had not gone near it because going near it that morning would have been wanting something.

Now he asked for it, in front of the yard, and Mara did not argue, and Nessa opened it.

He put two fingers on the brass, and the writing came, and it was four lines, and the fourth was not the standing and not the condition. It was an answer to a question he had not asked out loud, in the plainest words the object had ever used on him.

```text
THE LAST FARE
USE: ONE ROUTE ACROSS ST. ORRA. TWELVE MINUTES.
TOLL: EACH TRAVELER LOSES THE REASON FOR THE JOURNEY.
RETURN: NO CONDITION IN THE RECORD.
```

He read it out for the board, because the board was the thing that had to carry it, and his voice did not do anything to it, and the yard heard a man read four lines and understood every one of them.

Nessa wrote it in the public book. **The object's own record states that the charter contains no return condition. This is confirmed by the reader and is not disputed.**

Then he felt it: old coins, the flat edge to the sound, the right hand going slack inside its own grip and the fingers staying a little open, a man who had let go of something on purpose. He put his hand flat on the crate to hold the room where it was, and the echo went through the floor of him and out again, and the taste of old coins stayed in his mouth for the rest of the day.

Nine minutes, twenty seconds, and a stranger's hand had told him in four lines of plain writing what he had known since three o'clock in the morning and had put into a fair hand on a sheet and read to nobody.

---

Mara Quill came and stood in front of the stool and did not ask him what he had found.

"Known and unknown," she said. "Out loud, in the yard, before you do anything at all, and I am going to stand here and make you do it in that order, because I know what your first three moves are and I have watched you make them before on a worse day."

"Mara—"

"Known." She held up the wax tablet. "In the yard, now, before the sale is reopened or suspended or repriced or anything, you are going to read out what is known and what is not, and you are going to read it out in the plainest words you own, and nobody in this yard is going to be told a thing by you that they could have got from somebody else in the room."

He stood up. Two hundred and forty people were inside and a hundred at the glass and the yard was so full that the people at the back were standing on the tram rails, and Adrian had been holding that room together since six o'clock and had not once given them a number they could hold.

"Known," he said. "The medicine crossed. Fourteen boxes, counted on the far side by two men with a photograph. The nine signal hands walked to the cut with their tools. The clinic has what we sent it, and the clinic sent a runner down the river stairs with a slip that says so, and it is in my coat."

"Entered," Nessa Pell said, and it was, and the yard made a small noise.

"Known. Twenty-nine people went out. Twenty are on the far bank. Nine were on the bus when the route closed: Tarin Kest, Dessa Rill, three escort stewards, and five of the eleven children."

"How do you know that?" somebody said.

"I do not know that," Adrian said. "I know what the bus turned around with. I do not know who got back on it, because I did not write it down, and because the term ended before the bus came out. Fen Ambrose was on the ramp and she counted. Nine is the number she has, and it is not in a charter, because there is no field in the charter to put it in, because the charter has no return field. That is a known uncertainty. Say it as one."

"Entered as an uncertainty," Mara said.

"Known. The route opened for twelve minutes and closed at twelve minutes, and the token is in the slot, and the term has ended. Known. The bus is not on the marked spur. Known. The bus has not come out of the Fault. Known. There are twenty people on a receiving ramp on the far bank, and nine people are somewhere between the two, and I do not know where, and neither do the Wardens, and neither does anybody on the line from this yard to the river wall."

"Unknown," Mara said.

"Unknown. Whether the return condition that I did not write is being applied, or whether the road is running on the outward term alone, or whether the road is running at all. I cannot read it. I can read the object, and I have read the object, and I am not going to read it again in the next hour to find out what I want, because a reading is not a rescue and the last man who read this thing in a hurry put a wall through a bus."

"You said at half past seven that you could extend the route," a voice said from the back. "You said it on the rail. I heard you."

"I said I could open the token again. I have not said I would."

"You said it to extend the *route*."

"I said the route could be opened again. That is not the same as saying the people in it would find the same road twice, and I would thank you not to repeat it back to me in a shape I did not use."

"Registrar's entry," Mara said. "The reader stated a known and an unknown list in the yard before any further step. The reader's own first statement of the morning, that the route could be reopened, is entered as a statement about the token and not as a statement about the bus, and no person should act on the first reading of it."

---

Selik Marne came in at ten past nine with four Wardens and no escort and a folded sheet, and he did not come to the crate. He came to Adrian, three feet away, in the middle of the yard, where everybody could hear him, which Adrian understood was the entire point and which was not a courtesy.

"Three things, in this order," Selik said. "The charter. The security. The return term."

"The charter is in the book and in the case."

"The charter is in the book and in the case and neither of those is the first thing I need." Selik held up the folded sheet. "I have had a bus on my line's conscience for six hours. I need to know what I am responsible for, in writing, before I move a single Warden. Charter."

Adrian took the sheet and gave it to Nessa Pell and Nessa read the seven fields aloud, and Selik listened with his head slightly down, and at the end of it he said, "That is a complete document. I will say that in front of your board and I will not be argued out of it. You wrote a good charter. Now the second thing. The security. Where is it?"

"The security is an undertaking by me, made in the room, in the terms I stated there. Lot 17's inspection press and the repair-crew term. Four hundred and ten coppers and a season."

"Entered in the book?"

"Entry is deferred to the return term."

"Deferred." Selik let the word sit in the wet yard. "Mr. Vale. I asked you a question. Is the security entered in the public lot book, where I can read it and where a person who loses money on this can read it?"

"No."

"Then there is no security on this charter, and I am going to say the next sentence in a yard of two hundred people, and I would rather say it here than in six weeks in a review, because here it costs you a sale and there it costs somebody a workshop." He looked around. "There is no security on this charter. There is a man in a wet coat who said a number out loud, and a press that may or may not be worth it, and a promise. That is all. Every one of you who bought something on the strength of that number bought it on a promise made by a person in a room."

A repair-crew foreman in a wet signal coat pushed forward from the middle of the crowd.

"I had a man off the far bank on Tuesday with a bad shoulder," he said, to Adrian, not to Selik. "You said the press stood behind it. I told my crew the press stood behind it. I told them Lot 17 stood behind it, and I told them a man who reads things stood behind it, and I told nine people that their season's work was standing on something real."

"That was true."

"That was true and it was in a room," the foreman said, "and I did not know there was a difference, and I am not standing here to be told I was a fool. I am standing here because nine of my nine are on the far bank and one of them is my brother, and if there is no security in the book then there is nothing behind those season's wages, and I would like to know now, in the yard, in front of everybody, whether there is going to be anything behind them or not."

The yard went quiet in a way Adrian had only heard twice before, and both of those times had been his fault.

"There is no security in the book," he said. "There is a press and three men and a season, and it is mine, and if this charter breaches I will stand it against the remedy, and I have said so in front of two hundred people, and I am not going to be able to prove a word of it without opening my own books to a registrar, which I have not done since I was twenty-two."

"Registrar Quill would do that tomorrow morning," Nessa Pell said, quietly, "if you asked her to."

"I know."

"Third thing," Selik said, and he did not raise his voice, and the yard leaned in. "The return term."

Adrian took the charter back from Nessa and held it, and read the field, and read it out loud in the yard, because it was his sentence and it was in his hand and there was no one left to protect by not saying it.

"Return: the return follows the outward term as an administrative extension of the same use. No separate toll is negotiated. Any traveler who returns pays the memory toll again."

"That is not a return term," Selik said. "I read that at eight o'clock this morning and I sent for you and you were not available, and I have read it four times since, and each time it has got smaller, and I do not think you wrote it that way, Mr. Vale. I think you wrote it at three in the morning to a stove, and I think you have been able to read it since, and I think every minute since has been a minute of knowing."

"Yes," Adrian said.

The yard made a sound that was not a word.

"I signed an undertaking on page eleven of that book," Adrian said, "in front of nine people, that before any next use I would state the full known toll, the return toll, the unresolved terms, and the remedy, and that I would not use a private promise to make a public charter appear complete. I stated the outward toll. I stated that the return would cost again. I did not state the return toll, and I used a private promise to make a public charter look complete, and I did both of those things inside an hour and I did them because the medicine was loaded and eleven children were in the cold and I could not make myself wait, and I said the sentence I said in the yard last night, and I am going to say it again in daylight so that you can all hear what kind of sentence it is."

Nobody stopped him. That was somehow the worst part.

"The outward crossing is the emergency. The return term can be written within the hour. And nobody is being deceived, because nothing has happened yet." He looked up. "Everything that is true of that sentence was true at seven o'clock last night, and it is not true now, and the difference between those two things is nine people on a bus, and the difference is me."

Mara Quill had her pencil down and her hand flat on the crate, and when he finished she did not write it, and the not writing was louder than anything she had said in three weeks.

---

Stenn came out of the gate at half past ten with sixty people behind him, which was the ward market, which was how you knew a thing was serious: the market brought its own body.

"Three things have happened since eight o'clock," he said, to Adrian, and he was not a cruel man and he had a ledger under his arm and he was going to use it. "One. My fuel and lamp rights were posted to the sale floor for the term, and the term ended at twelve minutes past seven, and I have taken them back down, because I do not post fuel for a road that is not there. Two. Your inspector came to my yard at nine and told the ward market that there would be no third opening for three days while a review sits, and the market has believed him, because he is a man from Lot 17 and Lot 17 is where the book is. Three. The three-day service I was refused two days ago is now the only thing sixty people are asking me for, and I am going to have to tell them no, and I would like it noted that the reason I am saying no is that the public road exists and is not working, which is a different sentence from the one I said the first time."

"Noted," Nessa Pell said, and looked at Adrian, and did not soften it.

Then the crowd at the back surged and a woman came through it with a handcart, and Adrian did not recognize her until she got to the crate, and then he did, because she was the market cooperative's second clerk and she had a cart of clinic stores and a slip from the fever ward and no patience at all.

"Three more boxes," she said. "Not fourteen. Three, and a draught, and the ward is out. They are due at the near abutment by four this afternoon, and the bridge has two supervised slots left in the day because of the halved manifest, and I am here to ask whether the road is going to be open again, because I will take my cart to the abutment this afternoon either way and I would like to know which of the two things I am doing."

That was the question the whole yard had been walking toward for two hours, and it arrived in the voice of a woman with a handcart, and Adrian understood that this was always going to be how it arrived.

"I can open the token again," he said.

"Can you," said Mara Quill.

"I can open the token again, and I cannot tell you the road will be the same, and I cannot tell you what the second opening will take out of a person, and I cannot tell you whether the nine people on that bus are on a road at all." He put his hand flat on the crate, and the echo sat in his jaw, and the yard was very quiet. "There is no security in the book. There is a return term that is not a term. And there is a bridge administrator standing in my yard telling two hundred people that the man who read the thing is the only thing standing between them and an unwritten clause, and he is right, and I would like to say the rest of it before you decide what to do with me."

"The board first," Mara said. "That is the rule and the rule is the only thing in this yard that is not frightened. The board can refuse to record a closing and it can call a review. It cannot grant a use. Board: is there a proposal in front of you?"

"There is no proposal," Nessa Pell said. "There is a charter that was closed, a return term that is not written, and a security that is not in the book. There is no proposal."

"Then the board cannot act, and the registrar can do the one thing a registrar can do, and Mr. Vale can do the other thing." Mara turned round to the whole yard. "The public road is suspended. No token opens at Lot 17 until a new proposal is entered, read out in this yard, witnessed by two fresh people, and recorded, and that will not happen today. I am not suspending it because the road is dangerous. I am suspending it because the last time it ran, the document that let twenty-nine people onto a bus had no field in it for what the trip back would take out of them, and I am not running it again until there is one."

"You are stopping the road," somebody said, "with six children and two stewards stood on a ramp on the other side."

"Yes," Mara said. "And I am going to go and be sick behind the inspection block, and then I am going to come back and do it properly, and I would like somebody in this yard to come with me and tell me I am not wrong, and I would rather it was one of the people it costs than one of the people it doesn't."

---

The search went out at eleven, and it went out badly, and Adrian went with the first party because nobody could stop him and because he had been on a rail for four hours and needed his legs to be doing something.

The marked spur was the near-side track behind the old school shelter, up to the old Ward Nine platform and its one-armed bell post, and it was a hundred and forty yards of wet rail with a lamp on a post at the end of it and a canopy that had been there since the first flood.

The bus was not on it. The joint was where Tarin Kest had said it would be, the ballast dropped away, the plate stamped, the lamp still burning because nobody had thought to put it out.

The platform was empty except for nine sets of wet prints going out and one set coming back, and a handcart, and a barrow with a broken wheel that had been a barrow yard in the last hour and had become, in the next, something else.

And the arm of the bell post had been turned.

It was a small thing. It was a short iron arm on a short post, meant to be swung by a hand, and it was pointing along the spur instead of across it, and the joint was four yards beyond where a man who trusted it would have stopped.

The Road House wheel man, who had come out with the search party because he could not be trusted to sit at home and wait, looked at it for a long time. "He would not have missed it by four yards," he said. "Not in his life. He has missed it by nothing in his life."

Nobody had been near the post. Nell Sarr's people had walked it twice and there was no print in the wet gravel but theirs, and the lamp was burning, and the arm had turned, and there was no way on that platform to tell a man whose reason had been taken whether the marker had moved or the road had.

"Write it down," Adrian said. "Both things. Write down that the arm has been turned and write down that it may have been turned by nobody, and do not write down a reason, because I do not have one, and if anybody puts one in that book I will find out who did it."

They searched until one o'clock. They got as far as the river wall on the near side and the top of the service cut, and they did not get past the cut, because Selik would not let a party of nine civilians go into a Fault that had not returned a bus in six hours, and he said so in a voice that was not raised and that four people argued with and lost to.

At one o'clock a courier came down off the river stairs with a clinic slip, and the slip said the next medicine case was three boxes and a draught, due at the near abutment by four in the afternoon, and by six there would be cots opening on the far bank that did not have anything to put in them.

And at five minutes past one, before Adrian had decided what the road was, a sheet of paper went up on the public notice board at Lot 17 in a hand he did not recognize, which was not a fair hand and was not a bad one.

It was a complaint. It had a heading, and a place, and a date, and a list of what was being complained about, and at the bottom of the list, in the middle of four hundred words that Adrian had not read yet, there was a line that somebody else had written first and that somebody else had thought worth writing down before anybody had agreed what any of it meant.

*The charter of the morrow was read to twenty-nine people and the return of the charter was not.*

Nessa Pell read it standing in the yard with the rain starting again, and then she came and told Adrian, because it was on the wall of a public lot and he had to hear it from somebody.

"That is a public complaint," she said. "It has a number on it. It is not the hall's number and it is not mine."

"Whose is it?"

"I do not know. It came in through the gate in the middle of the search, and nobody in the yard was watching the board then, and the gate list does not have a name against it, and I would not have noticed except that the hand is a school hand." She looked at him. "It is the boy who refused. Teo. He has written a complaint against a charter he was not on, in a yard he is not part of, about a return that has not happened yet, and it is better written than anything I have read on that board this month, and Adrian, he is nine."

The yard was emptying. The lamps over the board were still lit. Somewhere on the far bank, twenty people were standing at a ramp in the rain with six children and two stewards and no road, and somewhere between, nine people were on a bus.

Adrian put his hand flat on the crate one more time, in the last of the light, and felt the echo come up through the floor of him, and understood that the only thing he had left today was the habit of putting his hand there at all.
