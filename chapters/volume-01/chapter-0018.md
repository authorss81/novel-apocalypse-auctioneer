# Chapter 18: A Sale That Looks Complete

The back office of Lot 17 had a stove, a table, a lamp with a cracked chimney, and a shelf, and at two in the morning Adrian Vale had covered the whole table with paper and was writing terms.

The public board was six feet away on the other side of the wall, and he had turned it to face inward and put a sheet of waxed paper over it, so that the yard could not read the sale before the sale was finished, and so that he could not see the yard while he made it. He had told himself the second reason was tidiness. It was not tidiness. He could not work with an audience and he had never been able to, and the seven lines on the slate were sitting on the table with his handwriting beside them, and he had read them four times and answered three.

The inventory was on the other half of the table. He had gone through the workshop at midnight with a tally sheet and a lamp, and the number was worse than the impression. Two presses, one of them sound. A working vice. Forty-one hand tools of which six were honest. A bench that Tarin Kest had refused to buy for four hundred and ten. Signal cable, a partial load, sold on the ward market at eleven a coil. And the repair-crew term: himself and two men he could name, who owed him favours rather than wages, for one season, which was the only asset on the sheet that could not be stolen from him by a creditor.

He wrote it out in a fair hand, because a bad hand is not a humble hand.

**INVENTORY AVAILABLE AS A PRIVATE UNDERTAKING, NOT ENTERED IN THE PUBLIC BOOK**
**One inspection press, working. Value by ward market, three hundred and eighty.**
**One repair-crew term, one season, three men. Value by hours, unpriced.**
**Total: four hundred and ten plus a season.**

Four hundred and ten was the number he had got for the bench.

---

He wrote the auction sheet from the top, because that was how a lot was described and he had been describing lots since he was twenty-two.

**LOT.** The Last Fare, a brass transit token, cracked through the plate, in public custody at Lot 17 under a seven-day steward term. St. Orra Fault.

**USE.** One route across St. Orra for twelve minutes. One marked return at the old Ward Nine platform on the near apron of this bank, at the second rail joint east of the bell post, under the lamp, where the ballast drops and the plate is stamped.

**BEARER.** Tarin Kest, sole driver and route operator.

**BENEFICIARIES.** The Ward Nine clinic line on the far bank: fourteen cold boxes and five of the medicine crew, including Dessa Rill and Renn Fisk. The Needle Tramway signal crew and ward-market riggers, nine hands, with tools. The school shelter group, eleven children and three escort stewards, with Fen Ambrose as steward.

**TOLL.** Each traveler loses the memory of why they are making the journey.

**RETURN.** The return follows the outward term as an administrative extension of the same use. No separate toll is negotiated. Any traveler who returns pays the memory toll again. The driver is authorised to hold the line, take the outer shoulder, and come down by any route he can see; if the far side is not a road, he stops the bus and walks back toward the wall, and nobody follows him onto it.

**TERM.** One activation. It ends when the second of the two named return travelers reaches the marked return, or at twelve minutes, whichever comes first. No renewal.

**RELEASE.** The token returns to the public case, the handoff is entered in the book, no private transfer.

**REMEDY.** Warden suspension at the marked spur, posted for the whole of the term. If the route bends, the route keeper stops movement; the driver returns to the marked point if he can. Two fresh witnesses who have heard the whole of it and who do not travel may suspend the next activation. A remedy for a person the record cannot point at: a steward may add a child to the count out loud, in the yard, up to the opening of the case, and the added child is told the toll herself.

**SECURITY.** (blank)

He left SECURITY blank for forty minutes and then wrote **SECURITY: to be entered before the opening of the case** and did not look at it again.

---

The two destinations had been the hardest thing in the sheet, and he had solved them by lying to one of them in plain language.

The clinic wanted the far receiving ramp. The riggers wanted the far end of the signal cut, which was a mile and a half beyond the ramp and the only place the nine of them could do their work. The shelter wanted the far-bank school house, which was four hundred yards the other way from the cut.

There is one marked return on this road and the route is twelve minutes long, and Adrian had chosen the receiving ramp, and he had chosen it because a ramp is where people are who cannot get back on their own, and the eleven children could walk four hundred yards from the ramp to the shelter once they knew where they were, which they would not, and the nine riggers could walk a mile and a half from the ramp to their job, which they could, and both of those sentences were true, and only one of them was a reason.

He wrote the decision into the sheet as though it were arithmetic.

**The marked return is fixed. Groups are delivered to the far receiving ramp. Distance from the ramp to each group's stated destination is stated here, and each group's steward accepts the walk: shelter, four hundred yards, one adult and two escorts; signal crew, one mile and half, daylight remaining after the return is not stated; clinic, none.**

*Daylight remaining after the return is not stated* was the sentence he was least proud of, and he left it in, because Nessa would find it in the morning and would ask, and because a sentence he was ashamed of in a document other people could read was safer than a sentence he had removed.

---

Mara Quill came in at twenty past three and did not knock, which she had never done and which he had stopped minding.

She was in her wet coat and she had been at the yard until one and had gone home and had come back, and she put her tablet on the table beside the inventory, which she had never done before, and which put her tablet and his tally sheet side by side in a way he did not think had been an accident.

"Read the return," she said.

"I have read it."

"Read it aloud. I have been listening to documents being read aloud by you for eleven days and I would like to hear you say this one."

Adrian read it. The lamp ticked. The stove did what stoves do.

"That is not a return term," Mara said. "That is a note about a return. A term is something a person can refuse to. Nobody can refuse that, because it does not say what is being asked of them."

"It says the return follows the outward term, that no separate toll is negotiated, and that anybody who returns pays the memory toll again. That is a term. It has a beginning, an end, and a thing paid."

"It has a *shape*. It does not have a person in it." She did not raise her voice, and she never did. "Adrian. The toll is a memory. The toll is *why*. And a return is a second crossing. If the second crossing costs a second memory of purpose, then somebody in that bus is going to lose a second reason, and a person who has lost a reason twice is not the same as a person who has lost it once, and you know that, because you read it off a cracked brass object two days ago and the object said *not stated* and would not say one word more."

"The return is the same crossing coming back. It is not a second charter. If I write it as a second charter I am writing a document that says there will be two, and then somebody will count on one, and then the route will be used twice in a day, and that is how the token strands."

"That is a good argument. Give it to me again in a week when the return is written."

"I am going to write the return. In the next hour. After the sale, when the crowd is in the yard and the sheet is on the board and there is something to attach it to."

"You signed something." Mara had the lot book open. She had it open before she came in; it was not on her tablet, it was the book, the grey board one with the crooked sign over the hinge, and she had brought it into a room with a stove in it on purpose. "Page eleven. In the yard, in front of the room, three days ago. Read it out."

"No."

"Read it out, Adrian."

"Because I know what it says."

"Because you know what it says and I want it in this room, in this building, at half past three in the morning, with the stove going, so that there are two of us who have said it."

He read it out.

*"Before any next use, I will state the full known toll, the return toll, the unresolved terms, and the remedy. I will not use a private promise to make a public charter appear complete."*

"There," Mara said. "The return toll. You have written the toll of the return in general terms — everybody who comes back pays again — which is true, and you have not written who pays it, what it is, or when. And you have told this room that the return follows the outward term, which means the people on the bus are going to be told that the return is part of the same bargain they already agreed to, and the people on the bus are eleven children, nine riggers, five clinic hands and a driver, and not one of them is going to be asked anything about it. That is not postponing a document. That is a breach. It is a breach of your own handwriting, in front of me, and if you want to explain the difference to me tonight I will listen to it, and I will still be here in the morning."

And she said the sentence he had already said to himself, in his own voice, three times, in his own head, and had not once said out loud.

"The outward crossing is the emergency," Adrian said. "The return term can be written within the hour. Nobody is being deceived because nothing has happened yet."

The stove ticked twice.

"That is a very good sentence," Mara said. "I have been in this business eight years and I have never heard it said better, and I want you to know that I can hear how good it is, which means I can hear the other thing about it, and the other thing is that it is true in every part except the last one. Something will have happened. Something has already happened, twice, and the people who paid are in a depot in grey coats asking for blankets, and the only thing that makes *nothing has happened yet* work is that nobody has checked yet."

He had expected her to be angry. He had a whole shape ready for angry, and for cold, and for the red pencil.

"The children go across because children are dying of fever," he said. "The boxes go across because a lid is warm. The riggers go because the far bank rate rises every day they are stood on it. If I sit here until the return term is beautiful, then the beautiful term is written about people who are dead, and I have done that arithmetic before, in a different room, in a clean hand, and I do not know how to make the number come out the same way twice."

"Then do not do the arithmetic. Do the terms." Mara put her hand on the sheet. "You are not the only person in this city who can read a document aloud, and you are not the only person who can be frightened in a room. Let Fen Ambrose read the shelter clause. Let Stenn read the ward market's part. Let Orren read the remedy, and let him read it to a man who can count, and let Mara Quill read the return, and if the return is not readable in public by nine in the morning then the crossing does not open and the fever ward does what it does, and *that* will be a decision, Adrian, and it will be a decision on a page, and it will not be a postponement."

"It will be a delay with a signature on it."

"It is a delay either way. The only question is whose name is on it."

---

Tarin came in at four, because Tarin came in at four, and Selik Marne came in at twenty past four with a Warden escort he left on the step because the back office was four people deep already.

Tarin read the sheet standing up, in his road coat, with a lamp behind his head. He read it three times, and on the second time he put his finger on the return.

"Read that out," he said.

"It is written."

"It is written *about* something." Tarin looked up. "On Tuesday you sat in my yard and I told you what standing is, and I said a standing is a pair, a person and a place, agreed out loud in front of somebody who can check it. And you agreed, and you said you were not going to leave one half of a pair out of a sentence." He put his finger on the line. "This is the sentence. *The return follows the outward term as an administrative extension of the same use.* Who agreed that? With what? Out loud? In front of whom?"

"You did."

"I did not." Tarin's voice did not rise; it went down, which was worse. "I agreed to a return point. I agreed to a sentence about a road. I did not agree to a return with no terms in it, and I did not agree to *this*, and I am not going to pretend that this is the same thing, because the next time I open my mouth in a room I want the room to know that what I agree to is what I have agreed to."

The yard-side door opened and Selik Marne came in and stood with his hat in his hands, and read over Adrian's shoulder, and did not sit down.

"Who is paying for the loss if the return is wrong?" he said.

"The same people."

"Then the sentence is wrong." Selik put the hat on the table, which Adrian had never seen him do. "Mr. Vale. I have watched a hundred and sixty people come off my deck in eleven minutes because a float was bent, and I have spent the two days since writing down why that was correct, and I would like to add one line to your document, and the line is this: a rule that cannot be read aloud by the people it governs has already failed the people it governs, whatever the paper says about it. Your return is not a term. It is a confidence. And I will not stand a flag on a spur for a confidence."

"Then the flag is yours to hold or not to hold," Adrian said.

"It is, and I will hold it, and I will hold it on the joint under the lamp because the joint is a real place, and I will not hold it for a sentence." Selik picked his hat back up. "I will also tell you the thing nobody in this room wants, which is that I have read your undertaking, and the undertaking is a better piece of governance than anything on my bridge, and I would like you to notice that the only person in Alder Reach who is regulated by a promise he wrote himself is you. That is not a compliment. It is an observation about how few of us are, and about what it is likely to do to a man who is the only one."

He put his hat on and went out into the rain, and the four of them were alone again.

---

They left at about five. Mara went first, and she left the lot book on the table, open, at page eleven, which she had never done and which meant that the room was a room in which the undertaking was physically present.

Tarin went second and said nothing at the door, which was the loudest thing he could have done.

Adrian sat with the sheet and the tally sheet and the open book, and he did the thing he had decided at midnight he would do, and he did it carefully, because the difference between a man who is reckless and a man who has simply decided something terrible with good paperwork is about four minutes of attention.

He took a clean sheet. He headed it in his own hand.

**UNDERTAKING OF ADRIAN VALE, NOT ENTERED IN THE PUBLIC LOT BOOK**
**Lot 17 inspection press (one, working) and the Lot 17 repair-crew term (one season, three named hands) stand behind the charter proposed for the Last Fare on the morrow to the value of four hundred and ten coppers and one season of crew labour.**
**If the charter breaches, the inventory above is forfeit to the affected parties named in the remedy.**
**This undertaking is made by me and not by the public, and is not a security entered in the public book.**

He read it. It was inspectable, every item of it, in the sense that anybody could come to the workshop and look at the press and count the men. It was real. It was his. If the charter breached tomorrow, four hundred and ten coppers of his stock and a season of his crew's labour would go to a clinic relief account and to nine people he had never met.

It was not security. He knew exactly what it was not, and he was not a Binder, and he could not post a security, and the public book in this room said in Mara's hand that the inspection press and the repair-crew term were *available, not posted*, and he had agreed to that line, and he had put the public undertaking in his own handwriting on page eleven.

He put his auction stamp on the private sheet anyway. The stamp was the stamp he had used for seventeen years on private lot descriptions, and it made the sheet feel finished, and he noticed that he was using the feeling the way a man uses a handrail.

Then he went back to the auction sheet and filled in the blank.

**SECURITY: A private undertaking by Adrian Vale, made in the room, in the terms stated there. Entry in the public book to follow the return term.**

He read that sentence four times. It was the sentence that did the work. It said, out loud, in a document anybody could read, that there was a security, and that it was not in the book yet, and that the reason it was not in the book yet was the return term. So the absence of security was attached to a piece of work that was supposedly about to happen, and the room would read the absence as a delay, and the delay as a document, and the document as a reason to be confident.

Which is exactly the thing he had signed not to do.

He left the sheet on the table and went and sat on the stool by the sealed case, in the dark, with the yard lamps outside and the rain coming through the roof where it always came through.

It was temporary, he told himself, and the sentence was almost true. It was temporary in the way that most of his worst decisions had been temporary, which was that they were temporary until the moment they were not, and then they were the shape of a life. He had said a version of this to himself in the yard on Tuesday with a map on a crate and it had cost two medicine boxes, a wall, a winch, and a man who could not remember why he was driving.

He also told himself the thing that was the real argument, and it was the argument from the yard, and it was true, and he could not get past it.

If the return condition is in the charter, then the eleven children, the nine riggers, the five clinic hands and the driver are asked to agree to a second toll before they get on the bus. And a person who is told there is a second toll refuses. And a person who refuses a second toll is a person the shelter steward has to take off a list, and a person taken off a list is a child on the wrong bank of a river with a fever ward four hours away. And so the charter has to be written in a way that lets a person say yes, and the only way to let a person say yes to a toll you have not written down is to write the toll down later, in the yard, after the door is open, in a document that can be produced afterward and shown to everybody, including a person who can no longer remember agreeing to it.

And that is the whole of the argument, and it is exactly the argument he made at twenty-one with a clean hand, and eleven people came off the Alder Reach platform because of it and four did not, and he had spent seventeen years learning to add a number at the bottom of the page so that the page would look finished.

---

At half past five the yard door opened and Fen Ambrose came in with a lantern and eleven children behind her and three women in grey coats and a man with a signal case and a woman with a cold-chain sheet and two rigs, and Nessa Pell with a lamp and the yard's own count.

They had come to board at first light, three hours before first light, because children get cold and a mother who has decided something at midnight does not enjoy the waiting.

Adrian stood up in the dark office and looked at the table: the auction sheet, complete, with every field filled except one; the private undertaking, stamped; the lot book, open, at page eleven; and his own handwriting, three days old, in a fair hand, saying he would not use a private promise to make a public charter appear complete.

He put the private undertaking in his inside pocket, and the lot book back on its shelf, and the auction sheet under his arm, and he went out into the yard to begin a sale that was, in every way a person could inspect it, complete.
