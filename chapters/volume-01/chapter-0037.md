# Chapter 37: A Remedy for a Person Who Is Not There

Rell Bode had come from the river stairs with a basket and no chair and had been at the back of the yard for two hours, and nobody had put her on a platform, and that was correct, because nobody at Lot 17 had ever put Rell Bode on a platform and Rell Bode had lodged the first challenge against a public lot in this city in front of two hundred people and had won it.

She had a sick child at home and she washed at the river stairs and she was on the public page of the nineteenth as *a person who was refused and who said so*, and the reason she was at the back of the yard on the third of this month was that she had heard there was going to be a remedy written and she had come to find out whether anybody was going to write one.

"A remedy," she said, when Mara Quill gave her the floor, which was not more than a nod and a silence and which was the correct amount. "Write it for me. Go on. I will tell you what it says and you can put it in your book and we can both get on with our day."

"I would rather you said the whole of it in the yard than gave me a sentence to write down."

"Then I will say the whole of it, and it will take a quarter of an hour, and you will not be able to make it a sentence." Rell Bode put her basket down and did not sit on it. "On the fourteenth of last month I stood in a yard and asked a question, and it was this: a remedy that works by pointing at a person is not a remedy for a person who refused a name. And Registrar Quill wrote one, and it is called the no-name remedy, and it is a hearing, and then the book says *called, and not discharged*, and it says why.

"And it is a good answer. It is the best thing anybody in this district has written, and I am not going to stand here and take it back. And it has failed once and it failed in public and it cost a child, and everybody in this yard knows what a loud failure costs, because we have all been reading about it for three weeks.

"So here is what I am here about. **You have four bearers now.**" She said it the way she said everything, which was to a room and not to a person. "And that is a good thing, and I am not against four bearers, and I am not against them, and that is on the record, because a city where the only man who can drive is the man who drives is a city that stops when he stops.

"And you cannot write a remedy for it. And I am going to explain to the yard, in the plainest words I have, why the four bearers make the remedy worse and not better, and it is not philosophy, it is a shape."

She turned and pointed south, toward the river wall, which is three streets and a service gate away and which most of the yard could not see.

"Every charter that has ever been written in this city has one fault boundary at the end of it. The bus comes up out of the cut in the wrong place and it stops at a service wall, and the people who are standing nearest that wall are the people who get off it. On the nineteenth that was eight people out of a bus and a service cut four streets west of this yard, and the ones who were standing nearest the boundary were the ones who came off it, and it was a good thing for them, and it cost eleven hours and a child.

"Now put four bearers in it. Four men, on four sides of the same river, and the fault boundary does not move, and it does not know there are four men in it, and the person who is standing nearest the boundary when the boundary turns out to be somewhere else is **whoever is nearest it.** Not a bearer. Not a beneficiary. Not anybody in a roll.

"And I will tell you who that is, and I am not the only one who knows and I am not the only one who will say it. It is the households in the service-cut yards. The ones west of the depot, in the four streets between this yard and the water, in the two blocks that the ward market has never had a rate sheet for because the ward market has never been able to count them. They are on the ground nearest the place where a bent route arrives. They have been on that ground since before the flood and they were on it in the sixth year when a road nobody had written a charter for bent and put a bus across their washing line, and they are on it now."

Nobody in the yard had said any of that out loud before. It was in a book in a different city, in a flood-year ward ledger that came down here with a clerk and went into a drawer and was never read again, and nobody in Alder Reach had ever connected it to a bus.

"You are going to tell me that a remedy fixes it," Rell Bode said. "And I am going to tell you what a remedy can do. A remedy can put a number in a book. A remedy can put a date on a hearing. A remedy can put a sum of money in a drawer and say who may draw it. And none of those three things can go and stand a person who is nearest a boundary at the moment the boundary moves, because the remedy is a piece of paper and the boundary is a place, and the nearest person to a place is a person nobody has counted.

"And you cannot count them, because counting them is the harm, that is the whole of the position I have been in since the fourteenth and it has not changed in three weeks. **You have made a category for people who refuse a name and you have made it a public service with a steward whose name is on a page. And the price of that is that the category is a count and nothing can be done to a count.** So the remedy for a person nearest a fault boundary is a hearing about a number, and the number is four, and the four are on a floor on the far bank, and the ones nearest the boundary are not in the room and are not in the number, and there is no version of a remedy that fixes that, and that is to be entered in your book in those words, because I have been saying it for three weeks to rooms that were arguing about something else."

---

Mara Quill wrote it down. All of it. It took nine minutes and she did not summarise and she did not find the sentence in it that was easier to read, and the yard watched a registrar write down an argument she had lost.

Then she stood up and did the thing Rell Bode had not expected, which was to agree, at length, in public, and then to say what she intended to do about it.

"Every word of that is correct," Mara said, "and I am not going to argue with one clause of it, and I am going to add one thing, which is worse than anything in it.

"The remedy I have been asked to write is a **split** remedy, and the whole reason it is worse is not the geography. It is that I have spent eight years building instruments that work by pointing. The sealed page points. The register points. A roll points. My entire profession is the art of making it possible for a person in a room in six weeks to say *here is who answers*, and this is the first time in eight years that I have been asked to write one that **works without pointing at anybody at all**, and I want the yard to understand how much I do not want to, because I have wanted to be the person who can point for eight years and the whole of my standing with this district is that I am extremely good at it.

"So here is the remedy, and I am going to say the two halves of it separately, and the first half is the one I believe in and the second half is the one I am offering because I cannot make the first one sufficient.

"**First: a hearing.** On a stated day, in a public yard, the board seated, all four seats named. Anybody may come and say what they lost and nobody will ask them to prove it and nothing they say will be written down with their name in it unless they ask for it to be. That is the no-name remedy and I am not improving on it, because there is nothing to improve on it with.

"**Second: a pooled count.** Every affected person, group, household, or person who is not in a household puts a count into one pool, split the way the people actually are, and the pool is published before the hearing and published again after it, and the two numbers are set beside each other, and if they are the same then the hearing has something to work on, and if they are not the same then the difference is itself the finding, and the finding is written down as a finding about the counting and not as a fact about the people who were not counted.

"**Third: a payment rule.** Not a payment. A rule that says what happens on a stated day if the remedy is not discharged, and it is this: a sum is held in public custody by the steward, drawn from the paper levy at the rate the ward market has set, and it may be drawn **only** after a hearing, and **only** by a person who can say what they lost, and it is **never** nil on the ground that nobody came, because a nil would be a number somebody chose, and that sentence is not mine. It is out of the twenty-ninth and it is the best sentence anybody in this district has written this year, and I am putting a rule on top of it and I am doing it because the rule is what stops a quiet failure."

She stopped there, and looked at the yard, and Rell Bode said: "Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"There is a rest of it," Rell Bode said, "and it is the reason you have been talking for six minutes without saying the word, and I will save you the trouble because I have got a basket and a sick child and I have been in this yard since before noon. You are about to say that this does not work. Say it."

"I was not going to—"

"You were going to say it in a corridor to a clerk, which is what you did on the twenty-first, and the yard has watched you do it twice." Rell Bode was not unkind. "Say it in the yard."

Mara Quill looked at about three hundred people, and said it.

"**It does not work.** The hearing will be called on a stated day and it will not be discharged, and the reason it will not be discharged is the reason Mrs. Bode has given, which is that the remedy reaches people by counting them and the people who are nearest the fault boundary are not in any count and cannot be got into one without doing them the harm the count exists to prevent.

"That is not a fault in the design. That is what the design is. **I am offering this room a remedy that I know will fail, on a day I can name, at a cost I can set, in exchange for a road that may not exist at all.** And I have never in eight years offered a room anything where I knew the answer in advance, and I did not know it was possible to do that until about four minutes ago, and the reason it is possible at all is that this room has spent three weeks refusing to let anybody get away with an answer they could not check."

---

The cost was set in the yard, in daylight, by three people, and it is worth setting out because it is the first time in the eleven years of this book that a public instrument has been costed before it was agreed to rather than after.

The hearing: a yard, hired, for one day, at the rate the ward market uses. A clerk, appointed, for one day. Notice printed on the same stock as the comparison service and nailed to the same eleven boards. A reader, for anybody who cannot read, and there are always three hundred people in a yard and about a third of them cannot read the second half of anything. **One hundred and ninety-four coppers.**

The pool: the steward holds it, the ward market fills it, the rule for drawing on it is in the book. **It is not to be drawn by anybody who is a bearer, and it is not to be drawn by anybody whose name is in this book, and it is not to be drawn to make up the difference between what the hearing found and what the hearing was owed.**

And then Rell Bode asked the question that made the rule into a rule, which was: "Who pays for the day it fails?"

"Reading," Nessa Pell said. "Oil, five coppers. Six boards' worth of notice, eleven coppers, and the notice has to be reprinted because the rule is a paragraph long. A reader, four coppers, from the same fund as the one at the tram arch, and the tram arch is short-handed and I am not going to pretend that four coppers is what a reader costs. Call it nine for the day and it is a lie and I am going to enter it as nine with the lie in it."

"Enter it as nine with the lie in it," Mara said.

Which is how the public lot book of Alder Reach came to hold a sum that is wrong on purpose, with the wrongness written in the same line, which is the only precedent in nine books that the registrar has ever set, and which is now the standing form.

---

The remedy was entered on the fourth of the month, in full, in the words of the people who had argued for it and against it, with the objections in the order they were given and the four that Mara Quill did not answer in the entry and the one she answered in a sentence she is not proud of.

**THE SPLIT-USE REMEDY, LOT 17, ENTERED AS A REMEDY AND NOT AS A CHARTER.**

**A HEARING, on a stated day, in a public yard, before the board, all four seats named aloud, and no proof required, and no name entered unless asked for.**

**A POOLED COUNT, published before and published after, and the two numbers set beside each other, and a difference between them entered as a finding about the counting.**

**A PAYMENT RULE, drawn only after a hearing, only on a showing of loss, and never entered as nil on the ground that nobody came.**

**AND, IN THE WORDS OF THE REGISTRAR, ENTERED AT THE FRONT BECAUSE IT IS THE PART THAT MATTERS: THIS REMEDY IS KNOWN TO FAIL ON THE DAY IT IS CALLED. IT IS ENTERED AS A THING THAT WILL NOT BE DISCHARGED, BECAUSE THE ALTERNATIVE IS A THING THAT FAILS QUIETLY, AND A QUIET FAILURE CANNOT BE ARGUED WITH BY THE PEOPLE IT FAILED.**

Then the board, which is four seats and no owner and which was seated with all four named aloud: Mara Quill as registrar, not a vote and not independent; **Nessa Pell** as the sitting steward; **Sabin Tull**, a barrow-hauler, who had heard the whole thing; and **Marda Osk**, who had been at the tram arch at four that morning and had a cold and had come anyway.

And the board was asked the only question anybody had ever put to it that was not about a lot.

"Can this board record a closing on a remedy that the people offering it have said, in advance, in this yard, will not be discharged?"

And Nessa Pell said, "That is a procedural question and it is the wrong one, and it has to be answered anyway, and it will take the four of us a day, and I am asking for the day."

Nobody argued about the day.

---

They answered it the next morning, at nine, in the yard, with all four seats named aloud again, and it took eleven seconds per seat, which was the pace of every board in this book that had ever settled anything.

What the board said, and this is entered whole:

**The board cannot grant a use. It cannot waive a toll. It does not audit a security. It cannot strike a line off a notice board. It can refuse to record a closing, publish a result, call a review, uphold a challenge in part, and set or renew the short public custody term.**

**THE BOARD WILL RECORD THE CLOSING.**

**AND THE BOARD RECORDS, BESIDE IT, THE FACT THAT THE REMEDY WAS ENTERED KNOWING IT WOULD NOT BE DISCHARGEABLE, AND THAT this is the first time in the history of this book that the state of the room's knowledge has been entered with the instrument, and the board has no power to require that of anybody and is doing it because a book which records a thing that was known and not said is a book that cannot be used on anybody in it.**

Nessa Pell added one sentence on her own account, in her own hand, as the sitting steward, and it is the only sentence on that wall that is a clerk's opinion, and it is in the book:

**AND I WOULD LIKE IT NOTED THAT I HAVE BEEN A CLERK IN THIS LOT FOR ELEVEN YEARS AND THIS IS THE FIRST INSTRUMENT THAT WAS COSTED BEFORE IT WAS AGREED TO, AND THE FIRST ONE I HAVE BEEN ASKED TO HOLD THAT I CANNOT CHECK, AND I AM NOT COMING TO YOU WITH A PROPOSAL, I AM COMING TO YOU WITH A WARNED STATEMENT ABOUT MY OWN CAPACITY, AND YOU MAY REPLACE ME AT THE TABLE AND THE REMEDY WILL NOT CARE.**

---

Mara Quill took the entry down to the kitchen exchange under the Ward Nine tram line on the evening of the fourth, because Lina Vale had told her in a kitchen on the fifteenth that an entry in this book gets read back in that room before the table opens, with two people in it who are not clerks, and Mara Quill had agreed to it in front of nine people and had not yet managed it once in three weeks.

There were eleven people in the kitchen. Bett Ruhl was at the flour crates. Orren Pike was on the step and came in. Marda Osk was there, and a signal hand, and a trader from the terraces, and two people whose names are not in this book.

Mara read it all of it, in full, including the four objections she had not answered, and the one she had answered badly.

Then Lina Vale said, "Registrar. You have said in a yard that this will not work."

"Yes."

"Then say the next part here, where the people who are going to be in the count are, and not only where they are not."

"There is no next part," Mara said. "That is the entry. It says it will not work and it says what we are buying with it."

"No," Lina said. "There is a next part and you know what it is, and you have been not saying it for two days, and it is the reason this remedy is going to fail in public and it is the thing that has to be in the room tonight or the room has been told a fact and not a cost."

And Mara Quill said it, standing in a kitchen under a street, to eleven people, two of whom were not clerks:

"**The people nearest the fault boundary are the ones nobody counted. I know who they are. They are in the service-cut yards west of the depot, in the two blocks the ward market has never had a rate sheet for, and I have known that since the fourteenth of last month and I have not written it down, because the moment I write it down I am proposing a count of them, and proposing a count of them is what I was warned about in a yard by a woman with a basket.**

"**So the remedy fails on a group I could name this afternoon if I let myself, and I am not going to let myself, and the entry in the book is going to say *not discharged* and the reason it gives is going to be the protected category, and that reason is going to be true and it is going to be incomplete, and in a year somebody is going to read that entry and think the district did not know.**

"**The yard is to know. The kitchen is to know. That is the whole of what I came down here for, and I have read out twenty minutes of law to get to four sentences.**"

Lina Vale looked at her for a while.

"Write those four sentences on the wall," she said, "and put them under the three lines, and not in the book, because the book is the law and this is the truth, and a thing that is in both ends up in neither."

And Bett Ruhl took a piece of charcoal and wrote four sentences on a wall under three lines that have been there since the fourteenth, in a kitchen under a street, and the fifth, sixth and seventh lines on that wall are the first words in the history of Lot 17's public record that anybody has ever entered who was not a registrar, and they are entered because a registrar asked to be watched.
