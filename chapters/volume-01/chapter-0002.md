# Chapter 2: A Twelve-Minute Road

The back office at Lot 17 had three tables, two chairs that matched, and a filing cabinet that had been struck by a tram axle during the Long Fracture. The dent had never been repaired. Adrian had always thought a good filing cabinet should show what it had survived.

He spread the old tram map across the largest table and weighted its corners with a bolt, two tin cups, and a roll of electrical tape. The map had been printed before the collapse, when the river crossings were still governed by timetables and maintenance crews. A red line ran from St. Orra’s east platform through the underpass, turned north beside the old freight wall, and ended at Ward Nine.

Beyond Ward Nine, the line faded into a pale patch where the paper had been eaten by damp.

The Last Fare lay in the center of the route.

Rain tapped overhead. Adrian turned the token once, then again, keeping his fingertips away from the worn rim. He had spent the first hour after the sale doing ordinary work: writing the boy a receipt under the name *unknown runner*, separating the token’s municipal stamp from a dozen similar brass discs in his drawer, and searching the old salvage notices for a bus that had crossed St. Orra before the cut. He found three references. Two had been entered as scrap. The third said **eastbound coach, recovered fare, condition unsafe**, then stopped in the middle of a clerk’s hand.

He had also told Nessa not to let anyone buy the token. He had not told her why. That was not the same thing as keeping a secret. It was simply a decision he had not yet earned the right to explain.

He placed the token over the red line.

Nothing happened.

He tried the old platform mark first, then the point where the line passed beneath the river wall. The token remained ordinary brass. Its temperature changed with his hand and no more.

Then he set the blank lot card down beside it, face to face, the way Iven had once made him set a ledger beside a lie he was certain of. He had never been able to say why. The two of them lay there together on the old paper and gave each other nothing at all.

Adrian drew a small circle around the east platform.

The torn manifest lay beside the map. Its surviving columns were mostly useless: cargo weight, wheel count, and the words **conductor change**. A second line had been written in a different hand.

**Return only if the driver can name the near stop.**

Beneath the torn columns, a narrow transfer strip was still legible. It carried the old East Apron salvage board’s stamp, the name of Lot 17’s bonded salvage table as the receiving depot, and two faded witness marks. **INACTIVE TOKEN — CUSTODY CHARTER — FINDER: ORREN PIKE — RECEIVING DEPOT: LOT 17 — TERM: UNTIL ACTIVATED OR RETURNED — USE: NONE.** The strip named the finder and the receiving depot; the witnesses and the remedy for an unclaimed delivery were still legible. It authorized the token to leave the stranded bus while it remained unactivated. A custody charter was not a right to activate it.

The date was worn away.

He placed the manifest fragment over the token. Still nothing.

“Routes don’t work because a piece of metal matches a line,” he said to the empty room. “They work because someone says where the line is going.”

He turned the token over.

The black writing returned.

```text
THE LAST FARE
USE: ONE ROUTE ACROSS ST. ORRA. TWELVE MINUTES.
TOLL: EACH TRAVELER LOSES THE MEMORY OF WHY THEY ARE TRAVELING.
STANDING: A DRIVER WHO KNOWS THE ROUTE. A RETURN POINT.
A PASSENGER WHO REMEMBERS THE DESTINATION.
TERMS INCOMPLETE. REPEATED USE MAY STRAND THE ROUTE.
```

The room narrowed at the edges. A metallic taste spread across the back of his tongue, sharp enough to make him swallow. He set the token down before the words faded.

The first dizziness was mild. He could have blamed the coal stove and the stale air. The second was not mild. He reached for his pencil, then discovered it beneath his own hand. He had no memory of putting it there.

For a few seconds, the reason he was standing at the table seemed equally uncertain. Not why he had opened the office. Why he had needed to open it at all.

Then Nessa called from the sale floor, and the room came back.

Adrian wrote **CONTACT WARNING** at the top of the map. His handwriting slanted farther to the right than usual. He listed what he knew, what he thought he knew, and what he did not know.

**Known by inscription:** one route. Twelve minutes. A memory of purpose paid by each traveler. A driver, a return point, and a passenger who remembers the destination.

**Known by map and witness:** the bus route reached the destination bank at Ward Nine. A bus associated with the token was last seen out past the underpass, on this bank, inside the ring Tarin draws on his own road map.

**Unknown:** whether the return point can be Ward Nine. Who must be the passenger. Whether the memory is lost on every crossing or only on the first. Whether the route ends at twelve minutes or ends when the last traveler returns. What the Tally means by stranding.

**Unsafe assumption:** that the return is an administrative detail rather than another use.

He read the last line twice. His hand was trembling now. The tiredness had a particular texture, as if a part of him had been used to hold up a door and had not yet been released.

He did not touch the token again.

A knock struck the office door hard enough to shake the filing cabinet.

“Adrian.” Tarin Kest did not wait for an answer. He came in with rain in his beard, a road-map case under one arm, and the expression he wore when a machine had failed in a way that was going to cost somebody money. “The depot roof is going to cost me a week of work. If you have a new way to lose money, I can at least inspect it.”

Tarin had been running the Needle Road House since before the Long Fracture. He had driven freight through every pass and flood cut between Alder Reach and the coast, and he distrusted any description of a road that did not include the place where it would fail. His hands were broad and permanently scarred with grease. One thumb carried a black stain that no amount of washing had removed.

Adrian pushed the map toward him. “Line Six.”

Tarin looked at the token on the red cloth. His expression changed.

“Where did you get that?”

“The runner who put it under my block. He says it came from a bus that crossed before the cut.”

“St. Orra east coach.”

“You know it?”

“I know the route. Not the bus.” Tarin set down the map case without opening it. “Before the Fracture, Line Six went under the river wall, stopped at a maintenance bay that was never on the public map, then came up through Ward Nine. The official timetables called it a short diversion. Drivers used it when the lower platforms were flooded.”

“Why was it not public?”

“Because the maintenance bay was a transfer point for emergency crews. The city did not want people waiting there if the wall shifted.”

Adrian indicated the margin line on the manifest. He had read it twice.

Tarin read them and swore softly. “That was an old emergency rule. If the bus came back to a stop nobody could name, it was considered lost. It kept a route from accepting every broken vehicle that came down the road.”

“That was before the Fracture.”

“It was before people started making rules out of whatever the ground remembered.” Tarin traced the red line with one thick finger. “The near stop is here. Ward Nine old platform. There is a loading spur behind the school shelter, under a steel canopy. If the wall holds, a driver can find it by the tram bell post. The platform itself is cracked, but it has not fallen.”

Adrian felt the return point click into place, and immediately disliked the comfort of it. A map line was not a destination. A remembered place was not a safe one.

“Can you drive it?”

Tarin looked at the token. “I can drive a bus over Line Six if the river wall has not moved. I cannot promise I would remember why I was on the other side after the toll took hold. That is not a small distinction.”

“Someone has to remember the destination,” Adrian said.

“Then the route needs more than a driver and a line on paper.”

The words were not in the Tally’s notice in that form, but they matched the old warning in the manifest margin. Adrian wrote **passenger standing unclear** beside the return point.

Tarin opened his map case and spread a second route over the first. His was newer, patched at the corners, marked with wheel wash and flood depth. The newer line stopped at the underpass. A hand-drawn red ring enclosed the eastern bank.

“The bus is there,” he said. “Or was. The ground took a bite of the road after the last crossing. We cannot reach it to inspect the token in place.”

“Then we do not activate it.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“I sound relieved.”

Tarin glanced at the list on the map. “You have written a great many things in a very small space.”

“Most of them are questions.”

“The word *return* appears three times.”

“It is the part that keeps moving.”

A knock sounded on the office door, softer than Tarin’s. Nessa opened it only after Adrian called out. She had a municipal seal case in one hand and a red pencil tucked behind her ear.

“The registrar is here,” Nessa said. “She says she is conducting a routine audit of the sale book. She also says your private inspection item is now the only item in the building worth auditing.”

Tarin looked from the token to the door. “Mara Quill?”

“She’s outside,” Nessa said.

Adrian looked at the map. The list of unknowns had not made the route safe. It had only stopped him from calling it safe before he knew.

Outside, Mara Quill opened her red tablet and read the first line aloud.

“Adrian Vale,” she said from the threshold, “you are going to tell me what that thing does before anyone in this room dies for lack of a record.”
