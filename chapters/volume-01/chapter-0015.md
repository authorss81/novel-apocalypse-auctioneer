# Chapter 15: What the Driver Knows

The Needle Road House kept its bench under a corrugated roof that had been the roof of a tram substation, and under the roof the whole business happened at the height of a man's chest, which Adrian had come to think was the only honest height for the truth about vehicles.

Tarin's bus stood on the near side with its east axle out and a new one leaning against the wall beside it, wrapped in oiled paper. The new axle was the expensive fact of the afternoon. The old one lay on the bench in three pieces, and the middle piece had a bloom of grey metal on the fracture face that Adrian had looked at once and then had not looked at again.

"You are not going to ask me what that is," Tarin said.

"I am going to ask you what it means."

"It means I do not put that bus on St. Orra with that axle in it."

"It could hold for a short run."

"It could hold until the third time somebody leaned on it." Tarin turned the middle piece over with two fingers, the way a man turns a tooth. "The bloom is fatigue. Fatigue is not a crack you can see coming. It is a thing that has been arriving for a year and has finally told you, and if you ask it to carry one more heavy run it will tell somebody else, and the somebody it tells will be a child with shoes in his hands."

"How long."

"Two days if nobody rushes me. Three if I sleep."

"You have Bryn's fever to sit with."

"I have Bryn's fever to sit with and an axle to fit and a Road House that has four carts with bad hubs." Tarin set the piece down. "So do not do the thing where you look at me until I name a number. I have watched you do it to a loading ramp. Name what you want and let me tell you what it costs."

---

Adrian put the blank slate on the bench where the tools were and did not put a price on it.

"I want the return condition written honestly," he said. "For a second closing. I cannot write it because I do not know enough about how you bring a bus back to a place, and every time I try to write down what I know, it turns into a form, and the form is wrong, and I cannot tell you where it is wrong."

"Good," Tarin said. "Then do not write it."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer there is until you have been where I have been." Tarin came round the bench and sat on a stool, which he did not do in front of people. "You want to know what standing is. Everybody who has ever wanted a road through that Fault has asked me that, and every one of them has wanted it in the shape of a sentence they could put on a sheet. So here is the sentence, and then I am going to take it away from you, and you can have it back when you have earned it."

He picked up an old route card, a stiff brown thing the size of a playing card, and held it up so Adrian could see the printing.

"Line Six, pre-Fracture. Emergency working card. Driver's issue. That is what this is. It does not show the public stops. It shows the cut, the counterweight house, the fuel bay, the two places where the wall moves, and the emergency transfer point up beyond the river wall on the way through Ward Nine. Every man who drove the night service in the old city had one of these. That is all it is. It is a list of places where the world is not what you were told."

"And yours has things drawn on it."

"Mine has a ring in ink where the old bell post was, and a ring in red chalk inside it that a man called Adrian Vale drew with my chalk in front of a crowd, and I did not stop him." Tarin turned the card over. On the back, in pencil, in a large unhurried hand, were a dozen small marks with times beside them. "And that is mine. The times are when I came down this route with an empty bus in the last nine years and looked at where the wall had got to. That is not knowledge anybody can carry in a satchel. It is nine years of a man's knees."

He put the card down and picked up instead a length of chalk, and drew a short line on the bench's edge in the grease.

"You are asking me what standing is. Standing is not a property of the token. I have watched three men try to hold that token and it does not care what their hands are for. Standing is a relationship between three things: a person, a route, and a return point. Take away the person and there is nothing. Take away the route and the person is a man driving in circles who will insist he is nearly there. Take away the return point and the person has somewhere to go but no reason, and a driver with somewhere to go and no reason will drive on past the place he was supposed to stop, because there is nothing to make him stop and no memory to make him stop for."

"So the return point has to do the work his memory cannot."

"Exactly. It has to be something his hands know before his head asks." Tarin tapped the bench. "The bell post with one arm is good. It is good because it is ugly and it cannot be improved, and a man sees it and his body stops without consulting anybody. But it is not good enough, and here is why it is not good enough: a bell post is on the ground. Anybody with a spanner and an hour can move a bell post, and if they move it, my hands will stop a hundred yards further along, and on a normal road that is nothing, and on that road it is a different basin entirely."

"So the second closing names something firmer."

"The second closing names a joint." Tarin drew four short strokes on the bench. "Second rail east of the bell post, under the lamp, where the ballast drops away and the plate is stamped. My bus has hit that joint ten thousand times and my foot knows it and I could find it with the lights off, in the dark, with no idea why I was driving, and I would not go a yard past it. That is a return point. The bell post is a landmark. I want a landmark that is also a stop."

---

Adrian had been reaching for his pencil, and he took his hand off the slate instead, which was a thing he had not planned to do and could not afterwards explain.

"Do not write that down yet," Tarin said.

"It is exactly the sort of thing that has to be in the charter."

"It is exactly the sort of thing that has to be in your head for a week before it goes on the sheet." Tarin's voice went up half a tone, which from him was a shout from anybody else. "You want to put my foot in a form, and then a man from a ward market is going to read the form and agree that a joint is a suitable return point, and it will be in the book, and then the second time somebody moves a lamp the whole city will have a piece of paper saying that a joint is a return point, and it will be true, and it will be useless, because the next bus will be a different bus with different feet."

"Whose feet?"

"Whoever is driving. That is the whole point and you keep walking past it." Tarin put the chalk down. "I am not the bearer. I am the only bearer *I have*, and there is a difference, and in four years there will be a woman on the outer tram who can hold that line better than I can, and the day she can, the return point stops being my joint and becomes hers, and the same joint will be a different place to her because she comes down it from the other direction. And if the charter says *the second rail east of the bell post* and it does not say *in the bus of Tarin Kest, coming down from the far bank*, then in four years you have published a road that stops somewhere nobody goes."

Adrian sat with that.

"So the standing is in the person."

"The standing is in the pair." Tarin tapped the bench again. "The person and the place, agreed out loud, in front of somebody who can check it. That is all a charter is. It is a sentence that makes two things agree in public, and when the two things stop agreeing, the sentence is finished and somebody has to write another one. That is why I will not let a route be permanent. Not out of sentiment. Because a permanent route is a permanent sentence about a man and a place, and the man gets older and the place moves, and the sentence does not."

He picked the route card up again and looked at it for a moment, and then he said the thing Adrian had come for without knowing he had come for it.

"You cannot read me into a form."

"I could try."

"You would get it wrong and it would be my fault and yours." Tarin turned the card over and back. "You have read a cracked brass token and found out a toll and a set of conditions and it was the most useful reading anybody has done in this city in nine years. And every one of us who watched you do it has been quietly waiting for the day you read me. And you cannot. I am not a lot. I am a man on a bench. If you put me in a form you will have made an article, and an article does not stop a bus, and the day the article is wrong the article will still be in your book and the bus will still be in the water."

---

They ate at four, standing up, out of a tin between them, and Adrian's right hand was unreliable enough that Tarin took the tin without comment and held it.

Bryn came out from under the stairs at some point and stood at the end of the bench and did not say anything for a quarter of an hour, and then said, "I want to go in the bus."

"No," Tarin said.

"I would remember the route."

"You would remember the route for about an hour and then the route would be a place you went, and in a year you would tell somebody you were chosen for it, and it would be a good story, and it would be false, and I would have to listen to it." Tarin put the spoon down. "You do not get to want it. That is the whole of it. There is no arrangement in which wanting it is allowed, and if you want a life where you do get to want it, you should be arguing for bridges, not buses."

"That is not fair."

"No," Tarin said. "Go upstairs. Tell your mother I said the fever is going down."

---

Adrian asked for Dessa Rill in the end, and she came at five because Tarin sent a runner for her and she came without being asked why.

He had prepared three questions. He had them in order on the slate, and he got through one.

"Where does the road come in?"

"Under the west wall of the depot, past the fare box, then it turns at the old bell post." She said it the way a person recites a line they have said to themselves. "There is a curve where the tram boards used to be, and the wall on the left is not a wall, it is a thing that has decided where the road is, and if you touch it the road agrees with you for about a length of bus and then it stops agreeing."

"That is very specific."

"I do not know where it came from."

"Who is waiting for you at the far end?"

Dessa Rill opened her mouth, and there was nothing behind it, and she stayed that way long enough that Adrian felt the room go cold in a way that had nothing to do with the tin.

"I do not know," she said. "I know the place. I know there is a lamp on a roof of stacked loading boards and a handcart with a folded list, because I have said it out loud more times than I can count and the words are worn smooth. I do not know who put the cart there."

He put the second question away. He did not ask the third, which was about the lights, because he had asked Dessa about the lights twice already in the last three days and had gotten the same answer twice, and the answer was wrong.

"You said the lights were green," he said instead. "You said a driver cannot cross the river wall if the lights are green. But the Warden rule is that green means the span is stable. Which is it?"

Dessa frowned at him. "Green is the water," she said. "Green is when the wall has moved and the cut is full and you do not go. Everyone who drove that line knew it. Red is lamps. Red is lamps and you go when the lamps are red, because red means the pumps are running and the wall is being watched."

Adrian looked at Tarin.

Tarin had gone very still on his stool. "Say that again."

"Green is the water. Red is lamps. You go on red."

"That is the old rule," Tarin said slowly. "That is the rule from the working card. That has been the rule at that abutment since before the Fracture and I have never heard a Warden use it and I have never seen it in a signal book." He set the tin down. "Somebody taught her that. Somebody taught her the *working* rule, which is the card rule, the rule for the man in the street who is not the control, and it is in her the way the line is in her feet."

"She has lost her reason and kept her rules," Adrian said. "And the rules she kept are old ones, and the new ones are in a book in this city with my name beside the entry."

He understood, then, why Dessa could not be the passenger again. Not because she had forgotten. Because what she had left was a set of instructions for a city she was not in, delivered by a person with no idea what they were for, and a route held open by a passenger's memory is a route held open by whatever the passenger still believes is true. The thing he did not say was that nothing she had left was a belief about the river: whatever had made her dangerous to hold a route had gone out with the reason. He watched it go past him and did not pick it up, because picking it up would have meant an argument he did not have the hour for, and because it was his objection and not Tarin's, and he had never once in his life put an objection of his own in front of a yard.

"She can ride," Tarin said. "She can ride and be counted and pay what everybody pays. She cannot hold the destination for anybody."

---

The repair came to forty hours of shop time, four coppers of hardware, and a night shift, and Adrian had exactly none of the three in the way that mattered.

Tarin did the sum out loud, which he had never done before, and Adrian understood that it was a courtesy.

"Forty hours is five days on your hands," Tarin said. "The press and the crew are yours and I will not touch them for this, so forget that. Forty hours, and you do the wheel shop, because my wheel man has a bad shoulder and a worse temper, and you come in at six and you do not talk to me, and I do not care what you do with your evenings."

"That is a fair price."

"It is a shop price. Lot 17 has a yard full of salvage in it that has not been re-rated since the flood, and half of it is priced on what it was worth when it came in. Somebody is going to be misled by that inside a year, and it will be one of my people, because my people come to you. So: forty hours, and you walk my yard and put honest numbers on nine carts and a hundred and forty lots, and you do it for nothing, because the price of an axle is forty hours and you do not have forty hours, and I am not going to take the other thing from you."

Adrian put his hand flat on the bench, on the oiled paper of the new axle.

"Written down," he said. "In the book, in both books, as a cost of the second closing, before the second closing. Not afterwards. You can have my inspection week and my wheel nights and the honest re-rating, and the yard will be able to read what it cost to stand a bus on that Fault, and when somebody in four years says the public route was cheap, the yard can look at the entry."

Tarin looked at him for a while.

"You understand that is the first useful thing you have said to me since the token appeared," he said. "And I am aware that it is useful partly because it is visible. I would rather have it visible and cheap than invisible and free."

"Visible and cheap," Adrian said.

"Right." Tarin reached under the bench and came up with a second card. "Now sit down, because you have been standing there for an hour looking at a map the way a man looks at a horse he has been told is slow, and I am going to show you the thing that will make your second closing a different crossing from your first, and then I am going to bed."

The second card was older and dirtier than the first, and the printing on it was a different hand, and it was not a working card. It was a general arrangement drawing with a title strip: **LINE SIX — ST. ORRA SECTION — MAINTENANCE AND EMERGENCY TRANSFER — NOT FOR PUBLIC ISSUE.**

"Before the Fracture," Tarin said, "the public maps of that line stopped at the river wall, because the public maps were for people who were being paid to be customers. The section past the wall was not a passenger section at all. It was a transfer bay where emergency crews swapped from the street into the tunnel, and it is on this card and on no other map in this city, and it has been on no map since before I was born."

Adrian bent over it. "And the route goes through it."

"The route goes through it *or around it*, and which one depends on the day, and I have never once seen it decide in front of me." Tarin put a finger on the drawing. "The track past the wall does not run straight. It cuts through a service cut, and the cut is a place where the ground has been arguing with itself for seventeen years, and when the route comes up through there it can come up on either side of the cut. On the first night it came up on the wrong side, and there was a service wall where there should not have been a wall, and that is where my bus spent its afternoon."

"The maintenance bay."

"A different one. Same name, same family, wrong side of the water and four streets out." Tarin tapped the card. "This bay is on the far side, up through Ward Nine, and it has not been used in nine years, and I have never taken a bus into it. And here is the thing I have been chewing on since Tuesday, and here is why I did not tell you at the yard."

He took the chalk and drew a small hook on the bench beside his line.

"You wrote a return condition on a bell post, and the bell post is fine, and the bell post is at the near end where the ground has not moved in three years. But the part I do not trust is the part you cannot see from the near end. The bend happens up there, past the wall, in a cut, and I find out which way it has come out *after* the bus is already through. So a return point at my end tells me where to stop and tells me nothing about whether the road in front of me is the road I left on."

Adrian sat with that. It was the first time since the token appeared that he had been shown a thing about the crossing that his reading could not reach at all, and it was not a small thing.

"So the second closing has to say more than where to stop."

"It has to say what I am allowed to do when the far side does not look the way I left it." Tarin put the chalk down and did not draw anything else. "That is a sentence in a charter that I have not got yet, and neither have you, and I am not signing anything until somebody writes it. Not because I am cross with you. Because I have been driving for twenty-six years and I have watched exactly this thing kill people, twice, and both times the sentence was written afterwards by a man who was not there."

Bryn called down from the stairs that the fever was going down.

"Good," Tarin said, and picked up the tin, and looked at Adrian over the top of it. "Tomorrow morning. Bring your terms and bring the whole return, and read it at the bench, in the open, before anybody gets on my bus. And if you cannot write that sentence, then bring the question and we will fail in the daylight like people instead of at midnight like a foundry."

Then he put the two route cards on the shelf over the bench, side by side, the working card and the drawing that was not for public issue, and Adrian stood there in the grease-smell and the cold and understood that a road was not a thing he was buying.

It was a habit, kept by people, about a place that would not stay put. And the only question left in the morning was who was going to hold the destination in their head while everyone who had any other reason forgot theirs.
