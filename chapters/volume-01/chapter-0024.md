# Chapter 24: The Bill Comes Due

The first thing Mara Quill did on the morning of the twenty-first was get the copies out of the building, and the reason she did it first was that by six o'clock she had understood, standing in front of the notice board with a lamp, that the depot was about to become a witness in its own case, and a building that can testify is a building nobody can check.

She sent Orren Pike to the water office for a copy of Selik Marne's midnight note before there was any light in the street. She sent Nessa Pell's fair hand, which was the only hand in Alder Reach anybody could check, to three places inside two hours: the Brass Verdict Hall, the school shelter, and Bett Ruhl's kitchen exchange under the Ward Nine tram line, where Lina Vale had a wall in charcoal and eleven people who could not read. She did all of this without asking Adrian Vale for permission, in the flat voice she used for things that were not optional, and she wrote the three requests into the day book at the top, with the times, because the day book was the one document she had never once let anybody else write in.

By eight o'clock there were four copies of Lot 17's public lot book in the city, and by nine there were nine, because a copying clerk on the terraces had taken the whole of it to a tea table out of pure dislike, and Halloway Reed had sent a man with a barrow.

"You are aware," Adrian said, at the rail, "that in about four hours a man who sells counterfeit claims will have a complete record of every charter this lot has ever closed."

"I am aware," Mara said. "I have known it since eight o'clock this morning and I decided at eight o'clock this morning, and I would like it noted that I decided it and not that it happened to me. In four months somebody is going to use this book to sell a claim, and I would rather it was the complete one and that the copies were in nine hands and not one, and I would rather that the man who has nine copies had them from me and charged for nothing."

---

The board met at half past nine, which is not a thing a board does, and it did it because the steward term had two days left and Nessa Pell was not going to let a term expire in the middle of the worst week Lot 17 had ever had, which is how she got a board sitting at half past nine in the morning with four seats and a queue outside the door.

"State the seats aloud," Mara said, before anything else, because that was the rule and because the rule was the only thing in the building that had not been hit.

"Mara Quill, registrar. I am not a vote and I am not independent of anything on that board."

"Nessa Pell, sitting steward. Two days of term left and I would like one more week and I am asking in front of the yard so that nobody can say I was given it."

"Sabin Tull. Barrow-hauler. I carry claims and I read them before I sign them, and today I would like it entered that I have refused four in nine days and that I am sitting here because I have no claim on this lot, which is the only qualification I have ever been proud of."

"Marda Osk. Tea at the tram arch. I read the toll out loud to the people at the gate when they will not come in, which is most days, and I have no claim on the lot and I would like it noted that I sell tea to a hundred people a day and that not one of them has ever asked me whether a bus came back."

"Renew the steward's term for seven days," Mara said, "and I want it recorded that the sitting steward asked for it in the yard on the record rather than at the back of the room, and that the reason she gave is that the object in the case on that crate is due back into her term in a week and a term that expires in a crisis is a term that can be ended by anybody who is uncomfortable."

"Renewed," said Nessa Pell, and Sabin Tull, and Marda Osk, and the registrar, who abstained, and it took eleven seconds, and Adrian did not speak in it, and afterward nobody could have told you which of the three had done the most.

---

Then the child.

There was no name to write and there was a great deal to write, and Mara found that the second of those two facts had never once before been a problem in her working life and was now the entire problem, and she wrote it out in the yard on the crate with the book open and about two hundred people standing in the wet because there is no other room in Alder Reach that two hundred people can be got into.

**Entry, the twenty-first day. One child of the second school shelter group, marked on a sealed sheet and not named in this book, was found at the near-side maintenance spur at nine minutes past seven in the evening of the nineteenth, in the care of one escort steward, after eleven hours and forty minutes. She was carried to this lot at a quarter past ten. She did not wake. She died in the small hours of the twentieth, on the floor of the depot with six other children asleep around her and two women from the clinic with her, and nobody in that room was keeping the hour, and so this entry does not carry one, because I am not going to put a time on a thing in a book that no person alive saw happen. She is not named in this book because nobody asked her, and the category that keeps her name out of this book is the reason that no remedy can point at her, and that sentence is the whole of what this entry is.**

The yard was extremely quiet in the way a yard is quiet when it has understood that the person reading is not going to stop.

"The remedy is called," Mara said. "Not discharged. I am recording that now, in front of you, and I am going to say why in my own hand and not in a clerk's, and then I am going to hold the hearing, and it is going to take an hour, and it is going to be the worst hour of my year, and I am not going to pretend that holding it discharges it."

"Entered," said Nessa Pell.

"No," Mara said. "Not yet. First the account of what happened to the paper, because the child is the end of this and not the beginning of it, and I have watched a room turn a death into the reason it is late, and I am not going to let this yard do that to her this morning."

---

They found it in ninety minutes by comparing copies, which is what copies are for.

The first change had been on the notice board at four in the morning, and it was the one everybody had seen. The second was in the Ward Nine clinic's receiving file, where the slip for the fourteen cold boxes of the nineteenth had acquired a line in a fair hand that nobody in the clinic had written: **received, with a loss on the return, unsettled, carried forward.** The ward had received fourteen boxes on the nineteenth, which was true, and there was no return, which was also true, and neither of those facts was any use to anybody, and somebody had put them together in ink and the ink was not theirs.

The third was on the public lot book itself, and this was the one that made Mara sit down.

Page fourteen said, in Adrian's hand, dated the nineteenth: **SECURITY: a private undertaking by Adrian Vale, made in the room. Entry in the public book to follow the return term.**

Page nineteen said, in the same hand, dated the same morning: **SECURITY: none entered.**

Both were true at the time they were written, in the sense that both were true sentences about a book that did not have the entry in it. Both were in his handwriting. Both were dated the nineteenth. Neither had been struck. And the book now said two different things about the same charter on two different pages, with no entry between them recording that a change had been made, and there was nobody in the city who could say which page was the later one, because both of them had been written in the same ten minutes by a man who was in a hurry and had not thought about it.

"The deferral line," Mara said, "on the notice board at four in the morning. Is it in the book?"

"It is on the board," Nessa said. "It is not in the book."

"Then somebody has read a sentence that exists in exactly two places in this city," Mara said, "and put it on a third, and one of the two places it can be read is a man's inside pocket."

The room did the small noise.

"There is a fourth place," Adrian said.

Mara did not answer him.

"There are four places," she said. "I have the fourth one in my own coat and I have had it since the ninth, and it is the register of sealed pages, and this morning it says that three sealed sheets have been opened against Lot 17 since the ninth, and three is the true number, and I can produce all three, and two of the three are the escorts' sheets from the ninth and the third is the children's sheet from the sixteenth."

---

Nobody spoke for a while.

"Say the rest of it," Adrian said.

"The register is a list of the sheets, not the sheets. It carries a number, a date, and a category for every page I hold, and no name, because a name on that register is the harm. The original is in a municipal drawer in this building behind my own office door and I am the only person with a key, and what I read this morning is the fair copy, which a clerk makes in the ordinary way because the original is never carried out of that room." She put her hand flat on the crate. "Three rows. Two of them are dated the ninth and the third is dated the sixteenth, and those three rows are where I put them and none of the three has moved and every one of them is legible. One cell has changed and it is the only one. The children's row was the forty-third page I have ever sealed against this lot. This morning it is the forty-fourth, in the same hand, in the same ink, with the same date on it. And there is a forty-third in the register, dated the nineteenth, in the category of this lot, and there is no page, and there has never been a page, and the paper for it was never cut. I cannot tell you which of those two readings is correct: that a fourth sheet was sealed against this lot on the nineteenth and I have never held it, or that a page was numbered into my book by somebody who has stood in that room, and I am not going to stand here and tell this yard which of the two I believe, because I do not know, and a registrar who guesses in public is worse than a registrar who says so."

"And you cannot rule out that it was your page," Nessa Pell said, not unkindly, and said it out loud, which was the point.

"I cannot rule out that it was my page," Mara said. "I have had this in my coat for twelve years and I have never had a second key, and I was asleep for five of the last nine hours, and I want the following entered in the public book in my own words, because I am the registrar and nobody is going to say it for me: *the sealed-page register is one of the four places the change landed, and the registrar cannot certify her own instrument, and the change may have come from the page, and the registrar says so in public before anybody else says it for her.*"

"You have made yourself part of it," Adrian said.

"I have made myself *checkable*," Mara said. "Those are not the same and you of all men in this city ought to know it, and if you have got an objection to being checkable then I would like to know what to call it, because the word I would use for it is in your own undertaking on page eleven."

---

Selik Marne's Warden came at eleven with the midnight note in a Warden's copy and read the four lines of it aloud, and then said the sentence the yard had been waiting for since four in the morning.

"Mr. Vale. Line four of that notice says a security was offered. It also says the entry into the public book was deferred to a return term that has not been written. That is not a security anybody can look at, and you said in this yard on the nineteenth that it was real, and a real thing that four hundred people cannot inspect is not a security. It is a story about a security, and a story is cheaper than a press, and this yard has been sold a great many stories about a brass token for eleven days."

"Write it as your own," Adrian said. "That is your own opinion and it is right and I would rather it were on the board than in a corridor."

"It is not mine alone," the Warden said, speaking for himself for once, and his name was Culler Bray and he was eleven years on the bridge and he had carried a girl with a fever down a ramp in March. "Mr. Marne said the same thing to me at two o'clock this morning with a different word in it and the word was *hazard*, and a hazard is a thing you cannot account for, and you cannot run a manifest you cannot account for, and I will put his word in as well as mine because the two of them are the same word and he would not thank me for softening it."

"Entered," Nessa Pell said, "as the opinion of Warden Culler Bray and Warden Marne, and attributed, and not entered as a finding of this board, because this board does not audit a claim and says so."

Then Adrian Vale put both hands on the crate and asked for the line to come off the wall.

"I want it struck," he said. "I want the notice board to have a line through the four lines at four in the morning, dated, with my name against the strike, and I want the strike entered in the book, and I want it said in the yard that the security was never real."

"No," said Mara Quill.

"You said the board can record. Record that it is struck."

"The board can record what happened," Mara said. "It cannot record that a thing did not happen, because the registrar of this lot did not have the line in the book in the first place, so there is no book to contradict, and if I write *struck* on that board then this morning becomes a morning on which Mr. Vale removed a public notice from a public wall, and by Friday it will be a morning on which Mr. Vale removed a public notice about a failed charter, and by next month it will be a habit, and you will not be able to hear yourself explain the difference between the two."

"Read all four seals aloud," Adrian said to Nessa. "The last time anything came off this wall somebody was angry and I want the yard to know who is holding the stamp this time."

Nessa read all four. Four people agreed, which she had not been sure of, and she wrote it as four, and the yard made the noise it made.

---

"Registrar's proposal," Mara Quill said, "and it is the worst proposal I have ever put in front of a board, and I am putting it there because I have run out of better ones."

She put a clean sheet on the crate.

"The private undertaking goes into the public book. In full. His figures, not mine: one inspection press at three hundred and eighty by ward market; a repair-crew term of one season with three named hands; four hundred and ten plus a season. Entered as **offered and not verified**, and not entered as security, and not entered as posted, because he is a reader and he cannot post security, and this book has said *available, not posted* about his press and his crew since the second crossing, and I am not going to stand here and let a later clerk read a payment as a posting. And the entry says that anybody in this yard may go to the workshop and look at the press, and count the men, and price the season, and set their own figure, and put that figure in the book beside his."

"And what does the entry do for the people who cannot walk to the workshop?" Marda Osk asked from her crate.

"It does them nothing," Mara said. "That is the point of it. It puts a number in a public book and then hands the valuation to four hundred people, and it is the first piece of public machinery I have ever proposed that is *weaker* than what we had, and I am proposing it because the thing we had was a secret in a man's coat and a secret is only ever as good as the man holding it, and he has already been shown what he does with one."

"Seconded," said Sabin Tull, from the corner, and nobody had asked him.

Mara looked at him.

"I am a barrow-hauler," Sabin Tull said. "I have carried goods for six years against a piece of paper that said what the paper was worth and the paper was always wrong, and I am seconding a proposal that says *go and price it yourself*, because that is the first thing anybody has ever put in a book in this city that asks me for a job instead of asking me for a signature."

Four seats, four voices, one seconding, and a man in a wet coat who had been in the yard at four in the morning and had not put a hand on the wall.

It was carried.

And then Nessa Pell opened the case, because somebody at the rail asked whether the object had anything to say about the four documents that had changed overnight, and a public lot that has a question put to it in front of four hundred people does not get to keep the question shut.

Adrian put two fingers on the brass, and the writing came thin, and then it did not come thin, which it had not done to him in three weeks, and it came in four lines.

```text
THE LAST FARE
RETURN: NO CONDITION IN THE RECORD.
TOLL ON THE RETURN: NOT PAID.
NEARBY ACTIVE RECORDS: CHANGED.
```

He read it out for the board, because the board was the thing that had to carry it, and then he had to sit down, because the echo came up through the floor of him and took an hour he did not get back, and Nessa Pell wrote the time to the minute and the hour into the book, as she had been writing them into the book all week.

"Not disputed," Mara said. "The object states the return was not paid and states that records near the lot were changed. It does not say which, it does not say how, and it does not say it was to blame. The registrar is going to read that last part out because the room will want it: the object has not claimed to have done anything, and the room should not let it be talked into a culprit this afternoon, whatever it is feeling."

"It is the fifth time it has said something nobody asked," Adrian said, from the stool.

"It is the fifth time, and I have written all five down, and I have read four of them as answers to questions Mr. Vale did not put aloud, and I would like the board to note that I do not think that is a coincidence and I would like it equally noted that I cannot prove it and am not going to." She closed the case. "The book is open, the reading stands, and the object has said the only true thing anybody in this yard has said all morning, which is that somebody is owed a toll and it has not been paid. Everything else in this building is an argument about who pays it."

---

The hearing was held at eleven, in the yard, in the rain, and it took fifty minutes, and it is the part of that morning nobody in the book is going to be able to skip.

Mara Quill did not open the sealed page. She had said six days ago, in a yard, in front of eight children and two stewards, that she would not, and she did not, and the reason she gave was one word — *because* — and then she asked the affected people what they could say, in a room, in front of witnesses, which is the whole of the remedy and the whole of its limit.

The escort steward who had come off the bus could not say anything about the twentieth of the month, because she was on a cart on the nineteenth and asleep in a depot. The six children were in the school gym and two of them were asleep. Dessa Rill said four sentences and three of them were about a route, and the fourth was that she had been the one who drove the medicine for nineteen years and could not say why.

And the parent of one of the six children stood up in the rain and said, in a flat voice nobody in the yard had heard before: "If you had asked her, on the nineteenth, before she got on a cart, would she have told you she was going to die?"

"Cannot tell you," Mara said. "And I will not guess, and I will not let this room guess, and I would like the book to say that I was asked a question I could not answer, in public, by somebody with a right to ask it."

"Then the remedy is not discharged," said Nessa Pell.

"The remedy is not discharged," Mara said. "It was called at eleven o'clock this morning on a child of the second shelter group who is not named in this book, and it was not discharged, and the reason is that the category which keeps her name out of this book is the reason no remedy can point at her, and I have written that in one entry and I am going to write one more thing in the same entry, because I did the same thing in March and I am the only person in this building who will remember it if I do not write it down."

She wrote:

**The false alarm of the twelfth and the evacuation of the twelfth, when the span reported a movement it could not explain and one hundred and sixty people came off a sound deck and a woman with a feverish child was turned back down a ramp, are entered in the same book as this hearing, on the same page, in the same hand, and neither was a mistake and one of them was unnecessary and nobody who was on that deck has been told it was unnecessary. The spill of the twenty-first is entered beside them for the same reason: because a book that holds a correction and a failure and a cost in one entry is the only kind of book in which a reader can tell which of them was luck, and a book that separates them is a book that lets a clerk call one of them bad luck.**

"Read that back," she said, "and if it is too long for the page, read it anyway."

"Too long for the page," said Nessa Pell, and read it anyway.

---

And then they priced it, in the yard, in the rain, in the open, for the better part of an hour, and Adrian Vale stood on the rail and watched four hundred people find out what his workshop was worth.

The press was real. He was made to prove it and he proved it, by describing the frame, the flywheel, the throat, the depth of the screw, the leak in the top left corner of the bed that he had wanted fixed for two years, and a cooper's apprentice who understood tools said the *leak* was worth about nine coppers against him, and Adrian agreed with him in front of everybody, and wrote the nine coppers down.

The season was priced by the cooperative clerk, who had the ward rate sheet in an oilskin and did it twice and would not be hurried: three hands, one season, wheel-shop and repair rate, two hundred and fifty-eight coppers, and she said twice, and would not be hurried, and made him write down that she had not been hurried.

Three hundred and eighty, less nine, plus two hundred and fifty-eight. Six hundred and twenty-nine.

Nobody said anything for about four seconds, which is about four years.

"That is the whole of it," said a barrow-hauler near the door, in a voice nobody had heard before and everybody heard. "Six hundred and twenty-nine coppers. I have a barrow with a bad wheel and I have a boy who is nine, and if a man came to me today and put six hundred and twenty-nine coppers on the table and said *this is what is standing behind the road*, I would take it, and I would thank him, and I would never once in my life look at the press."

"It is real," Adrian said.

"It is *small*," the man said, and Adrian had no argument whatever, because the man was the yard.

And Nessa Pell read the entry aloud, all of it, twice, slowly, the way she read tolls.

"Six hundred and twenty-nine coppers, priced by the ward market and by the ward market's rate sheet and by a man in this yard who has handled a press before, entered in the public book as offered and not verified, with the nine coppers and the two hundred and fifty-eight entered beside his four hundred and ten, because a book that carries only the seller's figure is not a book, it is a hand."

She got to the end and read the last line of it, which was Mara's, written before the yard had priced anything, and which had been waiting three hours to be said out loud.

"*The claim this security was against has not been stated, because the charter that failed stated no return, and the person who cannot be named for a remedy is the reason there is no figure. Entered: a real security of six hundred and twenty-nine coppers against an unstated claim, and no person in this book is able to say whether it is enough, and neither is the man who wrote it.*"

Not one of the four hundred said anything at all.

And then a man on the tram rail, in a wet coat, with a bad shoulder and a season's work behind him, said the sentence that the whole morning had actually been about, and said it to Adrian, and Adrian agreed with every word of it.

"There is no security in the book," the repair-crew foreman said. "Now there is a figure in the book. And Mr. Vale, those are not the same word, and I want to know which of the two my brother is supposed to have been owed on Tuesday."
