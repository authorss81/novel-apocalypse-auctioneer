# Chapter 30: A Witness Who Is Not Named

The wall in Bett Ruhl's kitchen exchange had four documents on it by the tenth of the month, and Bett had put the last two up herself without asking anybody, which is the reason anybody trusted the first two.

Mara Quill's seven columns, in charcoal, copied in a careful hand by a woman who could not read and had copied the shapes, and then corrected by four people who could, in a night, in a quarrel. Then the three lines somebody had added, which had been on that wall since the fourteenth and were not going anywhere: **WHO CAN SAY NO / WHO PAYS IF IT FAILS / WHO ANSWERS WHEN NOBODY CAN BE FOUND.**

Then the fourth page, printed on the back of ward market claim stock, taped up in four pieces because it is a large sheet and the wall is not.

And then Warden Marne's order, of the ninth, copied out in charcoal in nine lines, with the date and the motion and the dissent of the ward market appointee, because a thing that is not on this wall does not exist in this kitchen, and Bett Ruhl had been copying other people's official notices onto her own wall since before the Flood and had never once been asked to stop.

Lina Vale stood in front of the four of them with her hands in the small pockets of a coat that had thirty-one keys in it, and did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was the whole of her morning.

Nineteen people on the far bank. The official span is shut to everything, including a box, and the Wardens will put one marshal and one lamp at the south end of the footbridge in the passable hours and will not put a name in a book about it, and he has told the yard why: a crossing without a manifest is not a crossing. The river stairs are a stair and a stair takes one person at a time in daylight and is a three-hour thing each way. The footbridge is an old thing with a plank deck and a handrail on one side, over a cut, and it is passable in about four hours a day.

Five on the deck is what a marshal can see with a lamp and a rope, and a crossing is twenty-two minutes, and there are four usable hours a day. Nineteen is five crossings and a spare, and the nineteen are not all in one place: four are in a receiving ramp, nine are in a signal hut a mile and a half up the line from the ramp, four children and two stewards are in a far school house four hundred yards the other way from the ramp.

So: the ramp and the signal hut on the first day, three crossings between them, because the signal hut is a mile and a half up the line and the daylight has to be spent getting people to the south end as well as putting them over. And the school house on the second day, two crossings, the two stewards first with the roll, because a roll that crosses after its children is a roll that crosses after them, and because the children cannot be moved in the dark; there is nowhere to have four children in a school house at midnight who have been on the far bank for three weeks and cannot say why they were sent.

Two days. And then the thing that made Lina put both hands flat on the flour crates: there is no roll, and there is not going to be a roll, and she is not going to get one, and without a roll there is no way on this earth to say that nineteen people arrived, except by counting them, and a count of nineteen is not a record. It is a number somebody said.

---

"Mr. Quill's category will not do this," Lina said, to the room, and the room was nine people and two of them were not hers. "And before anybody defends it, I want to say that I helped write it, so I know what it says. It says a group, a count, a steward, a destination, and a remedy, and a paper note of who chose to take part held outside the book by people the house chooses, and a sealed page for anybody who has to be identified. It carries a *passage*. It is very good at a passage. It was built for a bus that was supposed to come back."

"It came back," said Wenna Keel, from the wall. "Nine of them came back in a yard. It came back with nobody in it."

"It came back because a man stood in front of a bus and made it stop, and that is not the category and it is not going to happen twice." Lina turned round. "I have been sitting here for two days trying to work out how to bring nineteen people over a footbridge in daylight without putting a household in a book, and I have got it, and it is not hard, and I want everybody in this room to hear the whole of it before they agree to it, because the whole of it is worse than I would like."

---

The design took her twenty minutes and it went on the wall in charcoal afterwards and it is in Lot 17's book in the same words.

"A count. Nineteen, and the count is split the way it actually is: four from a receiving ramp, nine from a signal hut, six from a far school house, four of the six being children and two being stewards. The count is published. The count is the accountable thing. A number that anybody can check by looking at the far end of a bridge is worth more than a name that nobody can check.

"A destination. Lot 17, the near apron, and the school gym, and the school shelter's own door, and the far end of the signal line. All on this side of the water, written down so that nobody has to be asked where they are going.

"A remedy. If somebody stops on the bridge, a rope goes over from the north rail and two people go down after them, and the rope lives in this kitchen. If somebody does not arrive at the far end of a crossing, their group count does not close, and the count that does not close is published in the morning, in Lot 17's book and on this wall, and the closing of it is what says they got across.

"A steward. Me. I will put my name in the public book, which I have not done in eleven years and which I am doing now, and I would like the room to understand that that is not a small thing and that it is the entire point, and I will come back to it.

"And the witnesses." She stopped there, and she looked round the room, and she did not put the next part up straight away. "A witness in the book is a person a court can ask. What we have is different and I want it called by its own name. Each person who hands a group over signs a strip. On the strip goes the count, the place, the date, and a mark. Not a name. A mark. The strip is torn in half. The half with the writing goes in the jar under the third barrel. The other half goes into the hand of the person who is taking the group across, and when the group arrives, the two halves are matched, in this room, out loud, in front of anybody, and the two numbers are read against each other.

"So if nineteen do not arrive, then somebody in this room holds nineteen halves and there is no way on this earth to find out who, and the record that says so is honest, and the record that says *Bett Ruhl's household did not deliver* is the record that gets us all put down here inside a month."

"And the child," said Bett Ruhl.

"Yes," Lina said. "That is the part I have not got, and I have been not getting it for two days, and I would rather say it out loud in a kitchen than write it neatly on a wall."

She put her hand on the flour crates.

"On the twentieth of last month a child of the second shelter group died, and she has no name in any public record in this city, and the reason she has no name is that nobody asked her and the category that keeps her name out of the books is the reason no remedy can ever point at her. That has been true for three weeks and I have not been able to say it in a room.

"So here is the thing this design cannot do and I will not pretend it can. In eleven years of this city, a protected category has carried a *passage* twice and a *loss* once, and the loss is the one that matters, and the only thing we can do about it is put a number in a book and send the relief to the household that chose to receive it, and the number does not have her in it, and in ten years nobody will be able to check whether the number was right. Not one of you in this room. Me. Nobody. I am not going to tell you that the channel fixes that, because the channel is the reason the check is impossible, and both of those are the same fact."

Nobody in the kitchen said anything, and outside it started to rain on the tram line, which it did most days in the last year of the world before the Fracture, and which nobody under forty in Ward Nine had any memory of, and which everybody over forty had.

---

"You did not answer the cost," said a man called Colm Vaun, who was a carter off the river stairs and forty-one and had been in the room for two hours without speaking.

"No," Lina said. "You are right and I have been not answering it since I got here, so I will answer it now and I will answer it in a list, because a list can be written down and a feeling cannot, and I know which one of us is believed in this city.

"It is nine people, two hours a day, for two days. It is one coil of rope, which is Bett's and which is worth eleven coppers and is not coming back if the water is in the cut, which it will be. It is two lamps, one of which is Marda Osk's and she has already given it to this and does not want it back and nobody is going to tell her she can have it back. It is Bett Ruhl's floor and Bett Ruhl's stove for two nights and nineteen people fed, which is a pot and half and about four coppers of flour and the whole of what is under the fourth barrel, and I have asked her and she has said yes and I have written the four coppers of flour on the wall, which is the first time in eleven years there has been a price on this wall for anything, and it is four coppers, and that is the cost of a protected category, and it is not a secret and it is not free and it is not going to be paid by anybody else.

"And the last one, which is the one that matters. If this goes wrong, the first thing that will happen is that somebody from an office will come to this kitchen and ask who the steward is, and they will ask in a reasonable way, and I will not be able to say no without losing the channel, because a channel nobody can find is a rumour. So the answer is that I put my name in the public book. I will be the steward. I will be in Lot 17's book in a hand anybody can read, with a count and a destination and a remedy attached to it, and anybody who wants to ask me anything can come to the tram arch and ask me in front of witnesses.

"That is the cost and it is mine and I have decided to pay it, and I have decided to pay it because the alternative is that this stays a thing that works in kitchens and is worth nothing the day it is needed, and I would rather be a name that can be asked questions than a rumour that protects nobody. I have been an unrecorded person for eleven years and it has been the best thing about my life and it has also meant that on the night of the nineteenth, when nine people were on a bus that did not come in, there was nobody in any book in this city who could be sent to look for them, and that is a thing I have had to sit with for three weeks and it is not about the closed category. It is about me."

Lina Vale put her hand flat on the flour crates, and did not put her name on anything, and the room let her have that.

---

"Before we agree," Bett Ruhl said, "the second time. I am not doing it a third time, and not a fourth, and I would like that said on the wall in the same night as the rest of it, because I have been doing this since the year before the flood and nobody has ever written down when it stops being free."

"What are you saying," said Bett Ruhl, from the stair door.

"I am saying I will do it and I want it recorded that I said yes, and I want it recorded that I said what it costs, and I want it recorded that one person in this room has said no."

Every head in the kitchen turned.

Colm Vaun was standing by the stair with his coat half on.

"I have a household," he said. "I have got a wife and a girl of eleven who is the reason I have not crossed to the far side in three years. And I said yes to the two nights and the four hours and I am now saying no to the strips."

"Colm—"

"I am saying no to the strips, and I am saying yes to the ropes and the counting and the two days, and I am saying no to putting a mark on a paper that goes under your third barrel, because I have read what the boy printed and I know that a mark is not nothing if somebody comes with a warrant, and I am not a brave man, I am a carter with a bad knee and a wife, and I have watched what happens to a household in this city when a piece of paper exists." He pulled his coat the rest of the way on. "And I will not argue with you about it, and I do not want to be argued with, and if you need a fourth pair of hands on the rope I will be on the rope."

Nobody argued with him. It took eleven minutes to fix the design around the hole, and the fix was Bett Ruhl's and it was better than what Lina had brought.

"Every group of four is written on one strip," Bett said. "One strip, not four. Three of us write the count and the place, and the fourth person who is in the group does not touch it, and the group's own half goes to whoever is walking them across and comes back matched to ours, and the person in the group is not a party to it and is not obliged by it and if they never see the paper then there is no paper about them at all. And the three of us, we are not this kitchen. Marda Osk at the tram arch. Tove Alard at the table. And Wenna Keel, who has wanted her name written down in a public place since the fourteenth and can count nineteen heads in a street without a slate."

"That is three, and I am the fourth and I do not sign, because if I sign then I am a party and the steward should be the one who is a party and the one who can be asked," said Lina.

"Four," Bett Ruhl said. "I will write your name on the wall and you will not sign the strips, and that is the last thing I have to say about it and I am going to bed."

---

They walked it on the eleventh and the twelfth, and it went exactly as slowly as it had been predicted to, and it is worth setting down what it cost, because the cost is the reason it was done twice and not once and will be done twice and not once every time from now on.

On the first day, the four from the receiving ramp came over at about eleven in the morning and took twenty-two minutes, and one of the four clinic hands could not manage the third plank and went across on a rope with two people on it, and the marshal at the south end wrote nothing down because he had been told to write nothing down, and the strip came back matched.

On the first day, the nine from the signal hut came over after the signal hand had walked them down the line, in two lots, of five and four, and the second lot went at half past three with forty minutes of light left, and the marshal said afterwards that the correct decision was to have left them, and the correct decision was made by a man with a lamp and forty minutes, and not by a book.

On the second day, the two escort stewards went at ten in the morning with the school roll folded inside a coat and nothing else, because there was a rule about a roll and one steward had made it up, and they were across in nineteen minutes.

The four children came over at midday, with the steward who came back for them, and they came down the far bank of the cut in a line holding the rail on the good side, and they came down the stairs of the near bank holding each other, and not one of them could say why they had been sent, and nobody asked, because there was nobody in any position to ask them, and that is what the toll is and it is permanent and it does not wear off.

Colm Vaun was on the rope four times and did not have to be on it once.

And on the second afternoon, on the fifth and last crossing, with four children on the deck, the middle plank went.

It went the way a plank goes, which is not dramatically. It tilted, it took a corner of the handrail with it, and the four children went down onto the stringer and stopped, six feet above nine feet of water, and the steward who had come back for them went down after them with a hand on the rail and no rope at all, and Colm Vaun and two of the exchange's people had the rope over in about eleven seconds because they had practised it on the south bank for an hour the evening before, which was the only piece of preparation in the entire two days that was any use at all.

The plank is in the cut. It is nine feet down and it will be there until the water drops in the spring, and nobody is going to fish a plank out of a place where the ground has been arguing with itself for seventeen years.

So the footbridge is shut. Which means the far bank of St. Orra has no crossing of any kind on it, official or otherwise, and nineteen people came home from it on the twelfth of the month and it went back to being cut off that afternoon, and the difference is that this time everybody on both banks knows precisely what the price of the walk is and has written it on a wall in a kitchen, and a plank is in the water to prove it.

---

Adrian Vale came to the exchange on the thirteenth of the morning, which nobody had asked him to do and which everybody in the kitchen had expected for two weeks.

He stood in the doorway and did not come past the third barrel, which Lina noticed, and which is the most tactful thing he has ever done in that room.

"Mr. Quill reads the public lot book out loud in this room on Thursday," Nessa Pell had said, at the gate, on the Tuesday, in her fair hand, on a slip. "The whole of it for the month. She said a room ought to be able to hear a book in full once and the room is the one that is not in it. Any person may come. No name is taken at the door."

He said: "I am the reason there is a book, so I will come, and I will stand where I can be seen, and I will not touch anything."

"Sit on the flour crates," said Bett Ruhl. "That is what they are for."

---

Mara Quill read the public lot book of Lot 17 out loud in Bett Ruhl's kitchen exchange on the afternoon of the fifteenth, in full, from the ninth to the fifteenth, in a voice she had to stop four times.

Nessa Pell had copied the passage of the nineteen into the book in a separate hand, as a public entry, with the four elements and the strips, and the strip-counts, and the matched halves, and the cost — a coil of rope at eleven coppers, two lamps, two nights of a floor and a stove, four coppers of flour, thirty-six hours of nine people's time, and one plank — and at the end of it, the disposition of the plank, and the fact that the footbridge is shut and the far bank is cut off again.

And Lina Vale's name, in the public book, under **STEWARD**, in a hand anybody could read, with a count and a destination and a remedy attached to it, and nothing else.

Somewhere in the middle of the reading, in the entry about the passage of the nineteen, Bett Ruhl's household got counted. Not by name. It got counted — the household under the third flour barrel, the one that took in a signal hand struck off the rolls in year fourteen — appeared in the book as *household, four, giving and receiving, one member absent from the municipal record since year fourteen and by choice*, which is a sentence that gives the whole of a household's story away to anybody who knows the district and tells it to nobody.

Bett Ruhl heard it. Lina watched her hear it.

Nobody looked at her. That was the thing Lina would remember: that eleven people, in a kitchen, heard a sentence about a household that they all knew, and eleven people did not look at her, and that this had taken two years of Tuesdays and a great many arguments with a man called Orren Pike, and that it had cost nothing at all, and that a protected category which means something is a thing somebody has had to *keep* meaning for nine years, and that nobody anywhere has ever written down who paid for that.

She looked at Bett Ruhl and Bett Ruhl looked back and neither of them said anything, and after a moment Bett Ruhl put her hand flat on the barrel, once, and took it away.

---

Mara Quill closed the book at about five and said that was the whole of it, and then said, "There is one more thing, and it is not in the book, and Mr. Vale has asked to read it, and I am going to allow it because the man is a reader and it is his instrument and he will not be quiet about it."

Adrian had his hand on the book. He had put two fingers on the open page where the passage of the nineteen was written, and the writing came, and it was three lines and it was very short, and after it there was a gap, and the gap was the size of the gap that always came after.

He had not touched the brass. He was two streets and one street and four streets away from a case on a table in a depot, and what he was touching was a page of a public record in which nineteen people had been accounted for by hand, and a record that names a use is a thing a reader can read, and that is the oldest and least comfortable part of what he is, and it is why Nessa Pell has made him write down every reading he has ever taken and what it cost and where the brass was at the time.

"That is all of it," he said, and his hand was doing the thing it did, and the sound went flat at the edge, and Mara put the book down and made him sit down.

"Noted," she said. "And I am going to say what it means, and I want it understood that I am not sure, and I am saying it anyway because a book that waits for certainty is a drawer.

"The object has declined to certify the passage. Nineteen people went over a footbridge in daylight in five crossings and no charter and no token and no manifest, and the thing that reads charter has said, in effect, that none of it happened on its account. That is the correct answer. That is the *right* answer, and it took eleven years and a woman with thirty-one keys to arrive at it.

"And the third line is the whole of the arrangement in five words. Accounted for by hand. Not by the object, not by a seal, not by a roll — by hand, by a person with a mark on a strip, matched in a kitchen, out loud, in front of anybody. It is the worst system in the world and it is the only one that does not have a lid on it, and I am going to write that in the book, and I cannot tell anybody whether it will still be there in ten years."

"You will not know," Lina said, from the wall. "And neither will I. And that is the limit, and I would like it written next to the entry, and I would like the person who writes it to be somebody who is not tired."

Mara Quill looked at her.

"I am tired," Mara said.

"I know you are," Lina said. "You have been tired since the nineteenth and nobody in this room has said so out loud because you are the registrar and a registrar who is tired is still a registrar, and it has cost you about four things this month and I have watched three of them. So: the entry for the fifteenth is read and it is accepted, and it is a good entry, and it is thinner than the one you would have written on the eighteenth, and there is a name missing from it, and I will not say whose, and I would like you to read it back tomorrow morning, in this room, before the table opens, with two people in the room who are not clerks."

"And if it is the same tomorrow?"

"Then it is the same tomorrow," Lina said, "and it gets read anyway, at the table, to anybody who wants it, out loud, in a room, and that is the price of a process that answers to people and not only to offices, and it is a cheaper price than what we have been paying, and I have done the sum and I would like it read back in front of the witness."

Mara Quill agreed to it in front of nine people, which is the first time in eight years she has agreed to anything in front of anybody who was not a clerk.

---

They came out into the street under the tram line at about half past five with the light going, because Bett Ruhl's front door opens onto a street and people go out into streets.

The rain had stopped. On the wall inside, lit by two lamps, there were four documents: a registrar's seven columns in charcoal; three lines somebody added in the fourteenth and which were never going away; the fourth page, printed on the back of ward market stock, in four taped pieces; and a copy of Warden Marne's order of the ninth in nine lines of charcoal, with the dissent of the ward market appointee entered underneath it in a slightly different hand, because Bett Ruhl copies dissents too.

Adrian Vale stood on the apron and looked at the two of them — the entry in the book on one wall, the order on the other — and said the thing that Lina had known he would say and had not wanted him to say and could not have stopped.

"They are both true," he said. "That is the thing I have not been able to get past for a month. A registry says a crossing is administratively possible without anybody's name, and a warden says a crossing is not a crossing without a roll, and both of those are right, and one of them is in a book that a stranger can read end to end and the other one is in a room with a table in it, and between them they have made it possible for nineteen people to be brought home and impossible to say afterwards who watched them do it.

"That is a Hearth Line," he said. "Not a road. A Hearth Line. It is administratively possible, and it is not dependable, and it is the first honest description of anything I have ever been part of, and I have spent seventeen years trying to build a thing I could put a number on and I have got a room and a wall and a rope, and I would not trade the room for the number, and I have got no idea at all how to get nineteen people across a river in a year, and I am going to say that out loud here, on a street, in front of witnesses, because I said a different thing on a rail three weeks ago and it cost nine people a child, and this is the sentence I have got instead."

Lina Vale stood on the tram apron with thirty-one keys in a coat and a steward's name in a public book, and did not congratulate him, and did not tell him she had hoped for it, and did not tell him what it had cost her, because he had not asked, and because he would have written the number down and thought he was owed something.

"Thursday, in the afternoon," she said, "and it will take the whole of it, because it is a month of a book and I am not doing it in an evening. Anybody can come, and the door is the one you have not come through, and if you come through that one again I will tell you to go round."

"I will go round," Adrian said.

He went round.
