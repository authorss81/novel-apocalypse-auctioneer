# Chapter 44: The Question Nobody Asked

Mara Quill had built a process for eight years and the process had one shape, and the shape was this: two people who could be described and found had to be got into a room and made to agree to something in front of each other.

That is what a closing is. She had built instruments for eight years on that shape and she had got very good at it. She had a sealed page that pointed. She had a register that pointed. She had a schedule with seven items in it that pointed at a route request, a toll, a bearer, a term, a remedy, two witnesses, and a way to refuse. Everything Mara Quill had ever built was a machine for producing the moment when two people say yes to the same sentence, and she was extremely good at it, and on the sixteenth of this month she discovered that the whole of it had a hole in the middle the shape of an object.

A charter's terms are agreed by people. The anchor is not a party. In eight years, in nine books, in something over four hundred entries, Mara Quill had never once written a clause that said *and the object was asked*.

She had gone to bed on the fifteenth with that and had lain there for a while.

---

**Orren Pike came to the yard at about half past seven in the morning on the sixteenth, which was before the yard was a yard, and he came because he had heard on the terrace on the fourteenth that there was a closing on the eighteenth and that the book had a hole in it.**

He was seventeen and he had been on the point of writing something down for four days and had not written it, which for Orren Pike is a medical condition.

"You have to do the thing my mother could not do," he said, without sitting down, "which is write the list of what is not in it."

"There is a list," Mara said. "It is in the book. It is the ninth thing that was not in the charter of the nineteenth and it has been read out in this yard by three people including me."

"That is a list of what was not in one charter." Orren put a folded sheet on the crate. "That is not the same list. I have done it. It took me two nights and it is eleven lines and nine of them are about people and two of them are about the thing.

"**Every charter this district has written since the ninth of last month has a use and a toll and a term and a release and an appeal path and a remedy and a way to refuse, and every one of them is a sentence about what two or more human beings agreed to do. And not one of them has ever once said a word to the brass.**

"Eleven lines. And the eleventh one is the only one that matters and it is the shortest thing I have ever written and I have been stealing records since I was thirteen.

"**There is no clause in which anybody asks the object whether it agrees.**"

Mara Quill looked at the sheet for a while and did not pick it up, and the reason she did not pick it up was that if she picked it up she would have to admit in a yard of four hundred people on the morning of a closing that a boy of seventeen had found the hole in her profession in two nights, and she had about nine hours to decide whether she was going to admit that or not.

"Orren," she said. "I am going to say the whole of it and you are going to tell me if any of it is wrong, and then I am going to go and do it, and I am not going to have time to be careful about how it looks."

"Go on."

"**A charter's terms are agreed by people and the object is not a party, and that has been true in this book since the ninth and nobody has ever been hurt by it, and I have built eight years of practice on the assumption that it is the correct shape.** And the assumption is correct. A thing cannot consent. A thing cannot be asked whether it agrees and be made to mean anything by the answer, because an answer from a thing is not a yes and not a no, it is a reading, and a reading is not consent, and I have spent eight years teaching this district exactly that and I am not going to stop now because I have had an interesting morning.

"**And there is no procedure for it.** There is no form. There is no item on the schedule. I have looked, because I looked between seven and half past seven this morning, in nine books, and the answer is in a sentence you have read out loud to me in a yard and it is *the object is not a person*."

"Then you have found the procedure," said Orren.

"No, I have found the reason there is not one. **The reason there is not one is that asking the object is not the same as asking a person, and a record kept of the object is a record nobody in this room is protected from.** And Fen Ambrose is on the far side of a river with a strip-book and a woman by a kettle who has said no to being asked, and there are four people in this district who have found out what a record does to a household, and every one of them found it out in public, and I am not going to be the woman who builds a second one of those and calls it a service."

---

**What Mara Quill did about it, at about nine in the morning on the sixteenth, with about four hundred people in the yard and the board seated with all four seats named aloud, was the thing that made the next three days possible and it took her eleven minutes to say and about four hours to survive.**

"The object is in a locked case in the office behind that door," she said, "in this lot's public custody, under a seven-day term renewed four times in public, and **nobody in this yard owns it and it belongs to nobody and no man in Alder Reach can be given it and no man in this yard can be made to take it.** That is what a public custody is. So the question is not *may he ask*. He may ask. He is the reader and it is his hand and the cost is his and I have no standing to stop him.

"**The question is what the question is going to be, and the answer is that we do not know yet, and we are going to find out here, in public, and the first thing we are going to do is write down what it may not be.**

"Because the failure mode of this is enormous and I want the yard to hear me say it plainly, because I am the person who is about to do it.

"**If we ask the brass a question and get an answer, that answer goes in a book. And this book is a book that people can be hurt with, and a woman with a basket has told this yard that in those words on the twelfth, and she was right, and Mara Quill conceded it in a yard of six hundred and said the book would have to be fat. And an answer from an object is the fattest possible thing to put in a book, because a person can refuse to be asked and an object cannot, and so the answer is the one entry in this lot's history that nobody in it can stop.**

"So we write the limit first. **Before the question. In front of you. In capitals, on the crate, and a clerk reads it, and if the limit is not in the book before the question is asked then the question does not go in and I will say so in this yard myself.**"

---

**And the yard built it, in public, over about four hours, and it is the first instrument in Lot 17's book that was constructed in a room rather than in a back office, and the reason it is first is that it is the only one of them that had nothing to be built out of.**

**THE FORM. A question is put aloud, in a yard, by a person standing, with the object out of its case on the crate in front of him, and it is put as a question and not as a command and not as an instruction and not as a summoning, and there is a form to that which is a matter of grammar and not of law, and the form is: the questioner says *I am asking you* and then says the question, and he does not say what he wants and he does not say what he will do with the answer, and if anybody in the yard cannot tell whether a sentence is a question or a command then the sentence is not one and the clerk reads it again.**

**THE LIMIT. Entered in the book before the question is asked, in the words of the registrar, and it may not be added to afterwards:**

**ONE QUESTION. ASKED ONCE. THE OBJECT IS NOT ASKED TO AGREE TO ANYTHING, TO ANYTHING, TO ANY PART OF ANYTHING. THE OBJECT IS NOT ASKED WHETHER IT WILL OPEN, OR WHETHER IT WILL OPEN AGAIN, OR WHAT IT COSTS, OR WHOSE FAULT ANYTHING IS. AND THERE IS NO SECOND QUESTION UNTIL THERE IS A NEW CLOSING, AND A NEW CLOSING MEANS A NEW CASE AND A NEW DAY AND A NEW SET OF WITNESSES, AND NOBODY MAY COME TO THE SAME CASE TWICE.**

**THE PUTTER. A person who holds no claim on the object, no claim on the case, and no right in the closing. The object is in public custody and cannot be sold and cannot be inherited. The person who asks gains nothing from the answer and will be asked in this yard what he expected, and he will say so before the question is asked.**

**THE WITNESSES. Two fresh, independent, not travelling on the lot, who have heard the whole of it including the limit, and who are on this bank and will be on this bank in a week and can be asked what they heard.**

Then the limit was read by Nessa Pell off the slate, and it was entered in the book, and Nessa Pell said the time, and the time was twenty past one in the afternoon, and about four hundred people watched a clerk write down the shape of the only question anybody in Alder Reach had ever permitted.

And the two fresh witnesses were taken then and there, and they were **Rutt**, who is about fifty and was a signal clerk before her shoulder went and who is the reader at the ward market counter four days a week and who stopped reading a published document aloud on the sixth of this month in front of nine people because four bearers were three people and a mark, and **Mrs Halder**, who is fifty-eight and is the ward market's rate clerk and who found an eleven-year paper levy in a back office that produced no printed matter and has never been thanked for it in her life.

Rutt said, "I accept the role, on the same condition I put on the sixth, which is that I do not have to pretend I have understood the ninth line, and this morning I did not understand about a third of what was said and I have written down the part I did not understand and I am reading it out before I accept, because that is what a reader is for."

And Mrs Halder said, "I accept, and I have been a clerk in a counting room for thirty-one years and I have never once been asked to witness a thing, and the entry is to say that I asked what happens if the answer is a lie."

"If it is a lie the book is wrong and the book is wrong in public," Mara said, "and that is the correct outcome and not a reason not to ask."

---

**And then the yard had to decide what the question was, and it took about two hours, and four hundred people proposed about eleven questions and every single one of them was about the road.**

*Will it open again. How many more times. What is the second toll. Whose fault was the bus. Is the bell post's arm still moved. Is there a man in the room at the water office with a door on it.*

Every one of those was refused, and the reasons were read out, and they are worth having because they are the reasons a city gives when it does not want an answer.

**Will it open again:** refused, because the limit says the object is not asked whether it will open.
**How many more times:** refused, same reason.
**What is the second toll:** refused, because the ninth line of the return term says a second payment is not a smaller thing and is not the same thing and is not stated, and asking the object would convert an open question into a closed one, and there are about thirty people in this district living inside the open one and they were not asked to have it closed.
**Whose fault:** refused, because the book says the means is not established, and a question about a person is a question about a person, and this lot does not put people in questions.
**Is the bell post's arm still moved:** refused, because that is a question about a place and there is a man in this district who has been asked one question about a place and a foot of a service cut went down.
**Is there a standing order with no expiry in a room on the second floor with a door on it:** refused, out loud, by Mara Quill, in front of four hundred people, in one sentence, and the sentence was: "**That question is about a document and not about the brass, and the document is either read or it is not, and I have asked for it four times and I am not going to go around it by asking a piece of metal.**"

And then somebody at the back said, "Then what is left?"

And Adrian Vale, who had been standing about eleven feet from the crate with his hands in his pockets since about ten o'clock in the morning, took them out.

---

"**One thing is left**," he said, "and it has been sitting in a public book since the twenty-sixth with no answer attached, and it is the only question anybody in this district has ever published on purpose, and a boy wrote it on a notice board in a school hand and a registrar published it because the board could not answer it and could only publish it.

"**Who tells four thousand households of this district, and by whom, and for how long, if the crossing never opens.**"

He stood there for about four seconds.

"That is not a question about the road. That is a question about the day after. And it is the only one on your list that the brass is the right party to, because a thing does not get tired of a question and a thing does not go home and a thing does not have a household to protect.

"And I am going to put it to the object exactly as it is written in the book, in the words the boy wrote, and I am not going to improve it, and I am not going to say what I want out of it, and I am going to say that now, before the question, because the limit says I have to.

"**I expect the object to tell us nothing.** That is what I expect. I have been a reader for seventeen years and six answers have come off it and every one of them was short and none of them was any use, and the sixth one was *the object was not asked* and it was the most accurate thing anybody has said about this lot in five weeks. **I do not expect a thing about the road. I expect the object to hand back a sentence, and the sentence to cost me about twenty minutes, and the sentence to be in a book that people can be hurt with, and that is to be minuted before I ask.**

"And I want to say the other half. I want the road. I have wanted the road for five weeks and I have said so out loud, and if you think a man who wants a road is the wrong man to ask it, then you have got about nine seconds to say so and I will stand down and somebody else can put it and I will read it out for them."

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds, and the tenth hand of the signal crew said, from the back, with his page in his hand:

"He is the only man in this city who reads things, and you are not going to get another one this week, and the limit is in the book and it is in capitals, and I have read it twice and there is nothing in it that lets an answer be used as a term. **Ask it.**"

---

The case was brought out at about two in the afternoon and put on the crate with the ledger it had been kept in, and Adrian Vale touched it, and it was warm from the office and it was cracked through the plate, and about four hundred people in a tram depot in Alder Reach watched a man ask a piece of brass a question.

He said: "**I am asking you.** Who tells four thousand households of this district, and by whom, and for how long, if the crossing never opens."

And the writing came, and it came the way it comes, in short lines, and it came for about nineteen minutes and Adrian Vale stood at the crate the whole time with his hand on the brass and his right hand open at his side, and at about nine minutes his head turned slightly, and at about thirteen he put his other hand on the edge of the crate.

```text
THE OBJECT WAS ASKED ONCE.
THE OBJECT IS NOT A PARTY AND WAS NOT ONE BEFORE THIS MORNING.
A REPLY GIVEN WITHOUT A QUESTION IS NOT A PROMISE. SIX WERE GIVEN TO THIS LOT. NONE OF THEM WAS A PROMISE.
WHAT IS OWED IS NOT OWED TO A MAN. IT IS OWED TO THE PEOPLE WHO PAID IT.
SAY IT IN THE ROOM WHERE THEY PAID AND NOT IN THE ROOM WHERE THE PAPER IS KEPT.
A TOLL PAID TWICE IS NOT A SMALLER TOLL AND IS NOT THE SAME TOLL AND IS NOT STATED BY THIS.
IF NOTHING ELSE IS SAYABLE, SAY THE THING THAT IS ALREADY IN THE BOOK.
```

Then it stopped, and Nessa Pell entered the reading without being asked, because she enters every reading and its cost: **a reading, the second entered in this month, nineteen minutes; the object was contacted and not the record; the reader is not to read again before the seventeenth.**

And then Adrian Vale had to sit down, on an upturned crate, in front of four hundred people, because for about an hour afterwards he could not tell whether a thing he was looking at was nearer to him than it had been or further away, and everything in a room at a distance was the wrong size, and Nessa Pell got him a glass of water and Bett Ruhl got her coat out of the rain and put it round his shoulders and did not say one word about it, and about four hundred people watched the man who asked the question sit down and did not make a sound about it.

---

**And then the yard did the thing that Mara Quill had spent the whole morning being afraid of, about ninety seconds after the answer stopped, and it took about eleven people and it took about four seconds each.**

Somebody said, "*None of them was a promise*."

Somebody else said, "**Six answers. Five weeks. And none of them was a promise.**"

And about a hundred people in that yard said, more or less, in the voice you use when you have been leaning on something and have just been told it is not there: *then what have we been doing.*

And Rell Bode, who had come from the river stairs with a basket and had been at the back for four hours, said the sentence that everybody in the yard was going to remember from that afternoon:

"**Then it was never leaning on it,**" she said. "That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I have been saying for a month that a book is a shop and shops sell what people will buy, and I have been right about that, and the yard is to notice that I was not right about *this.* A thing nobody was leaning on is a thing that cost us nothing to be wrong about."

---

**And the board's first act, about two minutes later, with all four seats named aloud, was to refuse to let it be a revelation, and the refusal is the correct one and it is also thin, and the book says so.**

**THE BOARD WILL NOT ENTER THE ANSWER AS A FINDING, AND WILL NOT ENTER IT AS A CONFESSION, AND WILL NOT ENTER IT AS ANYTHING THAT THE TERMS OF THIS LOT DEPEND UPON, AND THE REASON IS THIS:**

**A reply given without a question was never a promise and was never relied upon as one, and every one of the six answers in this book is entered as a reading and not as a term, and no charter in this book has ever been closed on the strength of an answer, and no person in this district has signed anything because the brass said so.**

**AND THE BOARD RECORDS, BECAUSE IT IS TRUE AND BECAUSE NOBODY ELSE WILL: THAT IS A DEFENCE, AND IT IS A GOOD ONE, AND IT IS THIN, AND THE DAY A PROCESS GOES SIX WEEKS ON SIX ANSWERS THAT WERE NEVER ASKED FOR IS A DAY THE ANSWERS LOOK LIKE THE FOUNDATIONS OF SOMETHING TO EVERYBODY EXCEPT THE PEOPLE WHO BUILT IT, AND THE BOARD IS FOUR PEOPLE AND IS THE ONLY INSTRUMENT IN THIS DISTRICT THAT KEEPS ITS OWN COUNTS.**

Then Sabin Tull said, "That is the best thing this board has ever written and I am the man who has stood on that crate every day of it, and I am going to say the rest of it, which is that it is also a very good way of not looking at it. **Reading is not a promise and we never leaned on it. Fine. And a boy of seventeen leaned on it, and Marda Osk leaned on it when she wrote the eighth line out of a counterfeit's second page, and I seconded it, and if that answer was not a promise then the second page of a forgery is not a promise either, and about two hundred people in this district have bought something this month on the strength of it.**"

Nobody had an answer to that and the board entered it as an objection with no answer, which is one of the things this board is for.

---

**And at about six in the evening, with about a hundred and forty people left in the yard, Mara Quill did the thing she had said at eleven o'clock in the morning she was going to do, and she did it in public, in her own hand, on the page, and it is the only entry in the history of this book that a registrar has entered about herself in a state.**

> **ENTERED BY THE REGISTRAR, ON THE SIXTEENTH, IN HER OWN HAND, AND IT IS NOT A TERM AND IT IS NOT A FINDING AND NOBODY IS TO USE IT:**
>
> **I have built this process for eight years and every part of it is an improvement on asking a person something, and this morning I watched four hundred people discover that there is a party in this lot that has never been asked anything, and I was afraid, and I am entering that I was afraid, and the reason is that an eight-year improvement is not a small thing to hand to a room in one afternoon, and the second reason is that a registrar who has discovered a hole in her own instrument is the correct state for a registrar to be in and it goes in the book so that nobody in four years can say she was calm about it.**
>
> **AND I AM SIGNING THIS RECORD AND I CANNOT CERTIFY IT IN ADVANCE. Every entry I have made in this book for eight years I could certify before I made it. This one I cannot. I am the person who recorded it. I am not the person who can say whether it is true.**

And Orren Pike, at the rail, with his sheet of eleven lines folded back in his pocket, said to nobody in particular and was overheard by about nine people, which is the entire constituency he has ever had:

"She said it was the eleventh line. It took me two nights and she did it in an afternoon. **I would like it noted that I was faster and that nobody has offered me a job.**"
