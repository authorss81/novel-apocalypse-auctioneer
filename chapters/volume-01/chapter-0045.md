# Chapter 45: The Staging Line

The St. Orra staging yard on the morning of the seventeenth was about half a mile of bad ground between the Lot 17 apron and the foot of the river wall, and Tarin Kest had walked it four times before six in the morning and had a fifth walk in him and a bad shoulder coming on in the cold, which is the shoulder that has been bad since a bridge lot in the fourth year rebuilt itself for eleven hours and a half and never once admitted the road was unsafe, and which he had told three people about in the last month and had not mentioned to anybody else.

There is no road across that yard. There is a line. The line is chalked at the near end and pegged at the far end and it is about four hundred yards long, and the reason it is four hundred yards long is that a bus coming down out of St. Orra at the angle the route actually comes down does not arrive where anybody drew it, and Tarin found that out on the nineteenth of last month with a lamp in his hand and eleven hours and forty minutes of somebody else's life, and he chalked the line where the bus arrives and not where anybody hoped.

He was at the near end at about ten past five with a pot of tea and a lamp and about forty people, and the rest of them came.

---

They came because a yard of five hundred had been promised a road.

That is the sentence that Tarin Kest said out loud to Nessa Pell at about six o'clock, twice, and which she entered both times, and which is the reason for most of what happened on the seventeenth.

"**A road is a thing people bring children to see.** That is all this is. Five weeks of paper and a month of argument and about nine hundred people in this district, and what the nine hundred people have come here for is to stand in a bad yard at six in the morning and look at a bus. And I am not saying that is stupid. I am saying that by the time I get to the far end of this line I am going to have four hundred people behind me and a child asleep on somebody's coat, and a child asleep on a coat does not know what a toll is, and if a thing goes wrong at the far end then the last thing anybody in this yard will be thinking about is the terms."

"Enter it," Nessa said.

"Enter it in my words and not in yours, because you would make it sound like an instrument and it is not one. It is a boy of about five who came with his aunt at a quarter to five in the morning because somebody in this district told his aunt there would be a bus today and **that is the best public notice anybody has written in this city in five weeks and it was written by a liar on a stair, and I have found no way to be angry with him, and I am going to find one and it is going to take me longer than today.**"

---

**The first thing on the line at about half past six in the morning was not a person. It was a page.**

The tenth hand of the signal crew was standing at the near end with his copied page and a lamp, doing what he has done since the nineteenth, which is checking every number anybody in this district has said out loud against a page he has kept since the nineteenth, and by the half past six he had most of them.

He had found four wrong ones since the nineteenth, three of them in wardens' documents and one of them in a public book, and he had said so in a yard of five hundred and had not enjoyed it and had not stopped, and he was on this bank and had been on this bank since the nineteenth and all nine of his men had been home over the footbridge on the eleventh and the twelfth and having them home had not made one number in this district checkable.

"This morning's numbers check," he said. "Twenty-six offered, twenty-four travelling, two declines, and the declines were said out loud in a yard and I heard them. Eight clinic hands, six of them carrying a box each, two of them carrying nothing. Fourteen off the school roll. One bearer, one remembering passenger. **Twenty-four. It adds and it is on my page and I have never had a page go this well in five weeks.**"

"You have written it down," Tarin said.

"I have written it down and I cannot put it on the page, and that is the finding and I have been standing here since half past five working out why."

He held it up so that the lamp caught it.

"Everything on this page is a number somebody **said out loud in a room.** That is the whole of what a page like this is. Five weeks of numbers said out loud in yards and at counters and in kitchens, and I can check any of them against any other, and that is how I found the three wrong ones in wardens' documents and the one in a public book.

"And this morning's number is not one of those. **Twenty-six with two noes in it.** Not said out loud — read out loud, by a clerk, from a slate, after two people said the word. And I have got a column for numbers people said and a column for numbers a clerk read and I have never had to think about the difference because for five weeks they were the same number, and this morning they are two different instruments, and a number that came out of a refusal is not the same kind of thing as a number that came out of a count.

"**So I have the first number in five weeks that I cannot put anywhere, and I am telling a yard of about four hundred people about it at six in the morning because if I do not say it today somebody will use it in a year and I would like it said by the man who found it.**"

---

**The second thing on the line at about a quarter to seven was the far-side condition, and it was not a thing Tarin Kest had written, and he had brought two sheets and one of them was not his.**

"A far-side condition," he said, to about ninety people who had come up the line with him in the half light, "is a sentence that says what the driver is allowed to do when the far side is not the road he left on. It is Mr. Marne's sentence, from the fifteenth, and it has been in this book since the ninth and it is the sentence that stopped a bus four streets from where it should have stopped and let eight people out alive, and it cost eleven hours and a child, and nobody in this yard is going to hear me say that again today because every one of us has heard it and it is in the book.

"And here is what has been sitting in that sentence for five weeks and what I did not see until about four o'clock yesterday afternoon, and the yard is to know that I did not see it sooner and did not say so for three weeks.

"**A far-side condition is a thing the driver is authorised to do. And on that bus yesterday there were twenty-four people and one of them was driving it.**

"So what is the other twenty-three authorised to do?"

Nobody answered for about eleven seconds, and a woman in the third row said, "Go home."

"Not one of them can go home," Tarin said. "**The memory of the purpose goes and the place and the skill stay, and that is the whole of what we know, and it means that twenty-four people got off a bus on the far bank of a river in the middle of the morning and could tell you the road and not one single one of them could tell you why they were on it. Twenty-four people who are somewhere and do not know why.**

"And I have been the only person in this district for five weeks with a sentence in a book that tells him what to do about that, and every other man on that bus was in it as a passenger. **A charter with one condition in it and twenty-three people who have no condition in it is a charter about one man.**"

---

**And Selik Marne was on the line at about a quarter to seven in the morning, because Selik Marne has been coming to this spur every morning for nine years and had no intention of stopping on the eighteenth because somebody is selling a road.**

He came with his own lamp and a roll of chalk, and he stood at the top of the slope where the twine is, and he did not go past the second rail, and Tarin did not ask him to and neither did anybody else, and about four hundred people in five weeks have learned that the twine is nine feet below the crown and eleven feet above the rail and that the rule is *nobody past the second rail, ever*.

"You are about to take my sentence and make it bigger," Selik said, "and I have had a fortnight to think about whether I am going to allow that and I have decided that I am, and I want to say the reasons before you ask me, and the first reason is that you are right, and the second reason is worse than being right."

"Go on."

"**I have spent nine years writing conditions for the man who drives.** Four terms, a manifest, a responsible party at the abutment, a certified housing, and one rule about a piece of ballast, and every one of them is addressed to a person who is doing a job. That is what a bridge is for. A bridge is a machine for telling the man at the wheel what he may do, and I have never in nine years written a word about the people in the seats, because on a bridge the people in the seats are not a problem, they are cargo, and cargo does not get a clause.

"And that is the whole difference between my crossing and yours, and it has taken me nine years and a road of yours to see it, and I am not going to be embarrassed about it, because I would write it the same way again on a bridge, and on a bridge I would be right.

"**So take it. And take it further than you have written it, and here is what I am going to add, and I am adding these myself and they are mine and I am not giving them away.**

"**One. A line-holder at the top of the service cut, on this bank, for the whole of the period, named in the book, who is a person and not a mark and not a slot, and who holds the line and does not leave it, and nobody goes past it.** And I want the name of that person in the book in a place where anybody can read it at two in the morning.

"**Two. The ballast is not ground.** Whoever is in charge of this line will say, out loud, to every person who is going to be on it, that the ballast is not ground and that a foot past the twine is a foot into a hole. And the saying is a duty and not a courtesy, and if somebody has been told and goes past the twine anyhow then the line-holder says so and the book says so, and it is a fact about a place and not a fact about a person.

"**Three. Nobody past the second rail.** Which is my fourth term of the nineteenth, and the flag is out of the flood year, and the twine is four years old, and the three of them are one rule, and it has been right for nine years, and it is now in Lot 17's terms as well as on my spur, and that is not me being generous, it is me saying that a rule that has never been argued with has not been tested, and I want it tested, and I want it tested against me."

Tarin Kest stood in the half light on a bad yard in Alder Reach and said: "You have to say one more thing and I am not going to let you off it."

"I am not going to be let off anything, Mr. Kest, I have never been let off anything in nine years, it is my entire career."

"**The rule was argued with on the twelfth of this month and it held, and that is in the minutes of a bridge review and I want it entered in Lot 17's book as well, because in two years somebody is going to say a rule that cannot be performed is not a rule, and the reason it is a rule is that it was argued with in public by a man with a page and it did not move.**"

Selik Marne looked at the tenth hand, who was standing about four feet away with the lamp and the page and had not said anything for about four minutes.

"You are the counter."

"I am the tenth hand. I am not one of the nine and I have not been one of the nine and I keep a page."

"**You asked me a question on the twelfth, in this district's yard, in front of about six hundred people, and you asked me whether a rule that has never cost anybody anything is a rule or a habit, and I answered you, and the answer is in the minutes of the review table, and the rule held, and the yard has heard me say the same thing twice this morning and it is entered that the man who argued with it has been standing on my line ever since and has not asked me for anything.**"

"I have been counting," said the tenth hand.

"Yes," said Selik Marne. "I know. It is written in the minutes of the review of the order of the ninth, and the market wrote it, and it says that the order is correct and that this district does not want it, and I have read it and I have not argued and I am not going to."

---

**The third thing on the line was a word, and the yard had been failing at it since the twenty-fourth of last month and it failed again on the seventeenth of this month in front of about four hundred people, and it failed in the way it always fails, which is politely.**

Somebody wanted the six clinic hands with the cold boxes on the bus first.

The reason given was urgent. The word used was *urgent*, and about nine people in that line agreed with it, and about eleven did not, and somebody said *scheduled*, and somebody else said *priority*, and a cooper said that a man with a six-hour clock does not have a priority, he has a **clock**, and a chandler said that was a distinction without a difference and that every man with a clock in this district had had one since the flood year, and for about six minutes the argument was about the word and nobody could remember which of them had said what they wanted.

It is institutional and not emotional. It is not anybody's bad temper. It is that a yard of six hundred people in Alder Reach cannot agree what to call a thing when there is not enough of it, and has never been able to, and the reason is that there is no word in the district's ordinary speech for *this one before that one because there is a reason and the reason is written down on a lid in chalk.*

"**Then I am not going to call it anything**," said Renn Fisk, who had been standing at the near end of the line with a clipboard and six boxes' worth of chalk marks on it and the bad left shoulder of the third year.

"The line has to be in an order—"

"**The line is in an order. I have just written it. And the order is that the six with a box go first because I set the clock at twenty past five this morning and it is six hours and forty minutes now and that is the whole of the reason and there is no word in it.**"

"You cannot put a number in a queue—"

"I have put a number in a queue and it is the first time a number has been in a queue in this district in my life and it took four months to get here and I am not going to call it urgent, because urgent means somebody important thinks so, and it is not important, it is a thermometer in a box, and it does not care who I am and neither should the line."

"And if somebody has a fever and no box?"

"Then they go on the line in the place the line puts them and I will say why out loud and the yard can hear the reason and if the yard does not like the reason then the yard is welcome to come and set a clock on a box and take it from me."

Nessa Pell entered it, and this is what is in the book:

> **THE ORDER OF THE LINE, THE SEVENTEENTH, BY RENN FISK, RESPONSIBLE PARTY AT THE NEAR ABUTMENT, WHO IS RESPONSIBLE FOR BOXES AND HAS NEVER BEEN RESPONSIBLE FOR PEOPLE AND WAS TOLD SO IN THIS YARD BY MR. KEST AND HAS ACCEPTED THE TELLING.**
>
> **THERE IS NO WORD FOR IT. THREE WORDS WERE PROPOSED IN THIS YARD AND NONE WAS ADOPTED AND ALL THREE ARE ENTERED WITH THE NUMBER OF PEOPLE WHO USED EACH: *URGENT*, ELEVEN; *SCHEDULED*, SEVEN; *PRIORITY*, FOUR. AND THE LINE IS IN THE ORDER THE RESPONSIBLE PARTY PUT IT IN, AND THE REASON GIVEN FOR THE ORDER IS A CLOCK AND NOT A WORD, AND THE CLOCK IS SIX HOURS AND FORTY MINUTES AND IT IS WRITTEN IN CHALK ON SIX LIDS.**
>
> **AND ENTERED BECAUSE IT IS TRUE: THIS DISTRICT HAS BEEN UNABLE TO AGREE THE WORD SINCE THE TWENTY-SIXTH OF LAST MONTH, AND THE WORD IS NOT THE PROBLEM, AND A TERRIFYING NUMBER OF WORDS WILL NOT FIX IT, AND THE ONLY THING THAT WORKED THIS MORNING WAS A PERSON WHO HAD A CLOCK AND SAID SO.**

---

**And then somebody asked Renn Fisk the question that has been waiting in the yard for five weeks, which is not about the line and not about the boxes and is about him.**

Mr. Kest asked it, at about ten past seven, with about three hundred people on a bad yard in the half light.

"Mr. Fisk. You are the responsible party at the near abutment. You hold the roll, you hold the refusal window, and you are the last person on this bank before the case opens. And I want to say the thing out loud on this line rather than at nine o'clock tomorrow in front of a crowd, and I am going to say it in front of three hundred people and not four hundred, because I would rather be slightly wrong about the number.

"**You have been responsible for boxes since the flood year and you have never been responsible for people, and tomorrow you are, and it is not the same job and nobody in this yard has told you that it is not the same job, and I am telling you now because I am the one who is going to be on the other side of a river.**

"A box does not argue with you. A box does not say the word. A box does not stand at the far end of a line of four hundred yards and decide it is not going to be on the bus, and the only two people in this district who have ever done that to a room of this size are in this yard and they are eleven and about fifteen and they are not mine to use.

"And tomorrow there is a name on a piece of paper at the far end, and I do not know who is on it, and I have asked, and I have been told that there is a sheet and that the sheet names a person and that the sheet only opens if the remedy is called, and that is a rule and the rule is right and I have read it and I am not arguing with it.

"**And I am going to say the whole of it anyway, at ten past seven in the morning, on a line, to three hundred people, and then I am going to shut up about it.**

"The rule says a page like that may name a responsible party who has to be identified and may not be published, and that it opens only if the remedy is called, and that when it does open the person it names keeps the right to refuse care, to challenge the closing, and to say so on the record.

"**Which means that tomorrow at ten o'clock in the morning, when we open that case, we are going to perform a use with a person on the far side of a river who has not been asked, and the only reason we are entitled to is a piece of paper, and the paper is not consent, and I know the paper is not consent, and every one of these three hundred people knows the paper is not consent.

"**And I have never in my life driven anywhere with a paper in the seat instead of a man.** And I am telling you the truth about it, and then I am going to drink my tea, because if I do not say it on this line at ten past seven in the morning among three hundred people, I will say it at nine o'clock tomorrow among nine hundred, and the nine hundred will be a crowd and the three hundred are a line."

Nobody had an answer. Tarin Kest drank his tea. And the book has this in it, in the entry, and nobody has ever taken it out:

> **SAID BY TARIN KEST AT TEN PAST SEVEN IN THE MORNING OF THE SEVENTEENTH, TO ABOUT THREE HUNDRED PEOPLE, AND ENTERED AT HIS INSISTENCE AND NOT AS A PART OF ANY TERM: THAT THE CHARTER WAS TO BE PERFORMED WITH A PERSON AT THE FAR END OF IT WHO HAD NOT BEEN ASKED, AND THAT THE ONLY REASON THEY WERE ENTITLED TO WAS A SHEET OF PAPER, AND THAT HE WOULD NOT DO IT QUIETLY.**

---

**The line was walked at about eight o'clock in the morning, and it took an hour and ten minutes, and the walk is four hundred yards.**

The child was there. He was asleep on a folded coat at the pegged end of the line, on his side, with one hand shut, and he had been there since about a quarter to five in the morning, and his aunt said that he had asked whether the bus was the thing that went over the water and she had said yes and had not known what else to say to a five-year-old at a quarter to five in the morning.

**And at the other end of the line, at the rail, in a wet coat, standing about nine feet apart and not speaking to each other, were the two people who had said the word in that yard the day before.**

A boy of about fifteen who had written a complaint on a notice board in a school hand and asked a question in a school gym that nobody has answered yet. And a girl of eleven who had said no twice, and been asked twice, and whose reason the first time was *nobody had explained the toll to her*.

Nessa Pell read the count out at the near end of the line at about a quarter to nine in the morning, in the voice she uses for tolls, with about four hundred people in the yard and the whole of the district's attention standing in bad ground in the rain.

> **THE COUNT, THE SEVENTEENTH OF THIS MONTH, READ AT THE NEAR END OF THE STAGING LINE BY NESSA PELL, CLERK AND SITTING STEWARD:**
>
> **OFFERED: TWENTY-SIX.**
>
> **TRAVELLING: TWENTY-FOUR. EIGHT CLINIC HANDS, SIX OF THEM CARRYING A BOX. FOURTEEN OFF THE SCHOOL ROLL. ONE BEARER. ONE REMEMBERING PASSENGER, WHO IS A CONDITION ON THE USE AND IS NOT A BEARER AND IS ENTERED AS A COUNT AND NOT AS A SENTENCE.**
>
> **DECLINED BY THE WORD, WITH NO REASON OWED AND NONE GIVEN: TWO, and both on the fifteenth of this month, at about five in the afternoon and about ten past five, in a yard, in front of about six hundred people, and neither of them gave a reason and neither was asked for one.**
>
> **THE FIRST OF THE TWO IS THE PERSON WHO ASKED, ON THE TWENTY-SIXTH OF LAST MONTH, WHO TELLS FOUR THOUSAND HOUSEHOLDS OF THIS DISTRICT, AND BY WHOM, AND FOR HOW LONG, IF THE CROSSING NEVER OPENS, AND THAT QUESTION IS STILL PUBLISHED WITH NO ANSWER ATTACHED. THE SECOND IS THE PERSON WHO WAS OFFERED A PLACE ON THE NINETEENTH OF LAST MONTH AND REFUSED IT, AND THE ENTRY OF THE NINETEENTH SAYS *NOBODY HAD EXPLAINED THE TOLL TO HER*, AND SHE HAS NOW BEEN ASKED TWICE. NEITHER DECLINE IS STRUCK. NEITHER IS A HOLE.**
>
> **AND THE NUMBER THAT HAS NEVER BEEN SAID ALOUD IN A YARD IN THIS CITY IS TWENTY-SIX. Not the four hundred, which is a crowd. Not the twenty-four, which is arithmetic. **Twenty-six, with two noes in it, read out of a slate by a clerk, in a yard, in public, and nobody in this district has ever had a number with a person saying no inside it that they were all told about at the same time.**

---

**The refusal window stayed open through the seventeenth, all day, in the rain, with about four hundred people in and out of a tram depot and two crates and a pot of tea, and it closed at eleven minutes past nine on the eighteenth of this month, and it closed because a case was physically opened and not one minute before.**

Nessa Pell had written the time on the board on the sixteenth night, in her fair hand, and it was the first time in the history of this lot that the hour of the closing of a refusal window had been published in advance, and about forty people had come and looked at the board to see it, and about nine hundred had heard of it.

And a girl of eleven and a boy of about fifteen stood at the rail on the afternoon of the seventeenth and watched about four hundred people walk in and out of a yard past a table where a clerk sat and took nobody's name and gave nothing away, for about fourteen hours, which was the entire reason the window had been left open that long, and which nobody had known was a reason until it had happened.

And Tarin Kest, at the top of his own line, at the pegged end, with a lamp in his hand and a bad shoulder coming on in the rain, said the thing to about forty people that is the actual cost of the job he is doing and is in the book because he asked for it to be:

"**Every person in this yard who wants this road to stay open longer is a person who is going to ask me for it, and I am the wrong man, and it is not a small wrongness, and I want it written down that I know it.**

"I am the only pair in this city. I am the only man who can hold the place and the approach, and every person who is frightened today is going to come and find the man who can do the thing, and the man who can do the thing is me, and I am going to be asked all day by people I like to keep the road open, and I am not going to be able to say no to a single one of them without them going away thinking I am a cold man, and I am not cold, and **I am the wrong man because I am the only one.**

"That is what it costs to be the bearer. It is not the memory. I will lose that like everybody else, and the road will take it off me on the outward leg and I will come back down knowing the way and not knowing why I came up, and I have made my peace with that in the last three weeks.

"**It is that I am the only one and so every frightened person in this district has got exactly one address.** And there are four thousand one households and about nine hundred people came this morning and every one of them knows the address, and by Friday every one of them will have told somebody else, and in a month there will be a queue at my gate, and it will not be a queue for a bus.

"It will be a queue of people who want the road to be longer, and I cannot be the man who says no to that queue nine hundred times, and there is one man in this yard who is going to have to be able to say it in public, and it is not going to be me, because the moment I am the man who says no to the road being longer, the road is his, and the road will not be a road after that. **It will be a queue with a timetable and I have said that sentence in this yard before and I want it read back to me one more time in a month.**"
