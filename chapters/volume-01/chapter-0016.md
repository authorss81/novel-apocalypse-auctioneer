# Chapter 16: The First Public Service

By four in the afternoon Lot 17 had three things in it that had never been in it before, and Mara Quill took them one at a time because together they would not fit in a room.

The first was the crowd, which had come to argue about a second opening and had settled into the shape argument takes in a wet yard: a knot at the rail, a ring at the board, and a thin line of people at the gate who had come to sell something and were standing in the rain pretending they had not.

The second was the paper. Nessa Pell had spent two days copying the first charter into the public lot book in a fair hand, and the book lay open on the crate under the two lamps with its pages weighted at the corners, and people were reading it. That was new. The first time, the charter had been read aloud and had not been *looked at*, and the difference showed in the crowd, which kept drifting back to the crate the way water goes back to a low place.

The third was the fourteen people on the far bank, who were not in the yard and could not be sent for and had to be talked about in front of people who would benefit from them being talked about.

"Eight children," Mara said. "Two escort stewards. Four of the medicine crew, one of them Renn Fisk, who went once and came back and went again. That is fourteen. Nobody in this yard gets to be vague about that number, including me, and if anybody uses the word *those children* at me in the next hour I will stop the session and go home."

---

She did the board's business first, because the board had been sitting since seven and the session had been promised for four, and the promise was the only thing she had that nobody in the yard could do without.

"Two items before the route. Item one." She put Stenn's ledger on the crate, open. "The ward market's request for a published three-day service. The board is asked to record it."

"Record it as refused," Stenn said. "Don't record it as a favor. Record it as refused, with the reason, and let me take the reason back to sixty people who will otherwise believe it is being considered."

The yard made a small noise it did not expect to make.

"Noted," Nessa Pell said, and wrote: **Request for a published three-day standing service is recorded as refused. No such right exists. Repeated use may strand the route permanently, and a standing service is a permanent right wearing a schedule.** The book does not waive a toll and does not grant a use.**

"That is a hard sentence to take back to a room," Stenn said.

"It is a true sentence. Take it back."

"Item two," Mara said. "Mr. Vale's offer."

Adrian had been standing to the side of the crate, on the far stool, in the place she had given him the day before, which is the place of a person who reads the lot and has no vote.

"I am offering the inspection press and the Lot 17 repair-crew term," he said. "As security for whatever the board agrees to. The press is worth four hundred and the crew is worth a season. It is inspectable, it is mine, and it is a real thing rather than a promise."

"Noted, and refused," Mara said.

The yard waited for the rest of it, because refusals in this room had tended to come with a reason and the reason was usually the interesting part.

"The reason is in Rell Bode's challenge, which this board upheld in part yesterday afternoon. The remedy for a crossing has to work for a person the record cannot point at. A press in a shed on this bank does not work for a person the record cannot point at. It is not security for that, it is security for me." She looked at Adrian, and it cost her something to do it in public, and she did it anyway. "And there is a second reason, which is mine. Mr. Vale signed a page in this book. It says he will not use a private promise to make a public charter appear complete. If the security is not in the book, the charter is not secure, and if the charter is not secure, then a man in a wet yard has been told that a thing is safe because of something nobody can look at. That is exactly the sentence that has been sold to a great many people in this city with a different word in it."

"Say the other word," a voice said from the knot at the rail.

"I did," Mara said. "Belief."

The knot at the rail was quiet. The line at the gate was not.

---

"Then here is the rest of it," she said, "and it is the part that matters, and it is short."

She put a clean sheet on the crate beside the open book and weighted the corners with two coppers, because she had learned that people trusted a document that somebody had bothered to keep flat.

"A proposal enters this book in advance or it does not enter the book at all. Seven things. One: a route request, naming the use, the destination, and the count. Two: the toll, disclosed in the words that will be read aloud to the people who are about to pay it. Three: a named bearer, and a named remembering passenger if the use requires one, and what that passenger is being asked to carry. Four: a term with a stated end. Five: a remedy that works for a person the record cannot point at. Six: two fresh witnesses who have heard the whole of it and who are not on the bus. Seven: a way for affected people to refuse, in this yard, without giving a reason, up to the moment the case is opened and not one minute after."

"And who reads it?" Adrian asked.

"Nobody reads it, or a clerk reads it, or somebody who can spell reads it twice, and then we read it aloud here and anybody who does not understand it says so before we open the case." She tapped the sheet. "And the last part of seven is the part the first charter got wrong. Last time the refusal window closed when I said it closed. This time it closes when the case is open, and I am going to read the time out loud, and the board is going to check it, and Mr. Vale is going to stand on that rail and not touch the glass until Nessa says the word."

"Agreed," Adrian said.

"Mr. Vale has agreed in front of the yard, which is the second time in a week that he has agreed to something in front of the yard, and I am recording both of them."

---

The first proposal through Mara's own schedule was the one the crowd had come for, and it was not Adrian's, which she had expected and had been quietly afraid of, because she had wanted the first one to be his and it had better not be.

It came from Alma Dren, and it was a return.

"Eight children, two escorts, four of the medicine crew, on the far bank of St. Orra, in a receiving ramp that belongs to a clinic, in a building the clinic needs back on Monday." Alma had her hands in her sleeves. "The official bridge is closed to a quarter of its slots and will not have a manifest for them, because they were never on one. The road you opened last week will not hold a barrow. Mr. Vale's reading does not tell me where they are. And the school has a boy here who is the only person alive who can tell me what the crossing cost, and I have been trying to get it out of him for a week, and I have got four sentences, and none of them are the crossing."

"Name your bearer," Mara said.

"Tarin Kest, and nobody else, and if he says no then we do not do it today."

"Name your remembering passenger."

The yard went quiet in a way that Mara had been in rooms for eight years and still found difficult.

Alma did not answer at once. She looked at the back of the room, and Mara turned and found Wenna Keel standing up in the second row of the crowd with a boy's jacket over her arm, and understood.

"You told me last night that you would take the road if the terms are right," Mara said.

"I told you I would take the road," Wenna Keel said. "I did not say I would take a bus."

"You did, in front of twelve people."

"That was about writing my name down. This is about my mouth." The woman came forward. "I can hold a place in my head. I have been doing it since Tuesday, when I said Ward Nine and the second rail and the joint under the lamp, over and over, on a stair, in a kitchen, in this room. That is what you want, isn't it. A person who can say the place when nobody else can."

"I want a person who can say the place *and know what it is for*," Mara said. "Because the toll takes the why. So I am going to ask you a question and I want you to hear how it is built before you answer it. What is the destination?"

"The second rail east of the bell post, on the near apron, under the lamp."

"Good. And why are you travelling?"

Wenna Keel opened her mouth, and Mara watched her get to the edge of the sentence and stop, because she had heard the shape of the question and understood in the same moment that she was not permitted to say the answer. That her son's name was the answer. That the moment she said it out loud in this yard it would be on a page somewhere and she did not want it on any page, and that the toll wanted it anyway.

"I am not going to tell you," she said. "Because you told me to say the place and not the why. And because I have understood, standing here, that the road is going to take the why out of me on the way out, and if I say it now a piece of paper in a municipal drawer will have it for the rest of my life."

Mara wrote, in the public book, in the plain hand she used for things that must not be minimized:

**Remembering passenger: Wenna Keel, a woman, who has one of the eight on the far bank. She is asked to hold a place, not a purpose, and the toll will take her purpose. She knows this and has accepted it in this yard. Recorded at her own request, and her reason is not recorded, and that refusal is the point.**

"Then say it out loud for the yard," Mara said, "so that the yard can hear what it is agreeing to let you pay."

Wenna Keel turned around and faced them, and her voice was steady, and Mara had seen that steadiness before and had never once got used to it.

"I am going because my boy is on the other bank and I sent him there," she said, "and by the time this bus comes back I am not going to know that, and you are all going to make me get on it anyway, and I want that in your ears before it happens, because it is the last time any of you will hear me say it and I would like it to be said in the yard and not written in a drawer."

Alma Dren put a hand over her own mouth. Marda Oks, on her crate, put her face into her hands. Somewhere near the gate a woman who had come to sell something stood very still.

Mara did not write it down. She had promised the woman that the reason would not be recorded, and a promise of that kind was the only kind she had ever been certain of.

"Noted in the room," she said, "and not in the book. It stands as spoken."

---

Tarin read the terms himself, at the rail, at half past five, with the light going.

He read them the way a man reads a list of parts he intends to be responsible for, and he stopped twice.

"Here," he said, at the term. "You have written that it ends when the second of the two named return travelers reaches the marked point or at twelve minutes. Last time I was one of the two named returners and it was a formality. This time neither named returner is on the bus tonight except me and her." He looked at Wenna Keel. "That means the term ends when I arrive. If I do not arrive it runs out, and if it runs out I am still driving, and nobody has told me what to do."

"That is what I asked you for on Tuesday," Tarin said. "You did not write it."

"Then write it now, and it goes in before the case opens." He put his thumb on the board. "I want a sentence about the far side. Not the stop. The road. If I come up out of the cut past the river wall and the bay on the card is not the way it was when we went down, then I do not go into the bay. I hold the line, I take the outside shoulder, and I come down the way I can see. And if the whole of it is a service wall and there is nothing to see, then I stop the bus and I get out and I walk back toward the wall, and nobody follows me onto it."

"Not even for a child," someone said.

"Especially not for a child," Tarin said. "A child in a bus with a driver who has no reason is the safest thing in the district. A child on a road with a driver who is out of it is the thing that gets a name put in a report."

Mara wrote it, and Adrian read it over her shoulder, and neither of them asked the question that was obviously coming, which is that the far side might not be a road at all.

"And the return point is not the bell post," Tarin said.

"Second rail east of the bell post, under the lamp, where the ballast drops and the plate is stamped," Adrian said, "in the bus you drive, coming down from the far bank."

"You will put my feet in the book."

"I will put the joint in the book and the bus in the book and the direction in the book, because you told me that is the pair and I am not going to leave one half of a pair out of a sentence."

Tarin looked at the book for a long moment. "Then it is in the book," he said. "And if somebody moves the lamp, the joint is still the joint, and I will find it, and if I do not find it, then the sentence is wrong and the road is shut and we will find out why in daylight with the book open." He took off his cap. "Read the whole thing to the yard. All of it. The two-of-us count. The thing about the road. I want my bus's number called out loud so that a child can hear it."

---

Alma Dren did not board, and neither did Petra Sill's feverish child, and Mara had refused to let either of them be argued with, and neither of them had argued.

The witnesses were Coll Ferrin and Marda Osk, taken out of the two rotating seats because they held no claim on the lot and had both read the whole of it in the yard, and neither of them was on the bus. Nessa Pell read the disclosed toll out loud to every person in the yard three times, in the words the travelers were going to hear, and Marda Osk read it a fourth time to the people at the gate who had not come in.

"Every traveler loses the reason they are traveling," Nessa Pell said. "You will not remember why you went. If you come back, you pay it again. You will be able to say where you were and you will not be able to say why, and nobody can give it back to you, and if you are one of the eight who went before, I cannot tell you what the road finds in you a second time, because it will not tell me either."

The yard stood in the rain and let that be said out loud in public, which was the entire difference between the last crossing and this one, and nobody thought so at the time.

---

The case was opened at seven.

Adrian did not touch it. He stood on the rail with his hands in his pockets while Nessa turned the key, and Mara watched him keep that promise from the far side of the yard and understood that it was the first promise he had ever kept that had cost him something to keep.

He read the token once, aloud, for the board, and the reading came in the order it always came in.

```text
RETURN USE: ONE ROUTE ACROSS ST. ORRA. TWELVE MINUTES.

```

Mara read the last line twice before she let the yard see it.

"Not stated," she said. "Registrar's note. The object has taken this toll from these fourteen people once already and does not report what a second payment finds, and I am not going to guess at it in front of you. You are being asked to send fourteen people back onto a road that has already taken the reason out of them, and the honest position is that nobody in this yard knows what the second one takes. Not me, not the road, and not the man who can read it."

Somebody in the crowd said, "Then don't send them."

"No," Mara said. "Then we decide with the fact in the room, in daylight, instead of finding out at four in the morning." She looked at the board. "All four seats: does the record show a use, a bearer, a beneficiary group, a toll, a term, a release, a remedy, two witnesses who are not traveling, and a way to refuse?"

"Show," Nessa Pell said.

"Show," said Coll Ferrin, and Marda Osk, and Nessa Pell again, and the registrar.

"Open the case."

The wax broke. The token went into the slot above the old fare box, and the metal teeth took it as if the bus had been waiting for it, and the yard lamps dipped, and a black line opened in the rain outside that was not a road but the space where a road had been agreed to.

---

Tarin and Wenna Keel went out first, and two people paid. Then the bus was gone for six minutes and Mara stood on the rail with her hands at her sides and did not do the thing she wanted to do, which was to count.

The route came back up out of the cut and past the river wall, and Wenna Keel was at the window with her forehead on the glass, and Tarin came down the marked spur and did not take the bell post turn, and stopped with his wheels a hand's width short of the second rail east of the post, under the lamp, where the ballast dropped away and the plate was stamped.

He was out and behind the bus before the engine had cooled, standing in the road with his hands on his head, and a Warden with a white flag was on the spur, and Mara watched the first two of them come down the ramp.

The fourteen came down off the bus the way people come off a bus who have been sitting down for a long time and do not know where they are.

The two escort stewards came first, in their grey canvas coats, and they were still holding the school roll between them, folded tight, eleven days old, and they had kept it the whole time, which nobody had asked them to do.

"Eight," said the one with the roll. "Eight children, all of them off, all of them walking, and I am answering for all eight, and I answered for nine, and the ninth is on your side of the water and we did not argue with him."

"Recorded," Mara said, and her voice did what voices do, and she wrote the numbers down in the order they had been given so that the order itself was the record: *8 children. 2 stewards. 4 crew. 2 named return travelers. 16 travelers. Two paid on the outward leg. All sixteen paid on the return. Eighteen tolls taken. Sixteen people.*

Renn Fisk came down the ramp in a blue coat with a cold-chain sheet rolled in his fist, and he stopped in front of Adrian.

"I went over once and I got dragged back out of a wall," he said, "and I went over again knowing what it took, and I have just been over a third time." He looked at his own hands. "I told you I would answer for the boxes. I want it written that I answered for the boxes, and that I did not answer for anybody's reason."

"It is written."

"Then I will say the other thing." He looked at the ramp, where eight small people were standing in the rain under a Warden's blanket, looking at nothing with the particular attention of people who have arrived somewhere. "They are not frightened. That is what nobody warned me about. They are not frightened and they are not asking where they are, and two of them have asked me for a blanket as if this were an ordinary evening, and I have been across that river three times and I have never seen anything like it, and I would like somebody to write down that the thing the toll does is not pain. The thing it does is *politeness*."

---

The eight were brought into the depot and put by the stove with tea, and they were asked questions, and here is what the questions got.

They could all name the platform. Not one of them could say why they had been sent. They could describe the receiving ramp and the roof of stacked loading boards and the woman with the handcart. Two of them asked who was supposed to meet them. One of them said, when asked what had happened to her in the six days she had been there, "Somebody said I was waiting for something," and then looked at the adult beside her as though the sentence had been handed to her by the adult and she had decided to carry it.

Renna Ivers was seven. She had been the one who said she remembered being told, but not by whom.

Mara asked her four times. On the fourth Renna put both hands flat on her knees and said, with enormous patience, as though explaining something to somebody very slow, "It took the *why* the first time. And it took it again the second time, and I don't know if there was a third one. I looked. It isn't there. It's just me and the lamp post."

Nobody in the room said anything for some seconds after that, and Adrian found that he had to stand up and go outside.

---

The hearing was at nine, and Mara had to call it, because it was the remedy, and the remedy was the thing she had promised would work for a person the record could not point at.

Rell Bode had come, without being asked, from the river stairs.

The eight children were in the yard, on the tram rail, in dry coats. Alma Dren was with them. The two escort stewards were there in their grey coats. The sealed sheets were in Mara's coat and in the school's, and the names on them were the only names that could turn *eight children who cannot describe the crossing* into *eight people to whom something is owed*.

"Here is the choice," Mara said, to the yard, in the flat voice she used when she was about to do the thing she would be judged for. "I can open the sealed page. I have the page. The names are on it and the page opens tonight and in ten minutes I can have this room hear what the crossing took from eight named people, one at a time, with a witness for each, and it will be the most complete account of a toll in the history of this city, and it will be worth something, and every future charter will be argued against it."

"And then?" said Rell Bode, from the back.

"And then every one of those eight children has a name in a municipal book attached to a loss they cannot describe, for the rest of their lives, and the person who took the name is not me. It is whoever holds the book in ten years. I am only the first one."

"You said it was deferred seizure when you described it."

"I did."

"And now you are offering to do it once, on purpose, tonight, because it would make you useful."

"Yes," Mara said. "I am offering to do it once, on purpose, tonight, because it would make me useful, and I would like everybody in this yard to hear me admit that in that order."

Nessa Pell had her pencil over the book.

"Alternative," Mara said. "We do not open the page. We hold the hearing without names. The eight say what they can say in a public room, and the record says that the remedy was called on the fourteenth day and *was not discharged*, and it says why: the affected persons cannot describe the loss, and the category that protects them is the reason it cannot be described, and that is written down in a public book in plain words where anybody can read it. The loss is heard. The loss is not repaired. And in three years, when somebody builds a better remedy, there is a public entry saying a remedy failed here, and who it failed on, without saying who they were."

"That is a worse record," Nessa Pell said.

"It is a worse record and a truer one."

Marda Osk, from her crate, said, "Which of the two does Rell Bode want?"

"I am the one who is refused a name," Rell Bode said. "Nobody has asked me what I want since I came down the stairs."

"Then I am asking."

"You are asking because it is the only thing you can offer a room, and you want to be decent in public, and I will say this so you cannot pretend otherwise." Rell Bode's voice stayed level. "I want a book that fails in public, and I want it to fail often enough that the failing is expensive, and I do not want a book that succeeds once by taking a child's name out of a drawer. And I will say the harder thing too, because you have been honest and it costs me nothing to be honest back. My boy has a bad chest and he is not in either version of your choice. I am watching eight children who got a road and cannot say why, and I am watching a woman who sent one and cannot remember, and I am watching a man who went three times and has gone soft-voiced about it, and none of that touches a page yet. That is what I am watching."

"Recorded," Mara said, "in the room and not in the book, and the room is the record for it."

She turned to the four seats.

"Board: the remedy is called. The page is not opened. Is the refusal to open it recorded as a decision?"

"Recorded," said Nessa Pell, and Coll Ferrin, and Marda Osk, and the registrar, and they did not look at Adrian while they said it, because it was not his to look at.

Mara wrote the entry and read it out twice, and then she put the sealed sheets back in her coat, and the coat went in the stove with the eight children and the two stewards and the whole wet warm noise of a depot that had just discovered it could house something larger than a sale.

---

Alma Dren said the thing afterward, in the doorway, to Adrian, and she said it plainly, because she had never once in his hearing pretended that a thing was smaller than it was.

"You built the category that protected them, and you built it in a week, and it worked," she said. "Eight children are home and not one of them is a line in anybody's file. I have wanted that since the year it was explained to me that the last registry could do this."

"It is Mara's category."

"It is your reading and her pen and about forty other people. Do not be modest, it is not the hour for it." She turned to go, and stopped, and came back. "The cost is the other thing. She chose the room. The room is where the loss gets heard and the book is where the loss gets *used*, and she chose the one where a record cannot be made to look tidy, and every one of those eight is going to be an argument somebody raises in a bad mood for the next ten years, and she took that on herself, in front of witnesses, which is the only currency any of us have."

Adrian looked out at the yard. Selik's Wardens were still on the marked spur with the white flag furled, and the public board was lit, and the yard was full of people who had watched eight children come down a ramp and had seen nothing at all happen to them, which was the most expensive kind of nothing there is.

"I have wanted two things since I opened that case," he said. "I have wanted the road to work, and I have wanted the book to be a thing I could point at when somebody asked me why. Those are not the same thing and this evening is the first time I have known that."

Alma considered him. "Then which one did you want more?"

"I do not know. That is a new thing to have to say."

---

Nessa Pell locked the case at half past eleven and entered the handoff, and Adrian signed beneath her, and the token sat in its bed of oiled cloth with a crack that had gone from a bright hairline to a dull one and had reached the middle of the plate.

The two crossings had worked. Adrian had said so out loud to the yard when he posted the observed result, and so had Mara, and nobody in the yard who was under forty believed either of them.

Then the yard began to fill again, in the way it does when a thing has happened, and this time they came with questions instead of a crowd.

A woman from the Ward Nine cooperative had walked up from the far bank in the dark, three hours, with a list and a lantern, and she stood at the rail and asked how a group of eleven got on a bus for a return when the clinic's fourteen boxes had been refused twice.

A young man off the Needle Tramway had come with four others and a handcart of signal cable, and he wanted to know when there would be a third opening, and he had brought a ledger because he had already worked out what a route was worth to a repair crew, and he wanted it in writing.

And at the gate, at twenty to midnight, four people arrived in a single cart with eleven children in it, and a woman in a grey canvas coat got down and asked, not loudly, whether there was room on the next one.

Eleven children. Not eight. Not the eight who had come home. People who had seen the eight come home, and had gone home and told somebody, and had come to see whether it was real.

Nessa Pell looked at Adrian. Adrian looked at Mara.

Then the courier came up the yard at a run with a clinic slip in her hand, and the slip said that the fever ward on the far bank had eleven cots and no draught and no stock and a queue on the far side of the water four hours long, and that the next shipment was fourteen boxes and did not fit on any vehicle the Wardens would certify, and that the upper yard had four repair gangs in it that morning, and that the word going round the far bank was that the road had been open twice in a week.

Mara read the slip twice and put it flat on the crate and looked at it for a while.

"Fourteen boxes," she said. "Eleven children. Four repair gangs. One day, because the next shipment's coolant starts at dawn and the ward has eleven cots and no draught." She looked up at the yard, and the yard looked back, and it was very late, and everybody in it was already thinking the same sentence. "That is not a road. That is a city coming to the door."

"It is more than a road," Adrian said.

"It is more than one road, and we have one token, and the token is cracked through the middle, and nobody in this city has any idea whether it opens again." Mara put her hand flat on the slip. "Registrar's note, and I am writing it in public: the demand is now larger than the charter can honestly carry. From this moment, nothing crosses until the terms say who the crossing is for, in a public session, in daylight, before anybody boards."

"Then the day after tomorrow," Stenn said, from somewhere in the crowd, "the ward market will have heard that we said no to a demand we had already promised."

"Yes," Mara said. "It will."
