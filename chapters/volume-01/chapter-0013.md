# Chapter 13: The Paper Network

The kitchen exchange kept its secrets in flour, and Orren Pike had stopped pretending otherwise.

He crouched beside the third barrel in the cold store under the Ward Nine tram line, with a hooded candle between his knees, and he laid out what he had taken that night in the order he had taken it.

A copied claim on the left. Then a Warden fuel return. Then a page torn out of a school ration ledger. Then a salt-trading permit, which was older than the Fracture and had no business in the same pile. Beside them all he put the fragment he had lifted out of Halloway's warehouse office, the one with the mark on it and the place-name scraped away.

Above him, through the boards, boots went past in the alley. Two sets, unhurried, and then a third that stopped and started and stopped. Somebody was moving crates again, and the timing was the timing of a man who did not want to be counted.

Orren put his thumb on the scraped heading and made himself look at it instead of at the alley.

---

The copied claim was not one document. That was the first thing he had been wrong about for two months, and he had been wrong about it in a way he was not proud of, because he had wanted it to be one document, and then stealing it would have been the same as ending it.

It was one document the way a tune is one tune.

He had found three claims in three weeks, in three parts of the city, offered by three men who had never been in the same room: a lamp-trimmer at the tram arcades named Ossie Prent, who sold trimmed wicks to Halloway's warehouse and to the clinic and was liked by everybody who owed him money; a rope-maker at the river stairs named Jory Slade, who could splice anything that had ever been wet; and a man who wrote claims at a tea table on the upper terraces named Hask Vell, who had never carried a load in his life and had the hands for it.

Three men. Three parts of the city. One form.

He had compared the copies by lamplight with a hand lens lifted off a spectacle-maker's cart, and the type was not merely similar. It was identical down to a miscut *r* in *carriage* that leaned a hair to the left, a drag in the third line of the second page where the nib had been pulled sideways, and a space in front of the word *bearers* that four different sets of hands had reproduced exactly. Four. He had found the fourth under a different broker's table, offered by a boy of twelve who did not know what he was holding.

That was not four forgers. Forgery leaves fingerprints. This left a printing plate, and a plate implies a room, and a room implies somebody who set the words once and let other people carry the paper.

And then there was the third page.

Every copy had one, folded inside the second. Nine words in the largest type on the plate: **IF THE ROUTE CLOSES EARLY, THE BEARER REPAIRS THE ROAD FOR SEVEN DAYS.**

It was the sentence that had stopped the carriers at Lot 17. It was also the sentence that made the claim work. A man who cannot read can read *carriage*. A man who can read can find the fold. Orren had watched Slade explain it to two hauliers at a barrow yard in the rain, and he had not lied exactly; he had compressed a seven-day lien on a stranger's body into the word roads.

Orren had spent two days trying to decide whether Slade was a villain, and had arrived at the answer he had arrived at about most people in Ward Nine, which was that Slade had a sister in the fever ward on the far bank and the ward market had not moved her in eleven days.

---

Then he came to the mark, and the mark ruined the night.

He had assumed, since he was seventeen and had taken his first bridge form out of a registrar's drawer, that the broken circle was a signature. A mark like that had to belong to somebody. A seal belongs to an office. A signature belongs to a name. He had gone looking for the person, the way he went looking for Sela, and he had begun to build a shape in his head: a distant office with a distant name, a place no map in Alder Reach admitted existed. He had been quietly proud of how nearly the shape came to fitting.

It was not a signature. It was a claim about a *system*, and he proved it by accident, holding a fuel return up against the candle.

The fuel return was a Warden form. Ordinary, ugly, four columns, stamped on the back with a depot number and a date in a clerk's careful hand. Nothing to do with St. Orra, nothing to do with a fare, nothing to do with a road. A receipt for eleven gallons of lamp oil.

In the lower left corner, pressed in uneven wax with a small notch in the outer line, was the incomplete circle.

He went cold from the hands up and sat on the boards and thought about what he was holding.

Because a depot clerk does not press a wax seal on a fuel return. A depot clerk writes a number. If a depot clerk did press a wax seal on a fuel return, then either the depot had become something other than a depot, or somebody had stood in it and put the seal there to make the fuel return look like it came from a place with seals.

So he went and looked for the rest of it, and he found the ration ledger, and then the salt permit, and by the time the candles were down to their holders he had five documents from five unrelated offices across three years, all of them carrying the same broken circle in the same heavy pressure, and not one of them belonging to the others.

The ration ledger page was from the school shelter. He knew it because he had eaten from the shelter's soup line four times, and because he knew the cook's shorthand, which was a small hooked mark in the left margin where a quantity had been cut.

A school ration ledger. A salt-trading permit. A Warden fuel return. A counterfeit Fare claim. A bus manifest. Five documents, no common office, no common clerk, no common year, one impression.

*That* is not a man, he thought, sitting in the dark with his mother's name under his knee. *That is a coat.*

Somebody had found the shape — a shape that meant *this came out of one place where everything is counted* — and had learned to press it onto paper. And the people pressing it were not the people who made it. Prent had no die. Slade had no die. Vell had a wax stick he had traded a lamp bracket for, and Orren had seen him hold it, and Vell's hand shook when he was tired, which was most of the time.

The counterfeit worked because people cannot check a shape. They can check a name against a roll, and a name can be a lie, and a roll can be edited, and a market clerk is a stranger. What they cannot check is whether a document belongs to a system. A mark in the corner says: *there was a whole arrangement behind this, with more pages and more clerks and somebody at the top of it.* That is the only thing it says. It says nothing about where and nothing about who. It says *you do not have to read all of this, because it is part of something larger*, and that is the most valuable sentence in the world to a man who cannot read.

He wrote it out in his own hand on a scrap, in the shorthand he used for himself, because he wanted to see whether it was true.

**THE MARK IS NOT A NAME. IT IS A SENTENCE ABOUT A SYSTEM. SOMEONE IS WEARING IT.**

Then he sat there while the candle guttered, and understood that he had found something he could not use and had to keep anyway.

---

Above him the boards creaked. Bett Ruhl came down the ladder backwards, which was how she came down it when she wanted the lamp behind her and her face not in it.

She was fifty and grey and she ran the exchange the way other people ran a household, by knowing who owed whom and by never once putting it down. The two children slept upstairs behind a hung blanket. The household under the third barrel was her brother-in-law, a signal hand off the Needle Tramway who had been struck off the rolls in year fourteen, and he was the reason Orren's papers had been allowed to live under her floor for two months.

"Up," Bett said. "Both of you. Now, not after you have finished."

"Two minutes."

"You said that on Tuesday."

Orren gathered the papers in the order he had laid them out, and stopped when his hand came to the fragment with the scraped heading.

Because Bett Ruhl had not come down for the alley. She had come down for that.

---

"Give it to me," she said.

"No."

"You have put my house in that."

"Your house is not in that. Your house is in the other thing." He held up the fuel return, which was clean. "The Fare copies carry a return point that will drown a man. The mark is on a ration page and a fuel return and a salt permit, and I am keeping the mark. But this fragment has a margin note on the back naming a source record, and I wrote a name on the front of that margin when I was sixteen and I have never been able to make myself burn it. So it has a name on it, and there is a household under your floor, and if a broker or a Warden or a clerk gets that page out of this room and turns it round, then the page is a map. And you have been careful for twenty years to be nowhere on any page."

Bett Ruhl stood on the ladder and did not come down.

"Where is she?" Orren said, because he had learned that asking straight worked with her about half the time.

"Under your knees, same as two months ago."

"That is not a place."

"It is a place tonight." Her voice went flat. "You have been keeping a page with a stolen name on it in a house with a man who cannot be named. I have asked you before. You said the name was evidence. I have been feeding you for two months on the strength of that word."

"It is evidence."

"It is a hook." She came down two rungs. "You know what I mean by that, and you are using my own word, because it is the one that gets you down the ladder."

He looked at the fragment for a while.

Then he took the small knife off his belt, not the long one, the one he cut cord with, and laid the page on the flour barrel and cut it in two lengthwise, following the crease it already had, because the crease was where the source record had been written and the margin note was on the other half of the sheet.

Two pieces of paper. The counterfeit's broken form, and a regional heading whose place-name had been scraped away by somebody who had wanted a copy of it. And, on the other half, a note in his own sixteen-year-old hand: **S. PIKE — SOURCE RECORD WITHHELD.**

He put the first piece into the evidence bundle.

The second he held out to her.

"Take it," he said. "Burn it or keep it under the flour. I do not care. But it is not in the bundle and it does not go to the registrar and it does not go to Lot 17. If you burn it, then the record of what was done to my mother goes out of the world tonight, and I will have to accept that, and I will hate you for it, and I will still bring you the soup."

Bett Ruhl took the piece of paper. She did not look at it. She went back up the ladder with it, and Orren heard the flap close, and then a lamp being turned down.

He sat alone with four documents and a candle and the boots in the alley.

---

When he came up, the crate-moving had stopped.

The courier was in the exchange, sitting on the upturned bench with a bowl of something hot she did not touch, which meant she had bad news and had not yet decided how much of it to sell. She was the girl with the red scarf and the brass key sewn into her sleeve from two months ago, and she looked older, or the winter had done that.

"Your brother-in-law says the false west wall is open," she said. "They are loading out of the tram warehouse, not the one under the tram, and they are loading it before light. Four carts."

"Paper or people?"

"Both. They are moving the crates out of Ward Nine because something is coming they do not want standing in Ward Nine when it comes."

Orren's stomach went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the bowl. "Coming for what?"

"I do not know. He said to tell you the brokers are being paid to be a long way away tomorrow, and that means the claim is being used in the morning." She set the bowl down. "There is more and it is worse, and I am saying it now so that you cannot pretend later that I softened it."

"Say it."

"A carrier took the claim at the tea table this afternoon. Vell wrote it out and the carrier read the first page and the second page and never opened the third, because the third page was folded inside the second, and he signed." She took a breath. "It is a man called Sabin Tull. Nine boxes of clinic stores on a barrow with a bad wheel. He means to go at first light, before the Wardens change, and take the small-cargo lane to the abutment and try to make the official bridge take him, and if the official bridge will not take him he is going to try the fare."

Orren put the evidence bundle inside his coat and did the arithmetic, which was fast and total.

The official bridge had a quarter of its slots left and a feverish child at the head of its line. Vell's tea table was two streets from the staging yard. And there was a man called Sabin Tull with nine boxes of clinic stores and a barrow with a bad wheel who had been handed a piece of paper that said *one route across St. Orra*, and had never in his life been shown a fold.

He would open it. Not because he was dishonest. Because he would be standing on a road at four in the morning with nine boxes that are the right temperature for two more hours, and the fold is a thing you find when you have time, and he would not have time.

"Somebody has to tell him before the tea goes cold," Orren said.

"Not me."

"I know it is not you. Me." He was already at the door. "How long has Vell got him for?"

"Until the lamps are lit."

Orren stopped.

"Orren." The courier's voice was careful. "You can run to him. That is not nothing. But he is one man, and there are three brokers with the same plate, and if the copy you show him is the only copy in the city, then when you have shown it you have used it up, and tomorrow there is no paper in Alder Reach that says what that third page says."

She was telling him that a warning delivered once is a rumor, and a rumor is what a counterfeit lives on.

So he went back down and made copies.

He could not write well and could not spell, and what he had learned in two months of stealing was that a forger's tools are the only tools in a city that are not locked up. He had a bad printing plate of his own, cut for handbills in year fifteen, and a pot of the sort of ink the tram notice office threw away, and the truth was that the third page was nine words long and set in the largest type on the plate.

**IF THE ROUTE CLOSES EARLY, THE BEARER REPAIRS THE ROAD FOR SEVEN DAYS.**

He printed eleven. It took him the rest of the night, and his hand went bad at the sixth, so Bett Ruhl came down and finished the last five, and did not ask what they were for, and did not sign any of them.

---

When the lamps were being lit he went out into the rain and started up toward the terraces with a bundle of eleven in his coat and two loose in his hand.

The first he gave at the barrow yard to a man he did not know, because the man was loading clinic stores and had a bad wheel and a cold look. The second he put under the door of the rope shop at the river stairs, where Slade slept over the workshop. The third he slid into the lamp-trim box at the arcades for Prent, who would be opening at six and would know exactly what it was and who had put it there.

Then he went to the tea table, where the shutters were down and one lamp was still lit, and where Sabin Tull was sitting with a folded claim in his hand and nine boxes already stacked on the barrow with the bad wheel.

Orren put the copy down in front of him, printed side up, and said nothing about his mother, or the mark, or a system in a coat.

"Third page," he said.

Tull, who was twenty-nine and in a hurry and had a reason, unfolded the claim, checked it against the printed sheet, and went very still.

"Nine boxes," he said. "Of the fever stores. And it says *the bearer*."

"Yes."

"Nine boxes is not a bearer."

"No," Orren said. "It is whoever is holding the barrow. That is the trick of it. It never says the driver, so a driver thinks it is a joke, and a driver who thinks it is a joke signs."

Tull looked at the claim for a long time. Behind him the bad wheel sat against the kerb at the angle it always sat at.

"Where did you get this printed?"

"There are eleven in the city," Orren said, "and I can put you in front of any one of them, and there will be a registrar with a book open at Lot 17 before the lamps are properly out. If you take that piece of paper up to the abutment you will be a bare claimant on a fault, and bare claimants are the reason there is a man at the riverside with a winch and no apology." He put his hand flat on the printed sheet. "Take it to the board. Say you showed it before you signed. It is the only thing left that is worth anything, because right now the whole city believes that page is a road condition."

Tull stood up. He did not thank him. He folded the claim in half, then in half again, put it in the inside of his coat, and went to find a chock for the wheel.

Orren came down off the terrace in the first grey of the morning with his coat lighter and his head full of a shape he could not put anywhere: a fuel return, a ration page, a salt permit, a bus manifest, a counterfeit claim, one impression, no office, no clerk, no name, and somewhere a man in a good coat who had understood that a document can be made to look like the output of a settlement by nothing more than a shape in the corner.

He could not find the settlement. He could not find the clerk. He had five papers and a mystery and no idea at all which of the two was the dangerous one.

And four streets away, at Lot 17, the first lamp above the public board was already lit, because Adrian Vale had been in the yard since four, and Mara Quill had not gone home, and a board that could not grant a single thing was about to be asked to stand between a forged claim and a fever ward before the sun came up.
