# Chapter 9: Twelve Minutes of Public Road

The public lot board was ready before dawn, though the rain had not stopped.

It stood on the eastern wall of Lot 17, where the sale tickets used to hang. Mara had painted it black and ruled it into seven columns. The columns were not named *lot* and *toll* on the board. Those words belonged to the office. On the board they read **THING USED**, **WHAT IT DOES**, **WHO CARRIES IT**, **WHO IS AFFECTED**, **WHAT IS PAID**, **HOW LONG**, **IF IT FAILS**. The last column was wider than the others because Mara had decided that failure needed room.

Two lamps hooked above it. Their light caught the wet roof and turned the public record into a small bright island in the dark depot.

The medicine convoy waited outside beneath a canvas cover. Six cold boxes—the two that had crossed and been recovered, and the four that had stayed on the depot rail—had been transferred to a hand cart, and the cart had been tied to the rear of Tarin’s road bus with rope that did not matter as much as everyone pretended. The school shelter group stood under the tram awning: nine children, a steward, and two escorts. The children had been told there would be a road. They had not been told what the road would take.

Adrian had told them.

That was why they were still there.

He stood beside the board with the public charter in his hand. The page was crowded with corrections in Mara’s red pencil. Orren had checked the names of witnesses. Lina’s courier had brought the shelter’s count without its household roll. Tarin stood beside the bus, one hand on the door, the other on a route card he did not need.

The Last Fare lay in a glass case on a table near the board. The token had been taken from its sealed envelope under Mara’s and Adrian’s supervision. Its rim carried the hairline dark mark from the failed activation. The red cloth was folded beside it, no longer tied around the metal.

No one called it safe.

A Warden crossed the yard with a private buyer behind him. The buyer carried a flat brass claim in one hand and a folded permit in the other. He was not carrying the Last Fare. He was carrying a counterfeit made to look as though the public route had already been sold.

“Stop,” Selik’s Warden said.

“I have a claim,” the buyer said. “The route is open. My carriers are waiting.”

“No valid anchor object,” the Warden said. “No public charter. No dispatcher. You are carrying a brass copy and a bad temper.”

The buyer lifted the permit. “It is stamped.”

“The stamp is not a witness.”

Selik came out of the Wardens’ shelter. He had not slept. The inspection sheet was tucked beneath his arm, and the official bridge lantern swung behind him on a short chain.

“Where are your carriers?” he asked.

“East staging.”

“Show me their names.”

The buyer’s mouth tightened. “They are workers. They do not need names on a manifest.”

“Then they are not standing behind a public charter. They are standing behind a promise made by a person who wants to avoid the part you just admitted.”

Orren stepped forward with the master copy. He had slept in the kitchen exchange, but his hair was wet and his satchel bulged with papers. He read the hidden addendum without looking down.

“If the route closes before completion,” he said, “the bearer repairs the carriage and the adjacent road for seven days.”

The buyer lunged. A Warden caught his arm. The counterfeit claim fell into a puddle. The carriers waiting beyond the east staging saw the movement and stopped. One of them turned back toward the public road. Another shouted that they would not carry children under a labor clause they had never seen.

Orren kept reading. “The return point is the old salt-loading ramp. It flooded before the Fracture. This is not the Last Fare’s return point.”

Mara opened her tablet. “I am recording the claim as counterfeit and incomplete. I am not replacing the witnesses. The carriers are not crossing under it.”

The buyer turned on her. “You are blocking a private road.”

“I am recording that you brought a document with no valid anchor object, no public closing, and a hidden toll. Your carriers have refused movement after disclosure. If they proceed, they do so as bare claimants.”

Selik’s Warden took the permit. “No valid anchor.”

“Because the object is still in the public case,” the buyer said.

“Because the object is still under audit,” Selik said. “You do not get to bring the consequence through the front door while the key is in someone else’s pocket.”

The buyer looked at the school shelter. “A road is not a promise to a committee.”

“No,” Adrian said. “It is a promise to the people inside it. The committee is only how we make them visible.”

The buyer went quiet. For a moment, the rain seemed louder than the crowd.

Mara stepped to the board. “Open the charter.”

Adrian placed his page beneath the lamps. The hand holding it trembled. He had rested after the rescue, but rest did not erase the toll echo. It had merely given him enough time to recognize its shape.

He read the terms aloud.

“Lot: the Last Fare, a cracked brass transit token in public custody, belonging to the St. Orra Fault.”

“Use: one route across St. Orra for twelve minutes, with a marked return at the old Ward Nine platform. Repeated use may strand the route.”

“Bearer: Tarin Kest, sole driver and route operator.”

“Beneficiaries: the medicine convoy, the school shelter group of nine children, and the people waiting in Ward Nine who will receive the medicine.”

“Toll: every traveler loses the memory of why they are making the journey. Anyone who travels back pays the memory toll again.”

“Term: one activation. It ends when the second of the two named return travelers, Tarin Kest and Dessa Rill, reaches the Ward Nine platform, or at twelve minutes, whichever comes first. If either named return traveler fails to return, the term still ends at twelve minutes and the Warden remedy applies. The school group and the medicine crew remain on the far bank under Alma Dren and Oren Fisk, the clinic porter, who are named as responsible parties. There is no automatic renewal.”

“Release: the token returns to the public case, the handoff is entered in the public record, and the board posts the result. No private transfer.”

“Remedy: if the route bends, the Selik Warden at the marked spur acts as route keeper and stops continued movement, while Tarin returns to the platform if he can. Mara Quill suspends the next activation until the affected people have been heard. Any two independent witnesses, or any affected person through a witness who accepts the role, may appeal the result and request a public review before another closing.”

The list did not make the route safe. It made the risk speak in a language a person could refuse.

Mara looked at the shelter steward. “State your name and role.”

“Alma Dren,” the woman said. “School shelter steward. I have heard the terms. I accept the witness role for the shelter group. I do not accept publication of the household roll.”

The clerk hesitated over the board.

Mara spoke first. “Record the steward’s name. Record the group as nine children and the responsible shelter. Record that the children were told the memory toll and may refuse. Do not write the household names.”

A boy near the shelter raised his hand. “Can I refuse?”

“Yes,” Adrian said.

“Will someone stop me?”

“Someone will ask why you are refusing. No one will bid against you for the right to refuse.”

The boy looked at Alma. She nodded. He stepped back from the bus. His mother, or the woman who cared for him, did not pull him forward.

Mara recorded the refusal. “Passenger refusal received,” she said. “It does not cancel the charter for the people who remain.”

A freight driver stepped from the crowd. His coat carried a market-cooperative seal, not the black stripe of Tarin’s Road House. He worked the upper freight lanes under contract to the ward market, and he had no part in the medicine purchase.

“Ewan Latch,” he said. “I am neither a buyer nor a bearer. I accept the role of independent witness to the written terms and the remedy.”

Alma Dren was not a passenger, did not drive, and did not bear the route. She was the shelter steward, and she accepted only the limited role of confirming that the shelter group had been told the terms and could refuse. The children remained free to reject her witness role through their own refusals. She was not speaking for the medicine convoy, and Ewan was not speaking for Tarin.

Alma Dren and Ewan Latch had not spoken to each other before. They did not need a shared history to make their acceptance independent. They needed the same facts, freely understood, and separate reasons for accepting them.

Mara read the seven fields again. “Do either of you accept that the route may fail, that the toll may repeat on the return, and that the remedy may not restore memory?”

“Yes,” Alma said.

“Yes,” Ewan said.

Mara wrote their roles in the public record. She did not write her own name as a witness.

“Registrar Quill is recording,” Adrian said.

“I am not standing in for consent,” Mara said. “I am making the consent inspectable.”

Orren stepped forward. “I checked the written record. I read the hidden addendum. I found the false return point. Do I count?”

“No,” Mara said. “You are the source who found the hidden page. You have not accepted the witness role, and asking you to sign would turn a record check into a ceremonial signature. Keep the household out of the public book, and bring the evidence when it is called for.”

Orren’s face tightened. “I could be counted if I gave a name.”

“No. A witness is not a signature collected because a person is useful.”

He looked at Adrian. “You asked me to check the record.”

“I asked you to find the lie. You did.”

That was enough. Orren did not trust the process. He trusted the page in front of him, which was a smaller and more durable beginning.

Mara turned to Tarin. “You are the only bearer.”

Tarin looked at the bus. “Dessa Rill is the return traveler. She is not the bearer.”

“Named separately,” Mara said. “Dessa, state your role.”

Dessa stepped from the shelter awning. She was a medicine-convoy driver in a blue coat, her hair tied beneath a rain cap. Adrian had seen her only in the confusion after the rescue. The memory of the maintenance bay had not taken her name, but it had left pauses where a purpose should have been.

“I will remember Ward Nine before the first crossing,” she said. “I will return with Tarin. I am not a witness and not a second bearer. I am the passenger whose standing gives the route a destination.”

“State the destination and the reason.”

“Ward Nine. The school shelter, the clinic lines, and the people who need the medicine. I am returning to bring the next clinic team across if the first load holds.”

Mara repeated it in the record. “The destination and reason are recorded. They are not permanent. The toll will take them.”

“I understand.”

Mara turned to Tarin. “You are the only bearer. Do you accept the role, the memory toll on both legs, the marked return, and the right to stop before you take the token?”

Tarin looked at the bus, then at the school children. “Yes. I accept on those terms. If the route bends, I stop carrying forward and take the marked platform when I can. If I refuse before boarding, the charter does not open.”

“Recorded,” Mara said. “You may still refuse until the token leaves the case.”

Mara turned to Alma and Oren Fisk, a broad-shouldered clinic porter in a blue coat whose name was written on the cold-chain sheet. “You are named as the responsible parties who remain with the shelter group and the medicine crew on the far bank. Do you accept that duty?”

“Yes,” Alma said.

“Yes,” Oren said. “I will answer for the boxes, not for the people who refuse.”

Adrian felt the Last Fare’s reading settle into a painful order. Use. Toll. Standing. The three pieces were clear, and each piece carried a boundary he could not cross by wanting it to. The token was unstable. The route might bend. He did not know whether the memory would return in fragments, or whether Dessa would remember the destination but forget the person who needed her to remember it.

He had read enough to know that certainty was not the same as safety. It was merely a more dangerous kind of uncertainty.

Mara stepped back. “The charter is open. Anyone who understands the terms may refuse. No one may be added after the bearer takes the case.”

The crowd did not surge this time. They watched the school children watch the bus. A few people began to count the spaces, the boxes, the hands that could carry them. The work had become ordinary enough to be frightening.

Tarin picked up the glass case. The Last Fare weighed almost nothing in his palm. He placed it in the bus’s route-control slot, just above the old fare box. The slot had been empty since the depot stopped running trains, but the metal teeth fit the token as if the bus had been waiting for it.

Adrian stepped onto the tram rail. The route began at Lot 17, ran beneath the tram wall, and found the St. Orra line. He placed one hand on the fare box and read the term again.

“Use: one route across St. Orra for twelve minutes.”

The depot lamps dimmed. Rain ran sideways across the windows. A dark line opened outside, not a road this time but the space where a road had been agreed to exist.

Mara looked at him. “If you cannot close it, say so.”

“I cannot predict it.”

“Then say the uncertainty.”

“I cannot predict whether every traveler will reach the marked return. I cannot guarantee the token will hold for twelve minutes or that another activation will be possible after this one; repeated use may strand the route. I cannot promise that a person who forgets the purpose will choose the correct next action.”

“Good.”

“I can state the use, the immediate toll, and the standing. The route may still fail.”

“Then let them decide.”

The witnesses accepted again after the final disclosure. Alma and Ewan spoke their names. Tarin took the driver’s seat. Dessa climbed aboard and repeated Ward Nine under her breath, the reason attached to the place.

The medicine boxes went into the rear bay. The children boarded with their shoes in their hands. One more passenger refused and stepped away. Adrian marked the refusal on the public board and did not offer a private alternative.

Tarin closed the bus door.

“Last Fare,” Adrian said. “One use. One term.”

The token turned in the slot. The dark line outside widened. The bus rolled into it.

For the first minute, the depot heard only rain.

On the second, the engine note rose and fell as if the bus were traveling over a road remembered from below. A child pressed her forehead to the glass. Another asked her steward where they were going. Alma answered with the same words the record had given her, and the child seemed satisfied for the moment.

At the third minute, the road bent. The far bank appeared at the wrong height, as if the river wall had been moved aside by a careless hand. Tarin corrected. The bus climbed through a street that did not exist on any map, then dropped onto a service ramp and found the far platform.

The school children crossed first, guided by their escorts. The medicine crew followed with the cold boxes. Adrian kept his hand on the fare box and felt the route pull against his mind. The memory toll came in a series of small, hard knocks: he could remember the clinic, the children, the term, and the reason for the crossing, but each fact arrived with a gap around it. The gaps widened.

Tarin and Dessa returned through the opening. The bus came back toward the old bell post, its lights appearing one by one through the rain. The Ward Nine shelter steward waited beneath the broken canopy.

Eight minutes remained.

Tarin brought the bus onto the marked spur and stopped at the platform. He kept one hand on the wheel while the engine cooled. He knew how to bring the bus to the marked place. He did not know why he had carried the medicine across. Dessa stepped down first. She looked at the shelter, the old tram rails, and the people waiting beneath the canopy.

“I know this place,” she said.

Adrian’s hand tightened on the fare box. The token’s rim gave a dry, small click.

Dessa frowned.

“I know this place,” she said again. “Why am I here?”

The route closed before the twelfth minute. The dark line vanished. The token remained in the slot, cracked through one side, with a bright hairline reaching toward its center.

A Warden reached the bus with insulated tongs and lifted the token free. He placed it in the glass case, and Mara recorded the handoff in the public book: **release complete, no private transfer.** Adrian signed beneath her entry. Selik’s Wardens marked the platform. Orren copied the mark. Alma Dren counted the children and did not count the names.

The medicine had crossed. The school shelter had crossed. The return had ended at the promised point.

The loss was already walking away from the bus.
