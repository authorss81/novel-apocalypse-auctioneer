# Chapter 0951: The TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-FIFTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month At About One The Man Of About Forty-Eight Who Keeps A Tally Said One Reason Out Loud About Himself And Said He Was Not Going To Say It Again And Nobody Asked Him For It And The Page At The Back Carries Forty-Seven Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and ninety days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and forty-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and sixty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and seventy-nine days. Eight hundred and forty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and fifty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and fifty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and twenty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and thirty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and sixty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and ninety-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and one reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back of that lot book took one line for it this morning and the line carries the morning and not the reason, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-five mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and sixty-four. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifteen mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and thirty-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and thirty-fourth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries forty-seven lines and it did not carry a forty-eighth this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and seventy-sixth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and ninetieth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and eighty-nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and eightieth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the ninety-first morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and fifty days, the page at the back took one line this morning and the morning was read out loud before it was written, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said a figure out loud before the clerk's hand had moved, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

The boards went up after the light found the eleven feet and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank at the hour they come down. At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and put his hand flat on the stone afterwards and left it there a while, and nobody said one word to him.

---

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said a figure out loud, and he said it before the clerk's hand had moved.

"One thousand and ninety-four," he said, at the near end of the first table.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him and about four of them said it after him out of step with each other, and nobody asked him what it was a figure of, and the turner had his wheel going with a piece of ash in it that he had not picked out yet, and the man who digs loam was at the north end with his barrow standing on edge beside him, and the man of about thirty-two put his table up about four feet along from the second table, squared it with the back of his heel and put his apron on.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a boy of seventeen has gone on giving that yard a figure before the clerk's hand has moved, and that about four of them have not said what he takes the number out of, and that about four of them have not said why he gives it before she has written anything.

---

The man of about thirty-four stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hands behind his back and said nothing out loud at all in the first hour of it. The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing, the way he comes down, and went to about four feet off the near end of the first table and worked at the end of it with his short-handled thing, and he did not look up that lane once.

---

The turner's wheel did not change speed all morning and the man who digs loam opened the same wet strip at the north end and put the spade in and out of the same place and neither of them said one word. The man of about thirty-two went along the top of his own table with the cloth. The man at the end of the second table got up, went round the end of that table and put his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks and left it there a while and sat down again.

---

And at about one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said it, once, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, into about four hundred yards of air, in about nineteen people's hearing, and he said it to the yard and not to anybody in it, and what he said was about himself and not about the work he does with his hands.

"I have kept that tally nine years," he said, "and nobody has ever come and asked me what it is a tally of. That is the whole of what I have got this morning and I am not going to say it a second time, and nobody has asked me for it this morning and nobody is going to ask me for it on any of the mornings after this one."

---

And nobody in that yard asked him for it, and nobody asked him whether it was already written down somewhere, and nobody said it for him, and nobody answered him, and nobody thanked him for saying it, and the turner's wheel went on turning and the man who digs loam went on shovelling and the man of thirty-eight went on working at the end of his own table and did not turn round, and the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall again at about four and did not look over at the boards when he had said them.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who keeps a tally stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table at about one this morning and said one thing about himself and then said his number half an hour later, and that about four of them have said he did not say the two things in the same voice, and that about four of them have not said what a tally of mornings is a tally of.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along, which is what he does, and what he had said at about one was still going round that yard when he said it.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near the man at the foot of that wall, and he was not asked one question, which is the ninety-first morning running of that.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and twenty-fourth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

And then the clerk read the morning out loud, and she read it before she wrote anything, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, to about nineteen people at the foot of that bank, and she read it once.

"The two hundred and thirty-fifth morning," she said. "One reason was said out loud in this yard this morning and it is being written on the page at the back of that book, and the reason is not going on it, and that is the whole of it."

---

And she wrote one line on the page at the back of that lot book, in her own hand, and the line carries the morning and not the reason, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a clerk write one line for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and that page carries forty-seven lines and not one of the forty-seven is in the lot book and not one of them says what the man who keeps a tally said at about one.

---

Nobody thanked the man who said the reason and nobody thanked the clerk who wrote the line and nobody thanked the man of thirty-eight for being at the end of the first table all morning. At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up, and the boards went down the way they go down.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a page at the back of a lot book has carried forty-seven lines since yesterday afternoon and that the forty-seventh carries the morning and not the reason, and that about four of them have said the reason was said by a man who keeps a tally and not by the man who works at the near end of the first table, and that about four of them have not said what a line goes on a page for.

The page at the back of that lot book carries forty-seven lines and it did not carry a forty-eighth this morning and not one of the forty-seven is in the lot book, and three of the first thirty-three were lines nobody in this district heard read. Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is seven and it did not go back this morning. This district has made fourteen things and the fourteen did not move. Five is what this district does not have and the fifth of the five is unpaid on every morning of this stretch, and a line on the back of that book is not a payment of it. A sheet at a gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and by how much that figure is out has never been done by anybody in this basin. The lane count is thirty and it did not move, and a morning that goes like the others is not a page coming down that lane, and the measure of this stretch does not go up that lane at all. The toll is ninety-eight and it is spent and it may not be raised or lowered or paid twice. The ground at the foot of that low wall has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and a public lot is not a payment of the fifth of the five. The bid was appended at four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot and it will not be. The heading over the one name in that column still says whoever and the name does not satisfy the rule and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the space on the form that came down that lane is a different space on a different piece of paper and it is empty as well, and nobody in that yard has filled either one of them. The record has stood one hundred and fifty days and nobody in that yard asked what it is one hundred and fifty days of. Nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything this morning and one reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and one line was written on that page carrying the morning and not the reason and not the question, and a man of thirty-eight has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three and has not been told he is a good man, and a man who keeps a tally said one thing about himself this morning and a line went on that page carrying the morning and not the thing, and the page at the back of that lot book is at forty-seven lines and the lane count is thirty and it did not move.
