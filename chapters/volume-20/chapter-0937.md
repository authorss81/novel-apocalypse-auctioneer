# Chapter 0937: The TWO HUNDRED AND TWENTY-FIRST Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month At About Eleven The Man Of About Thirty-Four Said One Reason Out Loud Once In The Ordinary Voice And It Was About Himself And Not About His Work And Nobody Asked Him For It And Nobody Said It For Him And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud Before She Wrote It And Wrote One Line Carrying The Morning And Not The Reason And The Page At The Back Carries Forty-Five Lines And Nobody Was Thanked And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and seventy-six days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and thirty-five. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and fifty-one days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and sixty-five days. Eight hundred and twenty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and forty days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and forty-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifteen days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighteen days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and fifty-four days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and ninety-four marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and eighty marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and one reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back of that lot book took one line for it this morning and the line carries the morning and not the reason, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and fifty-one mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and fifty. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and one mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and twenty-one, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and twentieth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries forty-five lines and it did not carry a forty-sixth this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and sixty-second of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and seventy-sixth night of that run, having slept on five hundred and seventy-five of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and sixty-sixth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the seventy-seventh morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and thirty-six days, the page at the back took one line this morning and the morning was read out loud before it was written, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said no figure, and said so out loud, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

The boards went up after the light found the eleven feet and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank at the hour they come down. At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and put his hand flat on the stone afterwards and left it there a while.

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said no figure, and said so out loud, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him. The turner had his wheel going with a piece of ash in it. The man who digs loam was at the north end. The man of about thirty-two put his table up about four feet along from the second table and squared it with the back of his heel and did not look at anybody.

---

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at the near end of the first table. The man of about thirty-four stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hands behind his back and did not say one word out loud this morning until about eleven.

---

And at about eleven he said it, once, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in about nineteen people's hearing, and he said it to the boards and not to anybody, and what he said was about him and not about the work he does with his hands.

"I stood at the end of this table this morning because I could not make myself go home," he said. "That is the whole of it. I have not said that to my wife and I am not going to say it to her in that house either, because in that house I am the one who is standing at the end of a table. Out here I am the one standing at the end of a table as well, and out here nobody wants anything off me, and that is the whole difference between the two places and it has taken me most of a winter to be able to say a thing that small out loud in a yard."

---

And his hands came down off his back and went into his pockets and stayed there. Nobody in that yard asked him for it. Nobody asked him to say it again. Nobody said it for him, and nobody said one word back to him of any kind, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood in that yard and not one of them opened their mouth, and the turner's wheel went on turning and the man who digs loam went on shovelling and the man of about thirty-two went along the top of his own table with the cloth.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man said a small thing out loud this morning at about four feet off the near end of the second table and that about nine people were standing close enough to have heard every word of it, and that nobody in that yard has asked him one word about it since, and that about four of them have not said how a man gets used to having said a thing.

---

The man of thirty-four did not move off his four feet of that table for the rest of the morning. He put his hands back behind him at about half past eleven and he stood with them there and he looked at the boards and once, at about one, he put his hand flat on the top of the second table the way the man of fifty-six puts his hand flat on the stone, and took it off again, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who has never put a hand on that table put one on it about an hour after he said a thing out loud, and that about four of them have not said what the hand was for.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve with her scale in a piece of cloth and weighed a bar of iron out of a basket and wrote the figure on the edge of a board of her own with a bit of chalk and said nothing to anybody, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman with a basket came down that bank about an hour and a half after a man said a thing out loud and that about four of them have not said whether she knew.

---

The man of thirty-eight worked at the end of his own table through all of it and did not look over at the second table once, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the two men in that yard who stand about four hundred yards apart every morning have between them said one thing out loud about themselves in four days, and that about four of them have not said what the distance is for.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along, which is what he does.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near the man at the foot of that wall.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and tenth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read the morning out loud, and she read it before she wrote anything, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, to about nineteen people at the foot of that bank, and she read it once.

"The two hundred and twenty-first morning," she said. "One reason was said out loud in this yard this morning and it is being written on the page at the back of that book, and the reason is not going on it, and that is the whole of it."

And she wrote one line on the page at the back of that lot book, in her own hand, and the line carries the morning and not the reason, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a clerk write one line for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and that page carries forty-five lines and not one of the forty-five is in the lot book and not one of them says what a man said at about four feet off the near end of the second table.

At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up, and the boards went down the way they go down.
