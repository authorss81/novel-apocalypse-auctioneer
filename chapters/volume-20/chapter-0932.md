# Chapter 0932: The TWO HUNDRED AND SIXTEENTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Turner Put His Apron On The Second Table And Said Out Loud The Same Sentence He Said On The One Hundred And Eighty-Eighth Morning And Nobody Asked Him To Say It Again And The Yard Did Not Join It To Anything And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud And Took No Line And The Page At The Back Carries Forty-Four Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and seventy-one days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and thirty. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and forty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and sixty days. Eight hundred and twenty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and thirty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and forty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and ten days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and thirteen days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and forty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and eighty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and seventy-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and no reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back did not move and the clerk wrote the morning on it in her own hand, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and forty-six mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and forty-five. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and ninety-six mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and sixteen, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifteenth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries forty-four lines and it did not carry a forty-fifth this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and fifty-seventh of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and seventy-first night of that run, having slept on five hundred and seventy of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and sixty-first afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the seventy-second morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and thirty-one days, the page at the back did not take a line this morning, the morning was read out loud before it was written, and the morning itself was written on it in her own hand, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said no figure, and said so out loud, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said no figure, and said so out loud, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him and went on down the yard. The boy of seventeen has said a figure out loud on some mornings and has said nothing out loud on others and has said so out loud when he has said nothing, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a boy who tells you he has got nothing to say is not the same thing as a boy who says nothing, and that about four of them have not said what the difference is worth.

The turner came in at about a quarter to nine with his apron over his arm and put it on the second table and tied it behind him in one go, the way a man does a thing he has done so many times his hands have stopped needing him, and then he set his wheel up on its stand at about four feet off the near end of that table and turned it over once with the heel of his hand and looked at it and turned it over again.

---

And at about ten past nine, with the wheel going and the piece of ash back in it, he said the same sentence he said on the one hundred and eighty-eighth morning, out loud, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in about nineteen people's hearing, and nobody had asked him to say it again and nobody had asked him for it.

"A page goes down that lane," he said. "That is the only direction anybody has put out loud in that yard about that hill and I have turned things round long enough to know a direction when I am holding one. Down. Everything else about that lane is a person holding a thing, and the holding is a person, and I am not going to name the person."

---

And his wheel did not stop for it. Nobody in that yard asked him to say it a second time and nobody joined it to anything, and the man who digs loam went on opening the same strip, and the clerk wrote nothing, and the page at the back of that lot book did not take a line for a man telling a yard which way a hill goes.

He looked at the turner when the turner said it and then he looked at the boards and then he looked at his own feet, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man who has been shut for three mornings watched a sentence go past him this morning and did not take it, and that about four of them have not said what he took it for.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

"The two hundred and sixteenth morning," she said. "A sentence was said out loud in this yard this morning and it is not a reason and it is not going on the back of that book, and nothing is going on it, and that is the whole of it."
