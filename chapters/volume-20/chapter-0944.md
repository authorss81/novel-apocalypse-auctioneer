# Chapter 0944: The TWO HUNDRED AND TWENTY-EIGHTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month Nobody Said A Reason Out Loud And Nobody Said No And About Four People At The Top Of Eleven Feet Have Said Since That There Were People In That Yard This Morning Who Could Have Said A Reason And Said Nothing And The Page At The Back Carries Forty-Five Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and eighty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and forty-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and fifty-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and seventy-two days. Eight hundred and thirty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and forty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and fifty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and twenty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and twenty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and sixty-one days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and one marks have been cut off that board, and there are four hundred and eighty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and no reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back did not move and the clerk wrote the morning on it in her own hand, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and fifty-eight mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and fifty-seven. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and eight mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and twenty-eight, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and twenty-seventh morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries forty-five lines and it did not carry a forty-sixth this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and sixty-ninth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and eighty-third night of that run, having slept on five hundred and eighty-two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and seventy-third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the eighty-fourth morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and forty-three days, the page at the back did not take a line this morning and the morning was read out loud before it was written, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said a figure out loud before the clerk's hand had moved, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said a figure out loud, and he said it before the clerk's hand had moved.

"Nine hundred and eighty-eight," he said, at the near end of the first table.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him and about four of them said it after him out of step with each other, and nobody asked him what it was a figure of. The turner had his wheel going with a piece of ash in it. The man who digs loam was at the north end. The man of about thirty-two put his table up about four feet along from the second table and squared it with the back of his heel.

---

And at about ten the man who made things round came down that bank with a round thing under his arm and asked the turner, out loud, in the ordinary voice, whether the wheel wanted a new bearing put in it or whether the ash was getting into it out of the loam. And the turner said, without stopping the wheel, that the ash was getting into it out of the loam and that a new bearing would not stop that, and that he would keep the oil going. And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that about four of them were close enough to hear both sides of it and about four of them have not said which of the two men in it was right.

The man of about thirty-four stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hands behind his back and said nothing out loud at all this morning. The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine and worked at the end of the first table. The man at the end of the second table got up off his stool and went round the end of that table and looked at the wedge under the leg that rocks and put his own boot on it and left it there a while and sat down again.

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down that bank with her scale in a piece of cloth and set it up at about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed a bar of iron out of a basket and wrote the figure on the edge of a board of her own with a bit of chalk and said nothing to anybody, and the turner changed the oil in his wheel while she was setting up and neither of them said one word to the other.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along, which is what he does, and he said no sentence before it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near the man at the foot of that wall, and he was not asked one question, which is the eighty-fourth morning running of that.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and seventeenth time running, and nobody asked him anything, and the man who made things round had gone back up that bank with his round thing under his arm and was not in that yard when it was said.

"The two hundred and twenty-eighth morning," she said. "No reason was said out loud in this yard this morning and nobody said no out loud in this yard this morning, and nothing is going on the back of that book, and that is the whole of it."
