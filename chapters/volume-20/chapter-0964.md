# Chapter 0964: The TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY-EIGHTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Turner Works His Wheel Through The Whole Of This Morning Without Stopping And Nobody Has Asked Him Anything And The Page At The Back Carries Fifty-Two Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and sixty-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and seventy-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and ninety-two days. Eight hundred and fifty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and sixty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is.

Five hundred and seventy-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and forty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and forty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and eighty-one days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and twenty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four.

Nobody put anything to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything. No reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and nobody said no, and the page at the back did not move and the clerk wrote the morning on it in her own hand. The count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, the count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and the five things are still not paid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-eight mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and seventy-seven. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and twenty-eight mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and forty-eight, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and forty-seventh morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries fifty-two lines and it did not carry a fifty-third this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and eighty-ninth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his six hundred and third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and ninety-third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the one hundred and fourth morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and sixty-three days, and the page at the back took no line this morning. The morning was read out loud once before it was written. Nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight, which is thirty, and gave a figure out loud after it, before the clerk's hand had moved, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

The light had been on the eleven feet for a while by the time the boards went up, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank at the hour they come down. At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, laid his hand flat on the stone afterwards and left it lying there, and nobody at those boards said one word to him about any of it.

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he gave a figure out loud and gave it before the clerk's hand had moved.

"Seventy-six," he said, at the near end of the first table.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and about four of them said it after him out of step with each other, and nobody asked him what it was a figure of, and the turner's wheel went on turning the whole time he said it.

---

The turner set his wheel going at the boards and it did not change speed at any point in this morning. It did not stop for a piece of cloth or for a drink of water or for anybody who came and stood beside it, and it was still going at the same pace when the light came off the boards at about half past four.

---

And at about ten a man who works at the far end of the first table put his hand down on the frame of that wheel, stood beside it about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, said nothing at all to the man working it, and then went back to the boards.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man stood at that wheel this morning for about as long as the man of fifty-six takes to say four figures and did not ask him anything, and that about four of them have not said what he was standing there for, and that about four of them have said nobody has asked the turner anything on any morning of this stretch and that about four of them have not said whether anybody would have asked him if he had stopped.

At about nine that second table was carried a foot further along the ground by two men who did not speak to each other while they did it, and the near rail went with it on the near side and stopped in the same place against the ground. The man at the end of that table got up, took the cloth off the top of it, folded the cloth once and laid it on the boards, and sat down again and did not wipe that table again for the rest of the day.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the top of that table has not been wiped since about ten this morning, and that about four of them have not said what the cloth was doing there instead.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table and said nothing out loud at all this morning. The man of about thirty-two squared his own table with the back of his heel, put his apron on, and went along the top of it with the cloth.

She weighed two bars out of a basket and wrote the figures on the edge of a board of her own and said nothing to anybody and went up that bank at about one, the same as she goes.

About four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other. He put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along, which is what he does, and he said no sentence before it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near the man at the foot of that wall, and he was not asked one question, which is the one hundred and fourth morning running of that.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and thirty-seventh time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And the clerk read the morning out loud in the ordinary voice, once, standing about four feet off the near end of the second table, and about nineteen people at the foot of that bank heard every word of it before she wrote any of it down.

"The two hundred and forty-eighth morning," she said. "There is nothing on the back of that book this morning and I am not going to keep a clerk's voice up about it. The page is where it was and the yard is where it was."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line on the page at the back of that lot book, in her own hand, and did not take a line for it. About nine people at the top of eleven feet watched her, and nobody at those boards asked her anything, and the turner's wheel did not stop while she was writing.

At about half past four the light went off the boards, and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it, and about nine of them stood at those boards a while afterwards before any of them went up. The boards went down the way they go down, and the cloth was still lying folded on the boards where the man at the end of the second table had put it.
