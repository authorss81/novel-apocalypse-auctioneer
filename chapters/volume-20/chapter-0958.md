# Chapter 0958: The TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY-SECOND Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month Two Reasons Were Said Out Loud About Four Hundred Yards Apart At About Eleven And At About A Quarter Past Eleven And Neither Man Knew The Other Had Said One And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud Once And Wrote Two Lines Carrying The Morning And Not Either Reason And The Page At The Back Carries Fifty-One Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Six hundred and ninety-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and fifty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and seventy-two days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and eighty-six days. Eight hundred and forty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and sixty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and sixty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and thirty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and thirty-nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and seventy-five days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and fifteen marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is seven and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nobody asked the man of fifty-six anything out loud this morning and two reasons were said out loud in that yard this morning and the page at the back of that lot book took two lines for it this morning and the lines carry the morning and not either reason, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid. A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-two mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and seventy-one. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and twenty-two mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and forty-two, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and forty-first morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning and it is not moved by a decision. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled, and the page at the back carries fifty-one lines and it did not carry a fifty-second this morning, and the first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and eighty-third of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his five hundred and ninety-seventh night of that run, having slept on five hundred and ninety-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the one hundred and eighty-seventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, which is the ninety-eighth morning running of that, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and fifty-seven days, the page at the back took two lines this morning and the morning was read out loud once before it was written, and nothing that came down that lane is in the lot book or on the page at the back of that book, and the second space on that form is still empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight and said a figure out loud before the clerk's hand had moved, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him.

The boards went up after the light found the eleven feet and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank at the hour they come down. At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and put his hand flat on the stone afterwards and left it there a while.

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then he said a figure out loud, and he said it before the clerk's hand had moved.

"Two thousand and forty," he said, at the near end of the first table.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him and about four of them said it after him out of step with each other and nobody asked him what it was a figure of. The turner had his wheel going. The man who digs loam was at the north end. The man of about thirty-two put his table up about four feet along from the second table and squared it with the back of his heel and put his apron on.

---

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table and said nothing out loud at all this morning. The man at the end of the second table got up off his stool and went round the end of that table and put his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks and left it there a while and sat down again.

---

And at about eleven the man of about thirty-four said it, once, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in about nineteen people's hearing, and he said it to the boards and not to anybody, and what he said was about himself and not about the work he does with his hands.

"I have been at this table for the whole of the time I have been in this yard," he said, "and every one of the things I have said out loud in this yard I said standing at this table, and every one of them was about me. That is the whole of what I have got this morning and I am not going to say it a second time."

---

And nobody in that yard answered him, and nobody asked him to say it again, and nobody asked him how he knew, and nobody thanked him, and the turner's wheel went on turning and the man who digs loam went on shovelling and the man of thirty-eight worked at the end of his own table and did not turn round.

---

And at about a quarter past eleven the woman of about fifty-two, who was standing on the top of that bank about four hundred yards off the near end of the second table and who had come up it at about ten, said it from where she was standing, once, in the ordinary voice, in about nineteen people's hearing, and she did not come down that bank to say it.

"I came down that bank this morning with nothing to mend and I am going up it again in a minute," she said. "I did not come down for anybody in this yard. I came down because I come down that bank, and I am not going to start coming up it only when there is a net to mend. That is what I have got this morning and I am not going to say it again."

---

And nobody in that yard answered her, and nobody asked her to come down and say it again, and nobody thanked her, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman said one thing out loud from the top of that bank at about a quarter past eleven without coming down it, and that about four of them have said she had not come down it to be at the boards this morning at all.

---

And the man at about four feet off the near end of the second table did not know she had said one, and the woman on the top of that bank did not know he had said one, and they were about four hundred yards apart and neither of them went towards the other, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that two reasons went out of that yard about a quarter of an hour apart and that about four of them have not said how a yard hears two things and how a clerk reads it out once.

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down that bank with her scale in a piece of cloth and set it up at about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed two bars of iron out of a basket and wrote the figure on the edge of a board of her own with a bit of chalk and said nothing to anybody, and she did not go up that bank to say anything either.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air and nobody wrote it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and went to stand about nine feet further along, which is what he does, and he said no sentence before it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near the man at the foot of that wall, and he was not asked one question, which is the ninety-eighth morning running of that.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and thirty-first time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read the morning out loud, and she read it before she wrote anything, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, to about nineteen people at the foot of that bank, and she read it once, and she read it once only.

"The two hundred and forty-second morning," she said. "Two reasons were said out loud in this yard this morning and they are both being written on the page at the back of that book, and neither reason is going on it, and that is the whole of it."

And she wrote two lines on the page at the back of that lot book, in her own hand, one under the other, and both of them carry the morning and neither of them carries a reason, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a clerk write two lines for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and that page carries fifty-one lines and not one of the fifty-one is in the lot book.

Nobody thanked the man at the boards and nobody thanked the woman on the bank and nobody thanked the clerk who wrote the two lines. At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up, and the boards went down the way they go down.

Seven is what a clerk has counted and it is seven and it did not go back this morning. This district has made fourteen things and the fourteen did not move.
