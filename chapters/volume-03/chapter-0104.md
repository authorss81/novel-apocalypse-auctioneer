# Chapter 104: One Hundred and Eighty-Three

A man who does not read figures counted two hundred coppers out of a pail of change into a tin on the seventh of last month, and he was given the figure and not the reason, and he counted it twice and got it twice, and on the eighteenth of this month somebody asked him why, and it took about half an hour and it is the reason there is a page about it.

He is not a fool and he is not drunk and he is fifty-eight and he has carried water for the ninth place for about nineteen years and he is one of the four households on that ground. His name is in a rate book and the two hundred coppers came out of the rate for the ninth place, fifty coppers a quarter over four thousand and one households, and **six of it went on lettering, eleven on a bound copy of the line book, and a hundred and eighty-three on nothing.**

"What did you do with the rest."

"I put it back in the tin," he said, "**and I put the tin under the bed, and then in the third week of last month a clerk came up the bank and asked me for the tin and I gave it to her and she counted it and said there was a hundred and eighty-three on nothing and she wrote that down and I said that was a lot for nothing and she said it was, and then she asked me what I thought it was for and I said I did not know, and she wrote that down too.**"

"And you gave it back."

"**I gave it back. I have still got the tin. I have had the tin under a bed since the seventh of last month and I have not counted it again because I was afraid of counting it wrong.**"

Then he said the thing that nobody in that room had asked him for, and it is in the minute in his own words and the minute says he said it without being asked and that this is the fifth time in nine weeks that has happened to somebody in a yard.

"**I did not know it was a lot. That is the whole of it and it is about me and not about the book. Nineteen years I have carried water up that bank and I have known every week what I was carrying and I have not known once what it was for, and a man comes up on the twenty-ninth of a month with a rate sheet and asks me about a platform and I said yes, and I did not know that there was a tin under my bed with a hundred and eighty-three coppers in it that nobody was owed.**"

"So who did you think it was for."

"**I thought it was for the water. I have been paying for the water out of a tin for nineteen years and I thought the tin was the water and the water was the tin, and a clerk came up in the third week and told me they were two things, and I have not been the same since.**"

---

The asking began on the evening of the eighteenth of this month in a public yard in Alder Reach and it went on for four days, and it is the first asking anybody in this district has run about money.

The finding of the eighteenth is one sentence long and it is in the public lot book in the freight clerk's own words, and he has said that he wrote it and would like it in his own words because it took him nine years to be able to write it.

**A payment reaches a person by a name and a row, and about nine hundred households in this district are in no row, and nineteen people at the ninth place are in no household, and thirty-one people live on ground held by nobody, and the three of those are three different problems and they are all one problem, and the one problem is that a piece of paper cannot walk.**

The nineteen are not in a household and not in no row and not counted anywhere including on the sheet that collects the four thousand and one. The count rule says a person is accounted for by a count and not by a household, **so the nineteen are accounted for by the number thirty-one and by nothing else, and there is no list of them and no sheet and no name, and a person who cannot be named cannot be sent a piece of paper.**

That last sentence is the tenth hand's and it is in the minute and he argued for it and lost and then won it, and the order of those two things is in the minute.

"I said on the eighteenth that you cannot send a paper to a person you cannot find," he said, "**and about nineteen people told me that I had said a thing they already knew, and then the paperer said that not being able to find them was a different problem from not being able to name them, and she was right, and I had put the two in one sentence and I have been doing that all summer.**"

---

Then the party went up the bank on the morning of the nineteenth and it is five and a quarter hours to the ninth place in ordinary weather with four changeovers, and it took six hours and a quarter in the wet because it had rained on the night of the eighteenth, and the paperer put her own figure on a sheet and the figure is six and a quarter going and five and a half coming and eleven and three-quarters in the day, and the eleven and three-quarters is ten and three-quarters of walking and an hour of standing still.

Four households came as four households. Nineteen came as nineteen. The nineteen were counted at the door by the tenth hand with a pencil on the back of a sheet, and nobody in that room knew that was a count until he said it out loud, and he said it out loud before he did it and not after.

"You are all being entered as thirty-one," he said, "**and there are four of you who are households and there are nineteen of you who are not, and I am entering those as two things and not one, because if I enter them as one then a stranger can take this entry cold and read one number, and the one number is a household, and a household is the thing this whole bank was built not to be.**"

The asking about a slip took two days and it was not a slip in a book. It was a question asked of each person separately, in a front room, in about two hours on the nineteenth and about three on the twentieth, and the twenty-second of the four days is the day the slip itself was made and the twenty-first is the day twelve people were found who had not been counted in either column.

---

The first proposal was a slip of paper and it was proposed by a paperer of about thirty-eight who buys old paper off four houses once a month and who has been going up that bank for nine years for her own trade and has asked for a document of this district to be read out in a room more times than anybody alive.

A slip is about two inches by four. It carries a place to go to, a thing to be given, and the name of a person to ask for.

"It does not need the name," she said. "**It needs a description. A description of where a person sleeps and what they are called by the people who live near them, and any person at that address can be handed the slip and the slip does the rest.**"

It is a good instrument. It took about four minutes to make on the evening of the nineteenth in a counting room over a chandler's shop, and it was made out of a sheet cut down the middle with a pair of scissors, and there are about eleven of them in a folder, and the folder is in the ward market counting room, and the folder has a heading on it that says SLIPS.

It was refused by nine of nineteen people on the twentieth of this month in about two hours, and the ninth refusal is the one that matters, and three of the ten who said yes said something that is in the book.

One of the ten is a man of about fifty-one who supplies the Harrow engine's oil out of her own till and who has asked twice in two rooms that it be said that nobody asked her either. She said yes in about two seconds and then asked for a thing, and the thing she asked for is in the minute and it is eleven words.

"**Put on it that I did not ask for it, and let anybody use that against me.**"

One of the ten is a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing, and he said yes and then asked whether the slip was the same in four years, and a clerk said that the district had no instrument that could answer that, and he said that he would take it anyway, and the entry says that a man who asked a question he knew could not be answered and took the thing anyway is the reason the instrument is going to be used at all.

The man of about forty-one who has been at that place three years and is not on any roll said no, and he is the ninth refusal, and it is in the minute in his own words, and he said the same thing to a paperer of about sixty with a stick on the twentieth and she wrote it down, and the two entries are word for word the same because he asked her to write it down and then he said it again.

"**A slip with my description on it is a thing a stranger can carry to a door. A name is a thing a person hides behind. A description is a thing a person is caught with, and I have been caught with one before and it was a water office man in the sixth year and I was living on somebody else's ground at the time and it was over in about eleven minutes and it was not the water office man's fault.**"

"**And if I take the slip I have to keep it, and if I keep it I have to hide it, and a man who is hiding a piece of paper in this district is a man who has been given a job of keeping something quiet, and nobody has asked me whether I will do that and nobody is going to.**"

The clerk of nineteen years asked him one question afterwards and it is the only question anybody asked him in nine weeks, and it is in the minute.

"**If we do not write a description, what do we write.**"

"**Nothing, and I will tell you what to write instead. Write that a hundred and eighty-three coppers is owed to somebody at this place and that somebody is not known. A stranger who reads that cannot go to a door with it. He can come to the board and ask a man, and a man is a person and a person can say no to him.**"

---

**And the finding of the twentieth of this month is that a description is not a name and a slip is a receipt and not an answer, and that the ninth refusal out of nine was not about being found and was about being given a job.**

The count is entered as four counts and not as one, because entering them as one number would have been a household, and this district has learned that twice now and is not going to learn it a third time by accident.

> **AT THE NINTH PLACE ON THE NINETEENTH AND TWENTIETH OF JULY. FOUR HOUSEHOLDS WERE ASKED ABOUT A SLIP AND FOUR SAID THEY WOULD TAKE IT. NINETEEN PEOPLE WHO ARE NOT IN A HOUSEHOLD WERE ASKED SEPARATELY. TEN SAID THEY WOULD TAKE ONE AND NINE SAID NO. EVERY ONE OF THE NINE GAVE A REASON AND NOBODY WAS ASKED FOR A SECOND REASON. NONE OF THE NINE IS NAMED IN THIS PAGE AND ONE OF THEM ASKED FOR THAT.**

Nobody argued about the four and ten and nine and a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing asked the obvious question in about four seconds, and the answer went on the board at the ninth place in chalk as the twenty-third sentence anybody has written on it.

"You have got four and ten, that is fourteen people who will take a slip and five who will not, **and there are thirty-one people there, and where are the other twelve.**"

They found the twelve on the twenty-first and it took about half a day, and the finding is that nobody had counted the people who are not in a household and not in no row, because that is a third thing and there are only two columns in the form.

"That is the third time in nine weeks that a form has been the answer to a question about people," said the paperer, "**and the forms have twenty-nine columns and nine columns and I do not know how many in the third one, and none of them has got a column for a person who is not in a row and not in a household and not asking anything.**"

"And the twelve are not missing," said the man who keeps the board, from a doorway. "**The twelve are asleep. It is about two in the afternoon and the twelve are asleep because they worked last night, and there are five of them in the second house and the second house has a room in it that is not on any plan.**"

Nobody wrote that down for about four minutes and then a clerk wrote it down and the entry says that the finding of the twenty-first is that eleven of the twelve were found in about an hour and that the twelfth was a man of about nineteen who had been awake the whole time and had not answered because he had not understood that anybody was asking him, and that he was asked again in about four words and said yes, and that not understanding a question is not a refusal and is in the count as not asked.

"Then the count of not-askings is five," said the tenth hand, from the far end of the room, **and he was the only person in that room who had said the number out loud, and he said it twice.**

"No it is not," said the clerk of nineteen years. "**Not asked is for the woman of forty-five and it is a column and not a protection and it is not a bucket, and a boy who was asleep at two in the afternoon is a boy who was asleep, and if I put him in that column then that column becomes a place where this district puts people it did not reach.**"

**Nobody argued with her, and the count of not-askings stands at four with one standing refusal, and the registrar entered on the twenty-first of July that she had been asked to put a fifth one in and had refused, and the reason she gave is that a column is a column and not a bucket.**

The phrase *a count is a column and not a protection* is now on two pages in the public lot book and it has been on a wall since the seventh of this month, and nobody has ever asked her for the second page and she has never offered it.

---

And on the twenty-first of this month a man was asked to do the one thing he can do, and he could not do it, and the reason is the reason the book has carried since the eighth of March and is not about him.

A transfer of a sum. He may close one. **A transfer requires a holder, and a holder is a named person, and a named person has to be written on a sheet, and the sheet has to be a document, and no document in this district has a column for a person who is not in a row.**

"Can you close a transfer of a hundred and eighty-three coppers to nobody."

"No," he said. "**Not because it is nobody. Because the instrument asks who it is for and I have nothing to put in that line, and if I put something in that line that is not a person then the sheet is a lie and the lie is in a book nine hours away from the person it is about.**"

"And if we make a person for it."

"**Then we have made a person, and I have made about nine hundred of them in nine weeks without meaning to, and every one of those is a row, and this is what a row looks like from the inside.**"

---

**And the resolution of the twenty-second of this month is the fourth instrument this district has built and not named, and it is not a form and it is not a slip and it is not a payment.**

The ward market put the figure in its own book.

Not the hundred and eighty-three. The whole page. A heading, and a day, and the collection, and the two amounts that went somewhere, and the one that did not, and a line that says that no answer is entered against the figure and that the answer is not to be struck.

> **THE NINTH PLACE. TWO HUNDRED COPPERS A QUARTER, COLLECTED THE SEVENTH OF MAY. SIX ON A SIGN. ELEVEN ON A BOUND COPY OF THE LINE BOOK. ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-THREE ON NOTHING. NO ANSWER AGAINST THE FIGURE. THE ANSWER IS NOT TO BE STRUCK. ENTERED ON THE TWENTY-SECOND OF JULY BY THE WARD MARKET IN ITS OWN BOOK AND READ AT A COUNTER ON THE SAME DAY.**

The market's own figure for why is the figure of a man who has spent six years saying no, and he said it at the counter in the ordinary voice and it is nine words long.

"**A figure in a book can be read by a stranger. A figure in a book can be carried to a door.**"

"And the stranger is asking what it is for."

"**The stranger is asking what it is for, and the right answer to that question is that nobody knows, and a book that says nobody knows in the same type as everything else is the only honest thing this market has produced in nine years.**"

About forty people stood at that counter on the twenty-second and about half of them had not been in a yard in nine weeks, and the reader at the counter is about fifty and is a signal clerk before her shoulder went and is on eleven coppers a quarter for four days a week, and she read the page out twice, and a man at the front asked her a question about the second line and she answered it in four words and then read the second line again.

The question was whether the eleven coppers for the bound copy of the line book was money spent or money owed, and the answer was that it was spent, and the answer is in the page, and she read the page again because the rule of that counter is that a document goes out twice and because a man asked and because the rule is the same rule either way.

And then the registrar entered the second half, and the second half is the reason the page is a page and not a footnote.

"**A line with no name against it is a column and not a payment, and it is now a column that anybody can go and read, and the district has been trying to build an instrument for nine weeks and has instead built a place where an absence can be read in public, and I do not know which of those two things we have done and I would like it recorded that I do not know.**"

---

And what was left on the evening of the twenty-second of this month was a figure that cannot be moved, a slip that nine of nineteen people refused, a form with no column for them, and a man who can close a transfer and has nothing to put in the line.

The man who keeps the fourth toll board was not up the bank. He was in a counting room, and he had copied the market's new page onto a board, and the board has one line anybody may write a sentence on, and the twenty-fourth sentence on it is in chalk and it is the shortest one anybody has written there.

"**A COLUMN IS A PLACE A FIGURE CAN BE SEEN. A COLUMN IS NOT A PLACE A FIGURE CAN GO.**"

The clerk of nineteen years read that sentence out twice at a counter on the twenty-second, and the rule of the counter is that a document goes out twice, and the second time she read it a man asked her to read the word *go* again on its own, and she did, and the minute says she did, and that the man who asked said afterwards that he had been thinking about the word all afternoon and had not been able to get at the difference and that hearing it a second time had not helped and had made it worse.

And a man at the front of that counter asked what happens to it in the autumn, and the reader at the counter said that nobody knew, and read the line about the answer not being struck a second time, and the minute says that she read it a second time not because the rule of the counter required it but because the man at the front had not heard the first time, and that this is the first time in nine weeks that a document has been read twice for that reason.

The train on that siding had stood a hundred and fifty-four days on the fifteenth of this month and a hundred and sixty-one on the twenty-second. The five dates in a boy's index still have no names against them, and the fifth of them is in seven days, and the boy of seventeen has not been asked to write anything down properly in a room.
