# Chapter 806: The Ninetieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month Adrian Vale Went Down The Lane And About Four Miles Down It And Put Eleven Feet Of Fencing Wire Across A Mile And A Half Of Nothing At A Culvert And A Man Of About Sixty-Four Who Had Walked Past That Place Every Evening For About Five Thousand Evenings Found It At About A Quarter Past Five And Did Not Take It Down And Said Four Sentences Out Loud To Nobody Because There Was Nobody Within About Four Miles Of It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and forty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and twenty days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and thirty-four days. Six hundred and ninety-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and fourteen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and eighty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and eighty-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and twenty-three days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and sixty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and forty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and a length of fencing wire is not a document and has not been counted as one. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man put a piece of wire across a gap of a mile and a half about four miles off this morning and the count of things this district has made did not move.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and twenty mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and nineteen. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for seventy mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is ninety, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-four and did not move, and it does not move for a man who goes out of it. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the man who was in that yard every morning of this run of mornings was not in it this morning for the first time since the fifty-fourth morning after the count.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight, once at about a quarter past ten and once at about four, and got them three times, and that is the five hundred and thirty-first of those mornings, and about nine people in that yard had looked at the boards at about ten and had not found anything on them that said why.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and forty-fifth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and forty-four of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the thirty-fifth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and at about half past four the man of about sixty-four went off up that lane with his coat in his hand and did not put it on, and the man of sixty-one said good afternoon to nobody, and the whole of what has passed between those two men in thirty-five afternoons came to nothing this afternoon for the first time.

The record has stood five days, and the yard has been open since a quarter to eight, and there was no key in it and about nineteen people in it and one of the nineteen was standing about four feet off the end of the second table at about ten with his hands in his pockets doing nothing at all for about the length of time it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

---

He came down the eleven feet at about ten past ten and went down the lane about four hundred yards and stopped and came back and went down it again, which about four people in that yard had seen him do on the eighty-sixth morning, and about nine had not noticed either time.

Sabra Holt came down the bank about four feet and stood on the road at the bottom of it and did not come up.

"You are going to the gap."

---

"I have had it since the middle of the winter," said Adrian. "It is in the pocket of this coat and I have not looked at it in about ninety mornings and I have said yes to it and I have said no to it and I have not done either. I am going to do one of them this morning and I would rather do it while about nineteen people in this yard know I have gone than at about half past four in the morning on my own, because that is the only way I have ever done anything that has cost me something, and it has cost me something the other four times and it cost me more the other four times."

---

"You are going to the gap because somebody put a name on a page nine miles up a hill," said Sabra Holt. "That is not the reason and you know it is not the reason, and I am not going to let you walk four miles in front of about nineteen people with a reason they will believe."

---

"I am going to the gap because I have been in a yard for a hundred and eleven mornings and there is a length of fencing wire in my coat that is for a mile and a half of nothing and I have carried it since the winter and I have not been there," said Adrian, "and the wire does not have a reason and that is the first true thing about it I have said in about ninety mornings. I have thought about it for ninety mornings and every time I have come to a reason and every reason has been a coat full of other people's trouble. It is a mile and a half and I have got enough wire in this coat to do about eleven feet of it and I have known that for about ninety mornings as well and I have not said it out loud in a yard because there is no figure in this district that a person can be weighed against and there is about as much wire for it as there is for everything else I have been asked to do."

---

Nobody in that yard said anything for a while and the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eleven, out of the ordinary order, and got all four of them.

---

At about a quarter past two Adrian came back up the eleven feet and put his hand on the boards of the second table and said one thing before he went down the bank to wait for the light.

"I want it in a yard, and I am asking for it before I do it, and I am not asking you to write it down. I have found a mile and a half with a turn and a culvert on it, about four miles down that lane, and a man of about sixty-four has walked past it every evening for nine years and has never once stopped, and I put eleven feet of fencing wire across it at the culvert this morning, and there is nothing on either side of that mile and a half and a thing that is a mile and a half long is a fact about a place rather than a fact about a person, and that is the only sentence I have got and I have had it since the middle of the winter."

"I do not know what it is for. I have known for about ninety mornings that I did not know and I have not been, and I have done it this morning, and I would like somebody in this yard to be angry with me about it in about four days when it has not worked, because it is not going to work this afternoon and everybody in this yard knows it and I am saying it out loud so that they all know it at the same time."

---

The man of about sixty-four came off the stones' neighbour at about a quarter past four and picked his coat up off the grass and did not put it on, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet had been waiting for him to do it since about two o'clock and none of them had said so.

He went up the lane at his own pace and the lane count is twenty-four and did not move.

---

He found it at about a quarter past five, at the culvert, in the light going, and he stood in front of it for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and he did not take it down, and he did not go round it, and he did not stand on the other side of it and look back, and about four people who had walked that stretch of nothing at some point in the last nine years have said since that a man of about sixty-four has never once stopped at the culvert, and that on the ninetieth morning he stopped at the culvert.

Then he said one thing, out loud, in the open, about eleven feet short of a wire he had walked past for about five thousand evenings, and nobody heard it, because there was nobody within about four miles of that culvert, and the whole of what he said was four sentences.

"I have walked past here about five thousand evenings and I have never seen this, and I am not taking it down, and I am not going to be the man who takes it down, and there is a thing about it that I am not going to explain to anybody in a yard in about four mornings' time."

---

He came down off the wire and went on up the lane and got back to the foot of that low wall at about twenty to six and stood about four feet off the place where he sits and waited until the light had gone off the boards, and the man of sixty-one said good afternoon to him at about half past three in the afternoon and nothing at all at about twenty to six, because the two words belong to the afternoon.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and there was nobody at the foot of the wall to hear them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard them, and about nine of those nine have no idea that four sentences were said at a culvert about four miles down that lane at about a quarter past five in the afternoon, because there was nobody within about four miles of the man who said them, and that is the first time in this run of mornings that a thing has been said out loud in this basin and heard by nobody at all, and Orren Pike wrote the four figures in the box on the back of his own sheet and did not write the four sentences, because he was not there, and did not write what a man of thirty-eight said at about a quarter past two, because that was said out loud in a yard and was not entered anywhere, and the box is for mornings and not for sentences.

The boards of the second table were bare where the stone had been, and the stone is about four miles off in a coat against a man's ribs, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost has been four hundred and eleven on every morning of this run of mornings and its size of error is not computed and is not going to be computed by anybody in this basin in the next four days.
