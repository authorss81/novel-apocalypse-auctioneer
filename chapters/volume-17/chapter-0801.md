# Chapter 801: The Eighty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Of Fifty-Six Read Four Figures Off That Wall At About A Quarter To Eight And Got Four He Had Never Read Off It And Said Out Loud In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People That He Did Not Know Them And A Stranger Came Down That Lane With A Page And A Boy Of Seventeen Said Twenty-Four Before The Clerk's Hand Moved

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and forty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and ninety-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and fifteen days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twenty-nine days. Six hundred and ninety days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and forty-nine days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Four hundred and four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and seventy-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and eighty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and eighteen days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and forty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and one of the four conditions with no end on it is a thing nine miles up that hill that a man of sixty-one has held for seventeen years, and the number is four and did not move this morning.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and fifteen mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and fourteen. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for sixty-five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is eighty-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and the four figures on that wall changed this morning and this district made nothing. The lane count is twenty-four and it moved this morning, and it moved because a page came down that hill, and a boy of seventeen said the figure out loud from the boards before the clerk had the cover off, and about a minute after he had looked up the lane and seen a woman standing at the bottom of that bank with the page still in her hand.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and got all four of them, and that is the five hundred and twenty-sixth of those mornings, and it is the first of the five hundred and twenty-six in which he did not know what he was saying.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and fortieth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirty-nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the thirtieth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and neither of them had any idea what had happened at the top of eleven feet at about a quarter to eight.

---

The man of fifty-six had his hand flat on the stone at about a quarter to eight and he read the four the way he reads them, which is at the speed of a man who has read them so often that his mouth has got there before the rest of him, and he got all four, and then he stood with his hand on the stone and did not take it off.

He said the fourth one again.

He said it in the same voice, at the same speed, and it came out the same, and he took his hand off the stone and put it back on.

---

"Again," said the man of fifty-six, to about nine people who had been in that yard since before the light came off the boards.

Nobody said anything, because there was no question in it. It was not a question. It was a man asking a wall to do a thing twice.

He said the four again and he got the same four and this time he put both hands flat on the stone and stood about eleven feet below the top of that bank looking at them the way a man looks at a door he has walked out of nine years ago and found open.

---

Adrian had come up that bank at about half past two the day before and had gone down it once since and come back up, and he had been in that yard one hundred and six mornings, and he was standing about nine feet off the near end of the second table when the man of fifty-six stopped, and he understood what had happened to the yard before anybody in it said a word about it, and his stomach went first and then the rest of him.

He had watched that man read four figures off a wall twice a morning for a hundred and five mornings, which is every morning of his own in that yard and about thirty more before it. He had never once known what the figures were. He had assumed, and he knew that he had assumed, that the not-knowing was the shape of a district where nobody asks anybody what anything is for. He had built a hundred and thirty-five mornings of his own reasoning on that assumption, and it had been wrong since about a quarter to eight that morning, and the reason it had been wrong was that the figures had been right every single one of those mornings and now they were not.

The man of fifty-six had not slipped, and had not lost his place, and had not been ill. He had read four figures and had got four figures and had known, in about the time it takes to read them, that he had never said them before in his life.

---

"They have changed," he said.

---

Nobody in that yard said anything at all, and about four of them turned round and looked at the wall, which is a thing people do about four times in a year, and then looked back at him, because there was nothing to look at. A wall is a wall. He had said the four off it and there were four on it.

---

"Not changed," said the man of fifty-six. "I want to say it right, because I have got five hundred and twenty-six mornings of being careful about words in this yard and I am not going to start being careless now.

"The four on that wall are not the four I have read off it since the middle of the winter. I have read the same four off that wall since the middle of the winter, twice a morning, and I have read them to whoever wanted them and to nobody, and I have never once been asked what any of it was for, and I never once asked anybody.

"What is on that wall this morning I have not read off before. I know that because I know all four of the old ones the way I know the number of steps on that bank, and I have just said the four that are on it now, and there is one of them in there I could not have said a week ago, and I am not going to say which one it is, and I want that noticed, because if I said which one it was then one of us would be right about it and the other one would be wrong about it, and I do not know which of us that would be."

---

The clerk had not moved from the near end of the second table with her hand flat on the cover of the lot book, and she said one thing.

"I am going to ask you a question I have no standing to ask," she said, "and you may say no, and I am asking it because there are about nineteen people standing here who have just heard you say that you know four figures you cannot produce, and in about four years that sentence is going to be the only thing anybody has about this morning, and I have got a page with nothing ruled on it and I have not written on it this morning."

---

"I know what you are going to ask me," said the man of fifty-six.

---

"I am going to ask you whether you will say the four you have been reading off that wall since the middle of the winter, out loud, in this yard, this morning, on your own asking and not at anybody's word, so that a clerk can write them on a page with nothing ruled on it before anybody leaves."

Nobody in that yard had ever heard a rule about that, because there was no rule about it. There was a rule about a column, and it was in a book of a Council's, and it was about a reading, and it was not this.

---

The man of fifty-six looked at about four of them and then at the wall.

"Yes," he said. "And I am going to say why, out loud, before I do it, because I have got five hundred and twenty-six mornings of not being asked anything and I am not going to be one of the people in this yard who lets a thing be taken out of them by being slow.

"I do not know why. I have got no idea at all. I have got four figures I have said five hundred and twenty-six mornings and the wall behind them is nine years old and about four thousand people have heard me say them and not one of them, including me, has ever written one of them down. And I have always been proud of that. Not proud in a way I would say out loud. Proud in the way you are proud of a thing you have not had to explain.

"And this morning there are four figures on that wall that nobody in this yard has any memory of at all, and the only memory of the old ones is standing about eleven feet off the ground with his hand on a stone, and he is about to say them out loud, and about half the people hearing me have never heard the ones that are going to be gone in about four years' time."

He put his hand flat on the stone again.

"There is a rule in this yard," he said, "that a figure may not be corrected, or struck, or replaced, or taken out. Somebody made that. I do not know who made it and I have never asked. And I have just been told, by about nine feet of air, that it works in both directions, and it does, and that is what I have been proud of without knowing what I was proud of."

---

The stranger came down that hill on foot at about a quarter to nine with a page in her right hand, and she came down it fast, the way people come down a hill they have just been told has a time on it, and she got to the bottom of the bank with her breath gone and did not come up the eleven feet, and she held the page up so that about nine people at the top of it could see it and she said one sentence from the bottom of that bank in a yard that has never once been addressed from the bottom.

"I have got a page and I am not going to read it in the air," she said. "Somebody has to come down here or somebody has to have it carried up, and I am not doing either in front of nineteen people, and whoever wants to is welcome to come down the eleven feet and look at it before I say one word of it out loud."

---

Orren Pike came up the eleven feet of that bank at about ten past nine with his sheet against his chest and the pencil out already, because he is seventeen and he has been writing down every morning for four years, and he stood on the boards at the near end and looked at about nineteen people and then said a number.

"Twenty-four."

---

He said it out loud, in the ordinary voice, and the hand that had been on the cover of the lot book had not moved yet.

---

"It moved," said the woman who keeps a scale, from about forty feet off, without coming up the bank. "I have watched that figure every morning since the seventy-sixth and it has moved twice in nine mornings and both times because somebody came down that hill with a page in her hand, and it has not moved once for a thing any of us did, and the second time was a minute ago and the page is still standing at the bottom of that bank."

---

"No name," said the clerk.

---

"No name," said the boy. "The count takes no name. That is not my rule, it is the rule of the count."

The man at the end of the second table came down the eleven feet.

He came down it slowly and stood about four feet off her and looked at the page in her right hand without touching it, and he is about thirty-seven and has been at that table for fifteen years and he is on no page in this district, and he read the heading first, which is a thing about a man who has been careful in a yard for nine of those fifteen years.

"There is no name in that heading," he said.

---

"No," said the stranger. "There is no name in any of it. That is not a complaint, that is the job. I am a hand and I would rather be a hand than a name this week."

---

Sabra Holt came about four feet along the boards from the near end and did not go down the bank, because she is fifty-two and holds the third of the nine oldest keys and has been up and down that lane more times than anybody in that yard and knows the eleven feet better than the boards.

"Give it to me," she said, "and say nothing, and go back up the hill, and I will read it out loud in that yard in about four minutes and every person in it will hear the same words from me and not from you, and that is not a small thing you are being asked for and you know it is not."

---

The stranger gave her the page and went back up the lane and did not look at about nineteen people on her way up it, and the lane count is twenty-four and did not move again that morning because a page that has been handed to somebody in a yard is not a page anybody came down the hill with.

---

Sabra Holt read it standing at the near end of the second table and she read it twice, in the ordinary voice, and it is short, and it has a heading that is about a thing and not about a person, and the first line of it is a thing, and it says that an application has been received in respect of a district, and it says that the open matter of that district is to be taken into one permanent record, and it gives a form of words for that, and it gives no term and it gives no end and it does not ask anybody for anything.

Then it goes to the officer.

It describes her as the person who made the application, and it gives her age, and it gives the fact that she is an appointed administrator of nineteen years, and it gives that she holds the third of the nine oldest keys, and it gives that she has stated in writing that the district cannot produce a person.

---

The clerk had the cover of the lot book flat under her forearm and had not lifted it, and she said one thing, and about four people heard it and about nine did not.

"It came down the lane this morning about two hours after four figures changed on a wall," she said, "and I would like the yard to notice that neither of those two things is in the other's hands, and that I have no idea which of them caused which, and that I am a clerk and not a person who finds out."

---

"You are a person who finds out," said Adrian.

---

"I am a person who enters," she said. "I have been the clerk of that book for fifteen years and I have entered about nine thousand things in it and I have never once found out one, and the difference between those two jobs is the whole of what I have got and I am not going to be talked out of it in a yard by a man who has been in it a hundred and six mornings."

---

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall again at about a quarter past ten, because he says them at about a quarter to eight and about four and he said them at about a quarter past ten in between because the morning had turned over, and he got all four of them, and then he looked up.

"I am not going to do it in a hurry," he said, to about nineteen people, "and I want that said before anybody decides I am hesitating. I have got four figures in my head that I have been carrying since the middle of the winter and about half the people standing on these boards have heard them every morning for five hundred and twenty-six mornings, and the reason I have not said them is not that I am afraid of the page. It is that the moment I say them they are on a page, and the page does not move, and I am fifty-six years old and I am not going to be a man who put the last four figures of that wall into a book and then found out in about four years that the book was the wrong place for them."

He put the pencil down that Orren Pike was holding out to him, gently, the way a man puts down a thing he is not going to take.

"Say them this morning," he said. "Not now. Now there is a woman with a page about a permanent record in that yard and I have not read it and I have not read the eleven words in it, and one of us has not known the figures on that wall since a quarter to eight. I will say them in a morning when the only thing in this yard is me and a clerk and the four of them. And I am not going to be asked again today, and if I am asked again today I am going to say no out loud, and I would like the asking entered."

---

Mara Quill entered it. She entered it on the page at the back of the lot book with nothing ruled on it, in her own hand, and she read it out loud before she wrote it, and it is the ninth line on that page and none of the nine is in the lot book.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people in that yard had been standing in the same place since about a quarter to eight, and the boards of the second table were bare where the stone had been, and the stone is about four miles off in a coat against a man's ribs, and the wall is eleven feet below the top of that bank and it has four figures on it that a man in that yard did not know at a quarter to eight and has not said out loud since.

The bid has been open four hundred and forty-nine days and was not run, and the clerk entered it on the page at the back with nothing ruled on it and read it out before she wrote it, and it is not in the lot book.

Adrian Vale was still at the top of that bank at about a quarter past six. He has been in that yard one hundred and six mornings. He has not been given a bed, a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three, and there is a length of fencing wire in the pocket of his coat about four miles from a gap of a mile and a half that he has not been to, and the man at the end of the second table came back up the eleven feet at about ten past six and did not ask him what it was for.
