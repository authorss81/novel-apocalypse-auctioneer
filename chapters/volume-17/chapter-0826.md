# Chapter 826: The One Hundred And Tenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Thirty-Eight Went Down The Eleven Feet For The First Time In This Run Of Mornings And Stood On The Stones About Four Feet Off A Man Who Says Good Afternoon And Was Not Spoken To And At About Four A Man Of Sixty-One Came Up The Bank And Put A Finger On A Figure On The Boards And Went Back Down Without Saying A Word To Anybody

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and sixty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and twenty-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and forty days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and fifty-four days. Seven hundred and fifteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and twenty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and thirty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and forty-three days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and eighty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and sixty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man of sixty-one came up the eleven feet at about four in the afternoon, put a finger on a figure on the boards of the second table, and went back down without saying a word to anybody, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and forty mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and thirty-nine. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for ninety mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and ten, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-five and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has twenty-seven lines on it and none of the twenty-seven is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fifty-first of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and about four of them have said since that a man can be perfectly right about a figure since before the middle of the winter and still be the only person in this basin who has never once been asked about one.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and sixty-fifth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and sixty-four of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fifty-fifth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one came up the eleven feet at about four and read a figure off the boards of the second table and went back down without saying a word to anybody, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood twenty-five days, and nothing was entered on the back of that book this morning, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have now read a heading that a room nine miles up that hill uses to decide what a morning in this basin is worth, and not one of them can remember being asked whether they wanted to read it.

---

"One hundred and thirty-one," said Orren Pike.

---

"Have you got it ready," said the man at the end of the second table.

"No," said Adrian.

"It is the second morning you have said no to me about it, and I am going to stop counting and I am going to start again, and I would like about nine people in this yard to understand that a man of thirty-eight saying no twice to a question he asked himself is the only thing in this place I have wanted to keep my hands off for about nine years."

---

At about one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said the number. He said it into the middle of that yard in the ordinary voice, the way he had said it every morning of this run of mornings, and it went into the page at the back of the lot book in the head of the clerk before anybody had stopped what they were doing.
---

"You have known for two days," said Adrian.

"I have known for two days and I did not say, and I would like about nine people to notice that I did not say, and I would like about four of them to notice that it is the first time in nine years I have had a figure about this place that I have not handed over in about four seconds, and I did not hand it over because I wanted to see whether a man of sixty-one would come up a bank and put his finger on it."

---

At about half past three Adrian Vale went down the eleven feet.

He had stood at the top of that bank for one hundred and thirty mornings and he had never once been at the bottom of it, and it took him about four seconds to understand why. There was nothing to be done down there. Four figures on a stone, a man who read them, a man who sat on some stones about four feet off saying two words a day to another man, and about four hundred yards of road going away into the middle of nothing.

The man of about sixty-four said good afternoon to both of them.

"Good afternoon," said the man of sixty-one, and that was all he said to Adrian Vale that afternoon, and Adrian said it back, and neither of the two men on those stones had any idea that a third person had come down the bank at all.

---

Adrian stood there for about four minutes and did not say anything and did not try to.

That was the thing that went wrong in his head while he was standing on the stones. Every one of the twenty-five mornings he had got in front of somebody in that yard, he had done it by having something. A fencing wire. A figure off a wall. A page with nothing ruled on it. Nine words he would not say. A form with two empty spaces. A strip of paper that arrived off a table with nobody standing near it. And he had got in front of about nine people at the top of eleven feet with about six different things in twenty-five mornings and every one of the six had been the best thing anybody had brought into that yard in about four months, and there was not one of the six that had cost him anything, and there was not one of the six that anybody in that basin was any better off for.

Down here there was a man who had been on four hundred and sixty-five nights of a run and had been asked three questions in his life, and he was about to go out at about half past four on nine miles of road.

---

At about four the man of sixty-one came up the eleven feet.

He came up at the same pace he had come up on the one hundred and fifth morning and he did not look at about nine people at the top of eleven feet and he did not go to the near end of the first table. He walked along the boards of the second table and stopped, and he put one finger on the sheet that was lying on them, and he read one figure, and he took his finger off it and went back down the eleven feet.

He did not say a word to anybody.

Adrian Vale stood about four feet off and watched a man of sixty-one do in about four seconds the thing he had wanted to do since the eighty-fifth morning, and found that it did not do anything at all, and that the yard at the top of that bank had not even turned round.

---

"What was the figure," said Mavis Dorr, to the boards, about a minute afterwards.

Nobody answered her.

"I am not asking him," she said. "He came up eleven feet, put a finger on one figure out of about nine and went back down and about nine people in this yard did not even notice him go. I am asking whether anybody saw which one it was."

---

"I saw which one it was," said the woman who keeps a scale, from about forty feet off, and nobody turned round because they were used to her now and had stopped expecting to.

"It was the second line from the bottom," she said. "It is four and it is the figure that says what this yard is worth to a room nine miles off and I have been looking at that line every morning since it came down that hill and I would like somebody in this yard to notice that a man of sixty-one walked up a bank to look at it and did not say one word to anybody about it, and that I have been the only person in this district who has been able to tell anybody which figure it was."

---

"Where do you get it," said Adrian Vale.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally looked at him.

"Off the mornings," he said. "One a morning, every morning, and it is a hundred and ten this morning and it was eighty-four the morning before the eighty-fifth, and I have been keeping it since before any of this began and I could not tell you which morning that was, and nobody had ever asked me a single question about it before this morning."

"You have been saying it out loud for a hundred and ten mornings."

"I have been saying it out loud for a hundred and ten mornings and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have heard it every single time, and none of them has ever asked me where I get it, and I would like that entered somewhere and I am not going to enter it myself because I am not a clerk, I am a man who keeps a tally."

"What would it say if it was entered."

"It would say that I have been keeping a number in my head for a hundred and ten mornings and putting it out loud every one of them into about four hundred yards of air," said the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, "and it would not say what it was for, and I could not tell you what it was for, and that is the first thing anybody has asked me in about nine years and I have had about four seconds to think about it and I am going to leave now."

---

Mara Quill was at the boards with her hand on the lot book and she did not open it.

"You are not writing anything this morning," said Mavis Dorr.

"No," said the clerk.

"That is three mornings."

"That is three mornings and I have known in about four seconds standing here that I am not going to start again, and I am not going to tell you the reason, and I am not going to tell you on a morning when I feel like it. You are going to have to have got on with it."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about four of those nine had watched a man of sixty-one put a finger on a figure and go back down a bank, and the man of fifty-six said the four at about four, and up at the top of the yard a man of about twenty-nine who drives the cart put a shaft down and got a cart out of a gateway he has been getting it out of every morning for about nine years, and nothing was entered on the back of that book this morning, and four hundred and eleven has been on the sheet at that gatepost every morning of this run of mornings and its size of error has not been computed, and the fifth of the five is not paid, and the column has one name in it and the heading over it still says whoever.
