# Chapter 811: The Ninety-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of About Forty-Three Who Keeps Every Road In This Basin Came Up That Lane At About Ten To Eight And Stopped Which Is The First Time In Nine Years And Said That He Has Been Asked Four Times In Four Mornings For The Name Of A Person In This District And Adrian Vale Came Up The Eleven Feet At About Ten Past Five And Went Straight Back Down And The Clerk Entered Nothing And Said Why

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and fifty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and twenty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and thirty-nine days. Seven hundred days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and fourteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and nineteen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and eighty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and ninety-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and twenty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and sixty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and fifty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man of about forty-three kept a road for nine years and stopped at the top of one this morning, and a road is not a payment and is not the fifteenth of anything.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and twenty-five mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and twenty-four. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for seventy-five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is ninety-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-five and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has seventeen lines on it and none of the seventeen is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and thirty-sixth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have heard him do it five hundred and thirty-six times and a man who keeps every road in this basin came up the lane at ten to eight and had not heard a single one of them.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and fiftieth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and forty-nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fortieth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man who put a length of fencing wire on the boards of that table at about a quarter past six yesterday evening took it off those boards at about a quarter past six this morning and went down that lane carrying it, and nobody in this yard has said one word about it.

The record has stood ten days, and a man who keeps every road in this basin was asked four times in four mornings for the name of a person in this district, and he came up the eleven feet to say that he had run out of ways of saying no, and the lane count is twenty-five and did not move this morning because the road keeper went up that hill and did not come down it with a page in his hand.

---

He came up at about ten to eight and did not stop at the bottom of the bank, which is where he had stopped on the one morning in this basin anybody could remember, and he came all the way up the eleven feet and stood about four feet off the near end of the first table with a book in his coat, and about nineteen people were in that yard and about four of them had seen him on that lane before.

---

"He is about forty-three," said the man of fifty-six, from the wall, without turning round, and he said it the way he says the four, and then he stopped, and nobody at the top of eleven feet said anything about a man of about forty-three keeping every road in this basin.

---

The road keeper put the book on the boards of the first table, squared it the way a man squares a thing he has carried a long way, and did not open it.

"I have kept every road in this basin for nine years," he said. "I have not stopped at the top of this bank in nine years and I have gone up that lane and down it about nine thousand times and I do not keep relations, I keep the surface and the culverts and where the ground gives, and I have been asked three things in nine years and two of them were about the surface."

"I am going to say one thing and then I am going to go back down, and I would like it understood that I have thought about the hour of the day on the way up here and I chose about ten to eight because about nine people are standing at those boards and I would rather be asked in front of them than be asked in a room and have it carried."

---

"Ask," said the man at the end of the second table.

---

"I have been asked four times in four mornings for the name of a person in this district," said the road keeper, "and I have said no four times, of which two were mine and two were not, and I would like the yard to have all four of those refusals at once because I have never been able to work out how I am going to say the fifth."

"It is not a form. I want that said first, because everybody in that room nine miles up that hill has a form and the form is easy, and it is not the form. Somebody stood up in a room about four days ago and said, in the ordinary voice, that this basin was going to have to say who counts it, and that a place that has not got a person to say is not short of one, it has not gone looking, and that the road keeper keeps every road in this basin and does not keep a list of people, and that he had better bring one in."

"I said no on the first of those four mornings and I said no again on the second. On the third a different person said no, and on the fourth a person who has been in this basin for a long time said no with a reason attached, and this morning they did not ask me at all. They asked the woman who works the turn at the eleventh place on the Salt Verge, and she said she would find out, and that is the fifth thing and it is worse, because she is going to come and look at this yard and this yard is not going to be able to produce a person."

---

Nobody at the top of eleven feet said anything for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

---

"Then say what you came up here to say," said Mavis Dorr, from about four feet off the boards.

---

"I came up here to say who it is they want," said the road keeper, "and I have not got a name, and I have got a description, and the description is going to be a problem for this yard and I would rather hand it over than carry it about, because I have been carrying it since about four days ago and I am a man of about forty-three and I keep roads and not things."

"They want somebody answerable for a number. That is the whole of it and it took four mornings to get it out of four people. Not a person who is good. Not a person with a key. A person that a number can be checked against, on a morning, by somebody who is not the person."

"And I have been up and down this lane nine thousand times in nine years and I know every roof in this district and I have been telling them the description since yesterday afternoon and I have got one answer, and I am going to give it to that yard in the ordinary voice and then I am going to go back down, and I am not going to argue with anybody about whether it is a good answer."

---

He turned about four feet to the left and looked at the low wall at the foot of that bank, where a man of fifty-six was standing with his hand flat on a stone eleven feet below the top of that yard.

"They want somebody who reads a number out loud on a morning and can be asked about it separately," said the road keeper. "And there is one. There is exactly one in this district and he has been doing it twice a morning for five hundred and thirty-six mornings and nobody in this basin has ever once asked him for a number, and if you want a person that a number can be checked against, that is the man, and I would like this yard to understand that I have said it out loud in about nineteen people's hearing and that about four people are now going to go and ask him, and he is going to say no, and then I am going to have to come up this bank a fifth time."

---

The man at the end of the second table said, in the ordinary voice, "They did not ask me."

---

Nobody said anything.

---

"They have not asked you," said the road keeper, "and I have been up this bank twice in two days and you are the first person at those boards who has come at me instead of at him, and I am going to tell you what that is worth, because you are about to spend it. It is worth one day. It is worth one more refusal that they can put in a book, and after that the book has got three refusals in it and the book is the reason nobody is going to come here again."

He put his hand flat on the closed book, the way a man of fifty-six puts his hand on a stone, and then he took it off.

"I am a man of about forty-three and I keep roads. I am not telling that yard what to do with what I have just handed over. I am going back down to the surface and the culverts, and if this yard produces a person this morning I will carry the news down about half past four, and if it does not, I will carry it down about half past four anyway, and that is the last thing I am going to say in that yard."

---

He went down the eleven feet at about ten past eight and about four people at the top of it watched him go, and the lane count is twenty-five and did not move, and a man who keeps every road in this basin went up that hill and did not come down it with a page in his hand.

---

At about a quarter to eight about four people at the top of eleven feet had looked at the boards of the second table and had seen that they were bare where the wire had been, and about nine had not looked, and the one who came up that lane at about ten to eight and went back down it to the same spot was a man of about forty-three with a book in his coat, and nobody in that yard said one word about the boards then or since, and the wire is eleven feet long and it went down that lane at about a quarter past six in a man's hand and there is nobody in this district who can say where it is now.

---

Orren Pike had been at the near end of the boards since about ten to eight with his sheet against his chest and had not written anything, and he came out from behind the table at about ten past eight and said one thing to a man of about forty-three who was already on the third step of the bank.

"One hundred and sixteen."

---

The road keeper stopped on the steps and did not turn round.

"That is the number of mornings a man of thirty-eight has stood in this yard," said the boy, "and he stood in it yesterday morning until about ten past eight in the evening, and he was in it this morning for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall and then he went straight back down the lane and he has not come back up it."

"And I am writing that because a man who keeps roads told me at ten to eight that he came up the eleven feet at about ten past five and did not stop, and he is not going to say he stopped, and I did not go down the bank to see it, and I am writing a figure I have not seen because a person standing in that lane saw it."

"And I have written it in my own book and I have not read it out, and I am telling this yard I have not read it out, and that is the first time in four years I have written a figure and not said it and I do not know what to do about that either."

---

The road keeper went on down the steps.

---

The clerk had her hand flat on the cover of the lot book from about ten to eight and she took it off at about ten past eight and did not open the book, and about four people at the top of eleven feet noticed that she had taken her hand off and had not opened it.

"I am not entering this morning," she said, to nobody in particular and then, when about nine of them looked at her, to all of them. "I want to say why out loud, because I have entered seventeen lines on the page at the back of that book and every one of them was a thing that happened in this yard, and this morning nothing has happened in this yard. A man kept a road and came up a bank and went down it again. That belongs to him and it belongs to that lane and it is not ours to hold."

"And I am not entering it because I entered it, or I would have entered it seventeen more times by now, and I have thought about that this morning and it has got worse the longer I have looked at it, and I would like it entered that on the ninety-fifth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month the clerk of the lot book entered nothing and gave a reason, which is the first time in this run of mornings that has happened and the only time so far."

---

"And you have just entered the reason," said the man at the end of the second table.

---

"I have entered nothing," said the clerk, "and I have said a reason out loud in the sun, and those are two different acts and one of them is in a book and one of them is in about nineteen people, and I am going to let the one that is in the people be the one that is in the people for a change, because I have noticed that in this district the one that is in the book always wins and it does not deserve to."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and a man of about forty-three who keeps every road in this basin was two miles off with a book in his coat and heard none of it, and Orren Pike wrote the morning in the box on the back of his own sheet and ruled a space under it and left it empty, and then he wrote in pencil under the empty line that a man kept every road in this basin came up the eleven feet and stopped and went down again and that the count of strangers who have come down that lane did not move, and the boards of the second table were bare where the wire had been and bare where the stone had been, and the strip of paper about the length of a hand was on the boards about four feet along where a woman had put it nine mornings ago, and nobody has covered it, and four hundred and eleven has been on the sheet at that gatepost every morning of this run of mornings and its size of error has not been computed and was not computed by anybody in that yard and is not going to be computed in the next four days, and the fifth of the five is not paid, and the bid was not run, and the column has one name in it and the heading over it still says whoever.
