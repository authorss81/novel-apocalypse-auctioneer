# Chapter 829: The One Hundred And Thirteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Of Thirty-Four Said Out Loud At About Eleven That She Was Wrong About Three Figures And Stopped Writing Lines Nobody Heard Her Read And A Woman Of Fifty-Two Said Out Loud In About Nineteen People's Hearing The Sentence This Whole Run Of Mornings Has Been Carrying And A Boy Of Seventeen Agreed To Carry A Question Up A Lane On A Morning That Has Not Been Named

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and sixty-eight days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and twenty-seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and forty-three days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and fifty-seven days. Seven hundred and eighteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and thirty-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and thirty-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and ten days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and forty-six days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and eighty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and seventy-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and the thirtieth line on the back of that book was written this morning and not read out, which is the third of those, and the boy of seventeen has not been near those boards all day, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and forty-three mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and forty-two. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for ninety-three mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and thirteen, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-six and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has twenty-nine lines on it and none of the twenty-nine is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fifty-fourth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him and about four of them have been asking each other all morning what the difference is between a question and a figure and have got three answers and have not got a fourth.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and sixty-eighth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and sixty-seven of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fifty-eighth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of thirty-eight stood at the bottom of that bank for about four minutes and did not come up, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood twenty-eight days, and the thirtieth line on that page was written and not read out, and a woman of thirty-four said out loud at about eleven that she had told this yard the page would have about twenty-eight lines on it in about four days, and that it is going to have thirty, and that three of them are ones nobody heard her read.

---

At about four the previous afternoon the man of fifty-six had said the four off that wall and then, after them, in the ordinary voice, that he did not know what any of them was for.

This morning he said the four at about a quarter to eight and then he said it again, the same words, in the same voice, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it twice on one morning, and about four of them have said since that they were not sure until the second time that it was going to happen every morning.

---

At about a quarter to eleven the clerk wrote a line on the page at the back of the lot book and did not read it out, and about four people at the top of eleven feet saw her write it and about nine heard the silence where the reading should have been, and it is the thirtieth line on that page and none of the thirty is in the lot book.

---

At about eleven the clerk put her hand flat on the page at the back of the lot book and did not open it.

"I said three figures out loud in this yard about twelve mornings ago," she said, "and I was wrong about all three, and I am going to say that out loud and then I am going to stop writing them."

---

"Say them," said Mavis Dorr.

"I said about twenty-eight lines on that page in about four days, and it is going to have thirty. I said about four of them would be ones nobody heard me read, and there are going to be three. And I said it in about four days and it has been about twelve mornings." She put her hand flat on the book. "And I told this yard I was going to have to write about nine more of those and I am not going to, and I am going to say why, and I have never said why, and nobody asked me this morning so I am going to tell you anyway."

---

"You are not obliged to," said Sabra Holt.

"I am aware," said the clerk, "and that is the reason I am doing it in a yard and not in a book."

She took her hand off the page.

"A line that nobody heard me read is only worth anything if somebody comes back afterwards and asks me about it. And about nine people in this yard have not asked me about one of them, and I have been waiting twelve mornings to find out whether that was because they had not noticed or because they had noticed and did not want to, and it has not made one single bit of difference, and a woman who keeps going at it after twelve mornings of not mattering is a fool and not a clerk, and I am not going to do it any more."

---

"And the three that are on the page," said Orren Pike.

"Two of them are yours to find out about and one of them is not," said the clerk, "and I am not going to tell you which, and that is not a secret and it is not a game. It is a page. A page is a thing where about nine people can be asked separately whether a line is there, and I have got three of them and there is no way on this earth to tell which three, and I did that on purpose and I am telling you that I did it on purpose and I am not going to tell you the other thing."

---

At about two Mavis Dorr was at the boards and about four people at the top of eleven feet were with her.

"So he has asked it and he has answered it and he is going to go on answering it every morning," she said. "And the whole of what that is, is that about nine people in this yard are going to be able to say tomorrow that a man of fifty-six said he did not know. What does it get him."

---

"I am going to say one sentence," said Sabra Holt.

She came about four feet in from where she had been standing, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet turned round, and Mara Quill took her hand off the page.

"A record that can put a person's name in a column does not have to pay that person. A person who is named in a record is not thereby owed anything. And every market in this basin has spent seventeen years calling that a guarantee."

Nobody said anything.

---

"It is not to be entered," said Sabra Holt. "If a clerk in this yard writes it down then in about four years there is a page in this basin that says a sentence was owed, and a page that says a sentence was owed is worse than nothing, because a page is a thing that can be produced, and I would like this one to be a thing that about nineteen people heard a woman say at about two in the afternoon on the one hundred and thirteenth morning."

"I was not going to," said the clerk.

"I know you were not," said Sabra Holt. "That is why I said it to about nine people and not to you."

---

"I have been on a page myself," she said. "On the ninety-first morning, in a hand that is not mine, and nobody asked me. I hold the third of the nine oldest keys and I have been an appointed administrator for nineteen years and I have never once been asked what any of it was for. And eleven mornings ago I put that key on a table in this yard and four people came down that lane in about sixteen days and every single one of them asked me what was in the paper."

"And nobody asked you why you put a key next to a piece of paper."

"Not one of them," said Sabra Holt. "And I am not going to be comforted in that yard or outside it, and I would like about four people to notice that I have said the same thing twice in about eleven mornings and that it has got less true and not more."

---

At about three the boy of seventeen came round the near end of the first table with his sheet under his arm and he had not been near the boards of the second table all day.

He put the sheet on the boards of the second table, flat, and squared it against the edge.

---

"This is the one," he said. "I have kept it four years and there is a name in it and there is a morning in it and there is a figure in it that a man wrote and did not read out, and there is a hole in it that I left there on purpose about nine mornings ago. And it is the only copy of anything in this basin and about four people have told me that in the last nine days and I would like to have it noted that about four of them told me and about nine people in this yard did not."

---

"You are handing it over," said Adrian Vale.

"No," said Orren Pike. "I am handing it to the only person in this district who is going to walk up that hill. That is a different thing and it is what I have come down here to say and I have been not saying it since about eleven."

He put two fingers on the sheet.

"There is a question in that yard that about nine people heard a man of fifty-six answer at about two yesterday afternoon, and there is no sheet, and there is no column, and there is no form with a space on it. And there is no way on this earth to get it nine miles up a hill. Except a boy with a sheet in his coat, and I am seventeen and I have been carrying it since about eleven and I have decided to carry it properly."

---

"When," said Adrian.

"On the morning it gets named," said the boy, "and not before. I am not going up that hill tomorrow and I am not going up it next week. A man of about forty-one has not named his morning and a man of thirty-eight has not named his, and when one of them names one then I will walk up it with this in my coat at about six in the morning, and I am saying that now, out loud, at these boards, so that nobody can say in about four years that I did it because somebody asked me in a yard."

---

"You heard a man of sixty-one say that on the one hundred and fifth morning," said Sabra Holt.

"I heard it and I have been carrying it about since and I want it noted that I heard it before anybody else did," said Orren Pike. "And I know what it costs, because I have been keeping this sheet for four years so that it could not be got hold of. About nine mornings ago I stood at these boards and said it was the same shape as a column with a heading over it, only smaller and with me in it, and that was true then. This morning it is a different shape. A column with a heading over it can be asked about. A sheet in a boy's chest cannot. And I have just turned mine into a column and I did it in about four seconds and I did not have to think about it and that is the part I do not like."

---

Nobody at the top of eleven feet said he was right and about four of them had something to say and did not say it.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and then he said the second thing, and about nine people heard it for the second morning running, and a boy of seventeen put his hand flat on a sheet on the boards of the second table and left it there until the light went off the boards.
