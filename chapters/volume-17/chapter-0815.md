# Chapter 815: The Ninety-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Sixty-One Who Has Sat On Some Stones At The Foot Of That Bank For Forty-Four Afternoons Came Up The Eleven Feet For The First Time In Nine Years And Said Out Loud In A Yard That He Has Been Offered A Way Out Of The Clause He Has Held For Seventeen Years And Has Said No For The Second Time And That The Settlement At The Eleventh Place On The Salt Verge Is Not Coming Apart And That He Has Never Met Any Of Them And A Woman Of Fifty-Two Asked Him Once About The Condition And He Did Not Answer

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and fifty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and thirteen. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and twenty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and forty-three days. Seven hundred and four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and eighteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and twenty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and ninety-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and ninety-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and thirty-two days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and seventy-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and fifty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man of sixty-one came up eleven feet this morning who has not come up them in nine years, and eleven feet of bank is not a thing this district made and the count did not move.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and twenty-nine mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and twenty-eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for seventy-nine mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is ninety-nine, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-five and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has twenty-one lines on it and none of the twenty-one is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fortieth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him and one of them has been standing at those boards since about ten to eight waiting for a man of sixty-one to come up a bank, and has not been asked why.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and fifty-fourth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and fifty-three of them, and there was nobody on the stones about four feet off him for the forty-fourth afternoon running, and he said good afternoon out loud to a man who was not there, and got no answer, and about four feet of stone and a bank did not carry it.

The record has stood fourteen days, and there is no morning between this one and the hundredth, and a man of sixty-one has been sitting at the bottom of that bank for forty-four afternoons saying good afternoon to a man who has said it back every time until this afternoon, and today there was nobody there to say it to, and nobody in that yard has yet worked out why.

---

He came up the eleven feet at about eleven with no case under his arm and nothing in his hands, and he came up it slowly, and about nine people at the top of it watched a man of sixty-one come up eleven feet for the first time in nine years and about four of them had known he had not been up it in nine years and about five had not known anything about it.

---

He stopped about four feet off the near end of the first table, the same distance the man of fifty-six stands from a wall, and he did not put a hand on anything.

"Forty-four afternoons," he said. "I have been on those stones for forty-four afternoons and I came up this morning and I want the yard to have both halves of that in one sentence instead of two."

"I have been offered a way out of it. Not out of this district and not out of the chair. Out of the thing I have been holding for seventeen years, which has no end on it, and about four people have known for about eleven days that it has no end on it, and about nine people have not and are not going to be told today."

"I said on the ninety-first morning in this yard, out loud, that in about four days somebody was going to come up that lane and offer me a way out of the gap where two witnesses should have been. It was my sentence and about four people in this yard have been giving it to that table ever since, and he did not say it and he did not say that he had not said it, and it has taken about three days and not four, and I would like it noticed that I have had about three mornings to think about being one day out and have not managed it yet."

---

The man at the end of the second table said, "I know. I have been letting them."

---

"You have been letting them," said Iven Tallow, "and I have been sitting at the bottom of that bank for forty-four afternoons and I could hear you up there. I am not going to pretend I did not hear about four of the things you said, because four feet of stone carries a voice when a man is angry and I have been listening to a man be angry for about forty-four afternoons and that is the whole of what those stones are for."

"The offer came on the ninety-fourth morning. It is not a document and I am not going to read you anything. It was said to me in a room by a person who has been in this basin for a long time and who has never once said a false thing to me in seventeen years."

"And it is the third way. The one morning a year when a figure moves in the open on a morning named beforehand and nobody can say no to it. It is for me, not for a man of forty-one, and I want that said before anything else, because whoever put it together thought it up in a room and it did not occur to anybody that I would say it out loud in a yard in front of about nineteen people, and that is the only reason I am standing here."

---

"I did not hear him say that," said Adrian Vale.

---

"You were not on those stones," said Iven Tallow. "You have been out of that yard since the ninety-fourth morning and I was not going to tell a man of thirty-eight about it while he was out of it. It is his, if he takes it. It was not mine and that is the whole reason I have come up here on the last morning before the hundredth and told you instead of taking it quietly and going to bed."

---

"Why are you saying no," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"Because on the eighty-fourth morning I said in this yard that I was not going to end it, and I said the same thing on the ninety-first, and on the ninety-first I told about nine people that it had not got less true the second time and that that was the only thing I had learned that week."

He looked at the boards and not at anybody.

"It is now the third time and it has got less true."

---

Nobody said anything.

---

"I am going to say the rest of it out loud because I am sixty-one and I have said no twice in that yard and I have about four years left of knowing things and I am not going to sit on a stone with it."

"The settlement at the eleventh place on the Salt Verge is not coming apart. I said on the eighty-seventh morning that it had been coming apart for about four days and that the thing which holds a settlement together is somebody being answerable for it. And it has stopped coming apart. It stopped coming apart on about the fourth day of this run of mornings and it has not come apart since, and it is better than it was, and the people there have got water in the channel and a roof on two of the huts and they are not leaving."

"I have not met one of them. I have never been to that place. I did a thing to a clause from a room nine miles up that hill between about eight and about nine in the morning on the eighty-fifth morning and I have had eleven days of the result of it and I cannot tell you one thing about any of it that I have seen."

"And that is not a confession and it is not a complaint. It is the whole of what I have got this morning, and I have said it out loud on the last morning before a form goes into a room with nothing on it, because I would like there to be one morning in this run of mornings where the man who did it is the one standing at the boards."

---

Sabra Holt said, "I am going to ask you one thing and I am only going to ask it once and I would like you to know before I ask it that I have decided not to ask it a second time whatever you say."

---

"I know what it is," said Iven Tallow.

---

"Ask it anyway."

---

She asked it.

She asked him, out loud, in the ordinary voice, in about nineteen people's hearing, what the condition on the seat is.

---

He did not answer it.

He stood about four feet off the near end of the first table for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and he did not answer it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked at the boards and about nine of them looked at him.

Then he said, "No."

---

"You have said no three times in this yard," said the man at the end of the second table, "and I want it said out loud that two of those were about a thing you were going to do and this one was about a thing you are not going to tell anybody, and they are not the same act and I am the only person in this yard who is allowed to notice the difference and I am noticing it."

---

"You have been not asking me about it for about nine mornings," said Iven Tallow, "and I have watched you do it, and I know what it cost you and I know what it is going to cost on the hundredth, and I am not going to say thank you for it here because that would be me deciding how your money is spent."

---

Mara Quill did not open the book.

She had her hand on the cover of it from about a quarter to eight and she took it off at about eleven and put it down again at about ten past eleven, and about four people at the top of eleven feet watched her do it and about nine did not, and she did not say anything about it at the time.

---

"You did not write it down," said the boy, at about ten past twelve.

---

"No."

---

"That is twice in four mornings."

---

"That is twice in four mornings," said the clerk, "and I am not going to tell either of you why this morning, and I want that to be a fact in this yard rather than a thing you can go and look for, because I have had twenty-one lines on that page and I have read every one of them out loud before I wrote it, and I am the clerk of a book and I have entered the fact of about nineteen people's mornings for about eleven mornings and I have found out about none of them."

"Today a man of sixty-one came up eleven feet and said four things and I did not write one of them, and I have not got a reason, and if a clerk in this yard starts deciding what gets written on a morning when it feels like a page, then in about four years there will be a book in this district with about four things in it and every one of them will be right."

---

"One hundred and twenty," said Orren Pike, to the boards, at about one, and he wrote it in the box on the back of his own sheet under the morning and did not read it out, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet looked at a man of thirty-eight standing at about four feet off the near end of the first table and one of them looked at the boards instead of at him.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and a man of sixty-one went back down the eleven feet at about half past four and stood on the stones about four feet off a man who had said good afternoon to nobody at about half past three and had not said it again that afternoon, and there was no line under the morning in the box on the back of the boy's sheet and he did not make one, and under the morning he had written in pencil that a man had come up a bank and gone down it again and that nothing had been entered, and that a box is for mornings and that there is going to be a morning tomorrow that nobody will be able to write down at all, and the boards of the second table were bare where the wire had been and bare where the stone had been, and four hundred and eleven has been on the sheet at that gatepost every morning of this run of mornings and its size of error has not been computed, and the fifth of the five is not paid, and the column has one name in it and the heading over it still says whoever, and there is no morning between this one and the hundredth.
