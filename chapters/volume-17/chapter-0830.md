# Chapter 830: The One Hundred And Fourteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Who Enters Things Said Out Loud At About Eleven That She Was Not Going To Enter Anything And Gave A Reason That Was About Herself And A Man Of Thirty-Eight Said Out Loud In That Yard That He Was Not Going Up That Lane And A Boy Of Seventeen Said A Figure And Did Not Add Anything To It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and sixty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and twenty-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and forty-four days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and fifty-eight days. Seven hundred and nineteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and thirty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and thirty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and eleven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and forty-seven days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and eighty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and seventy-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nothing was entered on the back of that book this morning and the woman who enters things said so out loud at about eleven and gave a reason that was about herself, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and forty-four mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and forty-three. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for ninety-four mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and fourteen, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-six and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty lines on it and none of the thirty is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fifty-fifth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him and a man of thirty-eight was at about four feet off the near end of the first table when he did it and did not write one word down.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and sixty-ninth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and sixty-eight of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fifty-ninth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and neither of them said anything else, and about four feet of stone and a bank did not carry it, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood twenty-nine days, and nothing was entered on the back of that book this morning, and the clause has been extended and not ended, and the condition on the seat is still unnamed, and about nine miles up that hill a woman on working list forty-one has still not been told anything about herself.

---

"One hundred and thirty-five," said Orren Pike.

He said it and shut the sheet on his arm and went and stood at the near end of the second table with his hand flat on the sheet that was lying on it, and he did not say anything else at all, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that that was the first morning this week that he did not add one word.

---

At about eleven the clerk put her hand on the lot book and did not open it.

"I am not entering anything this morning," she said, "and I am going to say why, and it is about me and not about this yard, and I have been clerk of that book for fifteen years and I have entered about nine thousand things out of it and found out about one."

---

"Then say it and be quick about it," said Mavis Dorr, "because about four of us have been standing here since about ten to eight waiting for a book to be opened."

"I have found out in about eight mornings that there is a difference between entering a thing because it happened and entering a thing because you want it on a page," said the clerk, "and I have been doing the second one for the whole of this run of mornings without knowing that was what I was doing, and I am stopping, and I am not saying that I think anybody in this yard is the reason I was doing it."

---

Adrian Vale stood up off the rail at about four feet off the near end of the first table at about half past eleven and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stopped, because he had done that five mornings ago and had said he was going to do something else.

"I am not going up that lane this morning," he said.

---

"You said on the one hundred and ninth morning that you were going," said Mavis Dorr. "I am not going to argue with you about the number. You said it and I heard it and that is a morning."

"Yes," said Adrian. "That is a morning and I am not going to unsay it and I am going to say why in this yard so that about nine people can be asked about it separately tomorrow."

He put his hands flat on the boards of the first table.

"I have found out in the last four mornings that going up there with one question in my mouth is the clean thing to do and the clean thing is not what pays anybody. That is what is printed over the second column of a sheet that came down that hill, and I have read it, and I do not get a morning out of it, and the man of fifty-six does not get a morning out of it, and nobody in this basin gets a morning out of anything I carry up that lane on my own."

---

"Then what is it for," said Mavis Dorr.

"It is for a woman on working list forty-one," said Adrian Vale, "who asked me what this district has got, in a corridor, at about eleven at night, in about nine words, and I have not got an answer, and I am not going to walk nine miles this morning to hand her a sentence that I have had for about twenty mornings and have found out, in the last five of them, is worth something and not worth everything."

Nobody at the top of eleven feet said one word, and about nine of them were very still.

"And I am not going to name a morning," he said. "A man of about forty-one has been the one mouth for the number in this basin since about a quarter past eleven on the one hundred and first morning and he has not named the morning he starts, and he said on the one hundred and fourth that he had found out he did not want to name it, because the day he names it is the day he begins. I have been in that yard one hundred and thirty-five mornings and I did not understand him at all until about eight mornings ago, and this morning I am not naming one, and that is all I am going to say about it."

---

The man at the end of the second table said nothing for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

"I am not going to ask you to take me up that hill this time either," he said.

Adrian Vale did not answer.

"That is not an answer," said the man at the end of the second table, "and it is not a no, and I am not going to make anybody in this yard give me one. I have asked to go somewhere in that lane once in about ten mornings and I was refused, and this morning I have decided not to ask again, and a person with a heading over him has stopped being able to ask for anything at all, and that is what that costs, and there is a figure about how much of the four years I gave myself is left and I am not going to say it out loud again, and I am not going to say the rest of this in a yard."

---

The woman who keeps a scale said one thing from about forty feet off and did not come up the bank.

"I have been on that step nine years," she said, "and nobody in this yard has asked me a question in nine years either, and I am not going to say that I mind, and I am going to be on that step at the same hour tomorrow, and that is the whole of what I have to say about a man of fifty-six saying a thing out loud at about four o'clock two times running."

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and sixty-ninth night of that run and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and they said good afternoon.

"Nine," said the man of sixty-one.

He said it to the man of about sixty-four and not to anybody else, and the man of about sixty-four said nothing at all, because that is not a question, and about four feet of stone and a bank did not carry the figure to the top of the yard, and there are nine places on a sheet that came down that hill and a man of sixty-one has read one line of it twice and has not told anybody in that yard anything about it and is not going to.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and he put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he did not know what any of them was for, and about nine people heard it for the third time running, and Adrian Vale was at about four feet off the near end of the first table and did not write one word down.

---

The light went off the boards of the second table at about half past four.

Adrian Vale stood there and did not go down the eleven feet and did not go down the lane, and the yard emptied around him the way it emptied around him every morning of this run, and about four people at the top of eleven feet had something to say to him about what he had said at about half past eleven and did not say any of it, and one of them has said since, privately, to a man who keeps a tally, that she was glad he had said out loud that he was not going, and that that is not the same thing as being glad about anything else and she knows the difference and has been keeping a beam for nine years.

He has not been told that he is a good man. Nobody in that yard said it on the one hundred and fourteenth morning, and about four of the nineteen people there had it in them and did not say it, and he did not ask, and he has been standing at about four feet off the near end of the first table for one hundred and thirty-five mornings and has not got a bed and has not asked for one.

The fifth of the five things this district does not have is not paid and the count of it is still five. Four hundred and eleven is on a sheet at a gatepost and its size of error has not been computed by anybody in this basin. The clause has been extended and not ended and the condition on the seat is still unnamed and a man of sixty-one has been offered a way out of it and has said no out loud in that yard three times and the settlement at the eleventh place on the Salt Verge has nine against its name on a sheet and a channel that four days a year is given up by about nine people nobody has paid. The column has one name in it and the heading over it still says whoever and nobody in this district knows who ruled it, and the second space on a form that went down that lane is still empty and it is going to stay empty until somebody in this yard asks a question.

He asked one two mornings ago. It is in a clerk's hand on a page with nothing ruled on it and in about nineteen people's hearing and it is in no room nine miles up that hill and it is not going to be, and he put it down anyway, and he has not been told he was right.

And Orren Pike was still standing at the near end of the second table with his hand on a sheet he had kept for four years, in the light that was going off the boards, waiting for a morning that has not been named, and neither of the two of them said one word to the other, and about eleven feet of bank and about four hundred yards of air were between that yard and the two men at the foot of the wall who had been saying good afternoon for fifty-nine afternoons and who had never once said a third thing.
