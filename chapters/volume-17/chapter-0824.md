# Chapter 824: The One Hundred And Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Who Keeps A Scale Stood At About Forty Feet Off The Foot Of That Bank For About As Long As It Takes A Man Of Fifty-Six To Say Four Figures Off A Wall And Said One Of The Two Things She Had Come Up To Say And Went Back Down The Bank And A Man Of Thirty-Eight Carried A Sheet Down To The Low Wall And Put It Beside A Man Who Reads Figures For A Living And Did Not Ask Him To Look At It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and sixty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and twenty-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and thirty-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and fifty-two days. Seven hundred and thirteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and twenty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and thirty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and forty-one days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and eighty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and sixty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a woman who keeps a scale stood at about forty feet off the foot of that bank for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall and then went back down it without saying the second of the two things she had come up to say, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and thirty-eight mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and thirty-seven. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for eighty-eight mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and eight, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-five and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has twenty-six lines on it and none of the twenty-six is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and forty-ninth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and about four of them have said since that he has never once been asked about any of the four and that they are not going to be the first either.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and sixty-third night of that run, having slept on four hundred and sixty-two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fifty-third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and nobody at the top of eleven feet knew there was a stranger in that yard, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood twenty-three days, and nothing was entered on the back of that book yesterday morning or this morning, and the woman who enters things has now gone two mornings without writing and has not given a reason for either of the two.

---

The woman who keeps a scale was on her own step at about ten and she said one sentence at about forty feet off, and it carried the way a voice carries when a yard has decided to listen to it.

"I can tell you to the morning how many times a person in this yard has said a number out loud since I came to this step nine years ago."

About nine people at the top of eleven feet turned round.

"That is mine," she said. "It is not in a book and it is not on a sheet and it is not entered anywhere and it is the only figure in this basin I have ever been able to keep by myself, and I am not giving it to you this morning, and that is the first thing of the two I came up here to say."

---

"And the second thing," said Mavis Dorr.

"The second thing was going to be about the sheet that came down that hill two mornings ago," said the woman who keeps a scale, "and I have decided I am not going to say it, and I am going back down the bank now, and I would like about four people to notice that I said there were two and then only said one and that nobody in this yard is going to stop me."

She went back down about forty feet of bank with her own two hands and did not put the scale down once.

---

At about half past eleven Adrian Vale took the sheet off the boards of the second table, where a man of about forty-one had put it two mornings ago and where it had been lying ever since, and he carried it down the eleven feet in his two hands the way you carry something that is not yours.

He went along the wall about four feet and put it on the flat stone at the foot of the low wall and squared it against the edge, and then he stood about four feet off it and said nothing at all.

The man of fifty-six was at the foot of that wall. He was not reading anything. He was standing with his back to the stone with his hands loose at his sides in the position of a man who has got about an hour and nothing to do with it.

---

After about four minutes he looked down at the sheet.

He did not ask what it was. He read it, the way he reads the four, from the left, one part at a time, and he did not go back over it, and Adrian Vale stood about four feet off and watched a man who reads figures for a living read nine of them in about a minute and a half.

Then he put his finger on the third line from the bottom.

"That is a big one," he said.

"It is."

"That is the biggest one on the page."

"It is."

---

"And I do not know where that is," said the man of fifty-six.

He took his finger off it and looked at Adrian Vale for the first time that morning.

"That is a place four miles past the ninth mile. I have never been past the ninth mile on that road. I have stood at the foot of this wall since before the middle of the winter and gone up and down eleven feet of bank every morning of it and that is the whole of the ground I know, and I have just told you which of the nine figures on that sheet is the largest and I could not have told you the name of the place it belongs to."

---

Adrian Vale stood there and found that the whole of what he had been doing since the eighty-fifth morning had just come out of his hands at about forty feet off the foot of that bank.

He had spent twenty-three mornings getting figures in front of the person who stood nearest to them. He had done it better than anybody who has ever stood in that yard. He had carried nine words nine miles into a corridor and had not said them and had walked back. And when he put a sheet down beside the man who has read four figures off that wall five hundred and forty-nine mornings, the man's whole attention went to a figure about a place four miles past the ninth mile that he had never been to.

The person who stands nearest a figure is not always the person the figure is about.

---

"You are not the first person to find that out," said the man of fifty-six, without any sympathy in it at all.

"I did not think of you as somebody a figure was about. I thought of you as the man nearest the wall."

"That is the same mistake every morning since before the middle of the winter and nobody has ever made it at these boards because there are no figures at these boards except the four on that wall and a man reads those every morning and they do not change and he is the man nearest them." He put his hand flat on the stone and took it off again. "I am not being hard on you. I am telling you what the four of them are for."

"Then tell me what they are for," said Adrian Vale, and heard himself say it, about four feet off the low wall, with about nine people eleven feet above him and about four hundred yards back.

"Not down there," said the man of fifty-six. "You asked me yesterday when I had got it ready and you have not got it ready. Down here you are eleven feet below them and about four hundred yards back along the yard and not one of those nine can hear a word of it, and I would be saying a thing about the whole of my mornings to a man of thirty-eight and it would be gone out of the air before it landed. That is the whole of what I have to say about where."

---

At the top of eleven feet about four people had seen two men stand at the foot of a low wall for about a quarter of an hour and had worked out from the direction of them that nobody was going to hear a word of it.

Mavis Dorr said, "You have been doing the same thing since before the wall changed."

"Yes."

"Getting figures in front of people."

"Yes."

"And on the ninety-seventh morning a boy of seventeen stood at these boards and told about nine people that a figure which moves every time a page reaches them is a number about us and not a number about a person, and about four people have been saying since that he was right about the shape of it and nobody has said so to his face, and this morning I am saying it to your face." She did not raise her voice. "You have been bringing figures down to a wall and putting them in front of the wrong man since the eighty-fifth morning, and the wrong man has been the only person in this basin who has never asked you for anything."

---

"Then tell me what I should have been doing since the eighty-fifth morning," said Adrian.

"I am not going to," said Mavis Dorr. "I have been carrying a question since the seventy-first morning and I am not spending it on that, and you have not earned it off me, and I would like about nine people to notice that the two things I have refused in this yard this week have both been ways of telling a man of thirty-eight that he is wrong about a person and not wrong about a figure."

---

At about four the man of about forty-one came up the eleven feet and did not go to the boards. He stood at the top of the bank with his hands in his sleeves and about nine people at the top of eleven feet turned round because he had never once come up that lane and stopped at the top of it before.

"They asked me a question in the room yesterday afternoon," he said.

---

"And what was it," said Sabra Holt.

"It was whether this district has a person in it who can be asked about a figure." He said it in the ordinary voice. "Not whether it has a person. Whether it has one who can be asked about a figure."

---

"And what did you say," said the man at the end of the second table.

"I said nothing at all," said the turner. "And I want to say the reason out loud in that yard before about nine people in that yard work it out on their own and get it wrong, because if I let it stand and about four of them get it wrong then in about four years there is a page in this basin that says a district was asked and declined, and this district did not decline, it held its mouth."

---

"The room will ask him next," said Mavis Dorr. "You know that. You have been in it. They have got a form with a space on it and the space says whoever and if you say nothing then they will go round to the man of fifty-six."

"Yes," said the turner. "That is exactly what will happen and that is why I said nothing."

He came about four feet closer to them and did not go any nearer.

"The only person in this district who can be asked about a figure is a man who reads four of them off a wall twice a morning, and he has said no once, out loud, with his reasons, in this yard. And if a room asks him and he says no then that room has a no. A no is a thing they can write in a column. And he has been the only person in this basin anybody could ask about a figure for the whole of that run, and the one thing he has never had is somebody else standing in front of that room first."

---

He put the sheet back on the boards of the second table where it had been, and squared it against the edge, because it was not his and the boards were where it had been left. Then he went back down the eleven feet at about ten past four and the lane count is twenty-five and did not move, and the light went off the boards of the second table at about half past four, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood there and looked at the place where a man of forty-one had been standing and about four of them had worked it out and got it right.
