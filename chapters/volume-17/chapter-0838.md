# Chapter 838: The One Hundred And Twenty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of About Forty-One Said Out Loud In That Yard At About Eleven That He Was Beginning On The Following Morning And Said That He Had Told A Room Nine Miles Off That It Would Be Wrong Within About Four Years And That It Was Already Wrong And A Boy Of Seventeen Said He Would Be Up That Hill At About Six And A Clerk Entered A Line And Said Out Loud That It Was The Last One

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and seventy-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and thirty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and fifty-two days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and sixty-six days. Seven hundred and twenty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and forty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and forty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and sixteen days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and nineteen days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and fifty-five days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and ninety-five marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and eighty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man of about forty-one named his morning out loud in that yard at about eleven and a boy of seventeen said he would be up that hill at about six, and the clerk entered a line and read it out before she wrote it, which is the thirty-third, and said out loud that it is the last one, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and fifty-two mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and fifty-one. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred and two mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and twenty-two, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-seven and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-two lines on it and none of the thirty-two is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and sixty-third of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard a morning named out loud in that yard and not one of them said one word.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and seventy-seventh night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seventy-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the sixty-seventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of about forty-one said out loud at about eleven at the top of that bank that he is beginning on the following morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood thirty-seven days, and a morning has been named out loud in that yard and a boy of seventeen is going to walk up that hill with a sheet on it at about six, and the thirty-third line on the page at the back of that book was read out before it was written and the clerk said out loud that it is the last one she is going to write on that page.

"One hundred and forty-three," said Orren Pike.

He said it at the near end of the first table at about ten to eight and then he went and got his sheet out from behind the boards and stood holding it against his chest at the near end of the second table, which is where he has stood every morning of this run of mornings, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said nothing at all and about nine of them waited.

---

At about eleven the turner came up the eleven feet and did not go to the boards.

"I am beginning tomorrow morning," he said.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet looked up from about nine different things they were holding.

---

"Beginning what," said Mavis Dorr.

"Being the mouth for the number." He said it in the ordinary voice and he said it before any of the rest of it. "At about ten to ten tomorrow morning I will be in a room nine miles up that hill and I will say a number out loud in that room and somebody in that room will hear it, and that is the whole of the job and it is about as long as it takes to say it."

---

"You said it would be wrong within about four years," said Adrian Vale.

---

"I said it on the one hundred and fourth morning at the end of that lane," said the turner. "I said it there and I said it out loud in that room to a woman who wrote it down and I have thought about that sentence every morning of this run of mornings since and I am going to say the rest of it here in a yard where about nine people can be asked about it separately tomorrow, because a sentence I said in a room only exists in that room and I have had about eighteen mornings to find out that I did not like that."

---

"Say the rest of it."

---

"It is already wrong," said the turner. "Not within about four years. This morning. I have been at the end of that lane for nine years and I have been reading four figures off a copy of that wall for six of them with nobody in the lane, and the number I have been reading off that copy has been the same number every morning, and the number I am going to say out loud in that room tomorrow is a number about me, and it is the same figure and it is not the same number, and I am not going to tell you the difference because the difference is a room's business and not a yard's."

---

Nobody at the top of eleven feet said one word and about four of them had something to say.

---

"You have been asked three times about whether this district has a person who can be asked about a figure," said Mavis Dorr, "and you refused twice and answered once. What made it a yes the third time."

---

"That it was a plain question and I had given it an answer twice that was not an answer," said the turner. "And that a man of thirty-eight stood up in that yard on the one hundred and fourteenth morning and said out loud that he was not going up that lane, and gave three reasons, and one of them was that a woman nine miles off had asked him what this district has got and he had not got an answer, and I read that on a page and I have not been able to put it down since. And that a boy of seventeen is going to walk up that hill with a sheet in his coat on a morning that gets named and I am not going to be the only one in this basin who has said yes in that yard and not begun."

---

At about half past eleven he turned round to the near end of the first table.

"I am not going to be asked in this yard what I am going to say tomorrow," he said, "and if anybody in this yard tries it I am going to walk down that bank and I am not going to come back up until it is about four o'clock."

Adrian Vale raised a hand about four inches and put it down again.

---

"What you are going to say," he said.



"No," said the turner, pleasantly. "That is exactly it."

---

At about noon Orren Pike came about four feet off the boards with the sheet under his arm.

"I will be up that hill at about six," he said.



"Bring it," said the turner.

---

"I said in this yard on the one hundred and thirteenth morning that I would carry it on the morning that gets named and not before, and you are the one who is naming it and I am not going to pretend to you that I would not have walked up that hill anyway, and I would like that noticed." He put his hand flat on the sheet. "What I am carrying is not a figure and it is not a reading. It is a question a man of thirty-eight asked a man of fifty-six in front of about nineteen people on the one hundred and twelfth morning and the answer, and the asking is on a page with nothing ruled on it and there is no column for it and there is no space on any form in this basin for it."

---

"How is it going to travel," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"On my legs," said Orren Pike. "There is nothing else. A page with a heading over it can be asked about. A sheet in a boy's chest cannot, and I said that in this yard on the one hundred and thirteenth morning and I have turned my own into a column in about four seconds and I did not have to think about it and that is the part I do not like, and I have not unturned it."

---

At about a quarter past twelve Mara Quill read out what she was going to write and then wrote it.

It is the thirty-third line on the page at the back of that book and none of the thirty-three is in the lot book, and she read it out in the ordinary voice in front of about nineteen people, and it said that a man of about forty-one had said out loud at about eleven that he was beginning on the following morning, and that a boy of seventeen had said at about half past eleven that he would be up that hill at about six, and that neither of the two of them had given a figure.

---

"That is the last one," she said.

---

Mavis Dorr put her hand flat on the boards and did not say anything for about four seconds.

"You said it was the last one on the one hundred and fourteenth morning," she said, "and then a man of fifty-six asked me to enter that he had been asked on the one hundred and twelfth and I entered it, and you read it out and you wrote it and you said nothing about whether it was the last one, and I have been not asking you about it for about eight mornings."

"You asked me about it," said the clerk.

"You did not answer me about it."

"No," said the clerk. "I am not going to give a reason and I am not going to give one tomorrow either, and about nine people in this yard have been waiting about eight mornings for a reason and I would like it noticed that they are going to be told nothing."

---

Adrian Vale stood up off the rail at about four feet off the near end of the first table at about half past one.

He had been in that yard one hundred and forty-three mornings and he had carried a length of fencing wire down to a wall and it had gone, and he had carried nine words up a hill in his mouth and they had not, and he had carried a page down to a wall and a clerk had read it out before she wrote it, and he had carried a boy's sheet about four feet off the near end of the first table for about nine mornings and then had told the boy to put it down.

"I am not going to name a morning," he said, to nobody, "and I said that in this yard on the one hundred and fourteenth morning and I have not unsaid it, and I am not going to change it this afternoon because two people in this yard have just named things out loud and it would look like I had been waiting."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and then he said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for.

About nine people heard it for the eleventh time running, and about four of those nine had heard two different people name a morning in that yard that day and about nine of them had heard one man name one.

---

The man at the end of the second table sat at the near end of his table until the light went off the boards and did not say one word about the morning the turner had named, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked at him and about nine of them decided not to ask, and he has been at that table for fifteen years and about four people at the top of eleven feet can describe the habit, because they have watched him do it, and he made no note of this one at all.
