# Chapter 835: The One Hundred And Nineteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month Sixteen People Said Five Out Loud At Those Boards And Three People Said Six And A Woman Who Keeps A Scale Kept The Sum Going Out Loud Until It Came To Ninety-Eight And A Man Of Fifty-Six Asked A Clerk To Enter A Line And She Read It Out Before She Wrote It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and seventy-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and thirty-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and forty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and sixty-three days. Seven hundred and twenty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and thirty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and forty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and thirteen days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and sixteen days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and fifty-two days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and ninety-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and seventy-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and sixteen people said five out loud in that yard and three said six and a man of fifty-six asked the clerk to enter a line and she read it out before she wrote it, which is the thirty-second, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and forty-nine mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and forty-eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for ninety-nine mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and nineteen, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-seven and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-one lines on it and none of the thirty-one is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and sixtieth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched a sum arrive at ninety-eight and not one of them said the last figure themselves.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and seventy-fourth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seventy-three of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the sixty-fourth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and sixteen people said five out loud at those boards and three people said six, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood thirty-four days, and the toll was paid in that yard at about two and came to ninety-eight, and the thirty-second line on the page at the back of that book was read out before it was written and is the second line a person in this yard has asked for.

A boy of seventeen stood at the near end of the second table all morning with his hand flat on a sheet and said no figure at all and would not give a reason, and about four people at the top of eleven feet offered him one and about nine did not.

---

The toll was paid at about two.

The woman who keeps a scale stood at the boards with the beam under her arm and read the shares out loud as they were said and kept the sum going in the air, and about nineteen people stood where they had been standing all morning and about nine of them watched the sum and about ten of them watched the people saying it.

"Mavis Dorr," said the woman who keeps a scale. "Five."

"Five," said Mavis Dorr.

"Sabra Holt. Five."

"Five," said Sabra Holt.

"And I am going to say yours before you get to the boards," said the woman who keeps a scale, to the man of about forty-eight, "because you are going to try to say it into the ground."

"Five," said the man of about forty-eight.

She went down the line. She did not hurry and she did not soften it. About sixteen people said five out loud in that yard in about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and every one of them said it themselves and not after her.

---

"Orren Pike," she said.

The boy came up. He did not say anything first.

"Six," he said.

He said it in the ordinary voice and he did not look at the boards and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it and about four of them have said since that they heard a boy of seventeen pay a figure he had not one single way of checking, and that is the first time any of them has heard anybody in that yard do that.

---

"The clerk," said the woman who keeps a scale.

"Six," said the clerk.

---

She kept the sum going and she said it out loud every time a share went on it, the way a woman keeping a beam keeps saying a number, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet found that they were adding along with her without meaning to.

"Twenty," she said. "Twenty-five. Thirty. Thirty-five. Forty. Forty-five. Fifty. Fifty-five. Sixty. Sixty-five. Seventy. Seventy-five. Eighty. Eighty-six. Ninety-two. Ninety-eight."

She stopped.

"That is the toll," she said, "and it is said out loud in that yard by me, and I am the only person in this district who can tell you whether it is right and I am telling you it is right, and I am going to be asked about it separately tomorrow and I will say the same thing again."

---

The man of fifty-six came up the eleven feet at about half past two.

He has not come up that bank in this run of mornings and he came up it the way anybody comes up it, which is badly, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stopped what they were doing.

---

"I have something to ask the clerk to write down," he said, "and I am asking her in front of all of you and not in a book by myself, and I want it entered that I asked and not what I say afterwards, which is what I asked for on the one hundred and twelfth morning and got."

---

Mara Quill took her hand off the page.

"Say what you want written."

---

"Write that the man of fifty-six came up the eleven feet at about half past two on the one hundred and nineteenth morning in front of about nineteen people and asked the clerk to enter that he had given six of the toll, and write that he asked to have the asking entered and not the reason."

Nobody said anything.

"And write that I asked out loud because I have been right five hundred and sixty mornings running and about four people in this basin know it and not one of them can prove which ones were new, and if I hand a reason to a page then the page has the reason and not the asking, and the asking is the only part anybody can be asked about separately tomorrow."

---

He read it back to her himself, out loud, in the ordinary voice, from the top of that bank, and about nine people heard a man who reads four figures off a wall twice every morning read out loud, once, from the top of that bank, about as much as a page at the back of the lot book holds in a line, and got it right the first time.

The clerk read it out once more and then wrote it, and it is the thirty-second line on the page at the back of that book and none of the thirty-two is in the lot book, and about four people saw her write it and about nine heard the silence where a reason would have gone.

---

"You said it is the last one," said Mavis Dorr.

"I did not say that," said the clerk.

"You said it on the one hundred and fourteenth morning and you have said nothing at all since and everybody at the top of eleven feet has been waiting about five mornings for you to say it again."

"That was before this one," said the clerk. "This is the second line a person in this yard has asked me for and I am going to say that it is the last one, and I am not going to give a reason, and if any of you want a reason you are going to have to do without it, and I am aware that that is the third reason I have not given in about ten mornings and I am aware that a woman who keeps going at it after about ten mornings of not mattering is a fool and not a clerk, and that was my reason on the one hundred and thirteenth morning and it is still my reason and I am not going to say it twice in a yard."

---

The boy of seventeen put his hand back flat on the sheet at the near end of the second table and did not take it off for about two minutes and about four people at the top of eleven feet could have said one word and did not say it.

---

Sabra Holt said, to about nine people: "Sixteen of us gave five."

Nobody took it up and nobody was asked to, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman of fifty-two standing in a yard saying a number out loud about what her own district did not get is a thing that ought to be entered somewhere and that the only page it could be entered on is the one that has got nothing ruled on it, and that is the fifth time in two blocks that somebody has said that.

---

Adrian Vale stood at about four feet off the near end of the first table through all of it with his hands behind him and did not write one word down, and he found out about four minutes afterwards that what he had been watching was not the sum arriving at ninety-eight. It was the man of fifty-six coming up eleven feet of bank to ask a clerk to write down that he had asked. Every figure that had gone into that yard since the eighty-fifth morning had gone the other way. Things went up a hill and came down as numbers, and a number went on a page and the page went somewhere, and the one thing in this yard that had ever gone the other way was a man of fifty-six walking up a slope to say please write down that I was asked.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and seventy-fourth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seventy-three of them, and they said good afternoon, and about eleven feet of bank and about four hundred yards of air carried nothing at all of the sum up or down.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six read the four off that wall and got all four of them and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and then, in the ordinary voice, he said that he does not know what any of them is for.

About nine people heard it for the eighth time running and about four of them did not turn round. One of them had heard him read his own asking off a page about an hour and a half before, out loud, in that yard, on purpose.
