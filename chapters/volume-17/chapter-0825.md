# Chapter 825: The One Hundred And Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Of Thirty-Four Wrote The Twenty-Seventh Line On The Page At The Back Of The Lot Book And Did Not Read It Out And A Boy Of Seventeen Counted The Lines On That Page And Said The Count Out Loud At The Boards And A Man Of Thirty-Eight Said Out Loud In That Yard That He Was Going To Ask A Question That Was Not A Figure And Was Not Going On The Sheet And That He Was Going To Do It Anyway

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and sixty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and twenty-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and thirty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and fifty-three days. Seven hundred and fourteen days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and twenty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and thirty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and forty-two days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and eighty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and sixty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and the twenty-seventh line on the back of that book was written this morning and not read out, which is the second of those, and a boy of seventeen counted the lines on that page and said the count out loud at the boards, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and thirty-nine mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and thirty-eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for eighty-nine mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and nine, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-five and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has twenty-six lines on it and none of the twenty-six is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fiftieth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him and about four of them have found out by now that a man can want a thing for nine years and be the only person in the basin who knows what he wants it for.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and sixty-fourth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and sixty-three of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fifty-fourth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the whole of what has passed between those two men in fifty-four afternoons is two words, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood twenty-four days, and the twenty-seventh line on that page was written and not read out, and about four people saw a woman of thirty-four write it and about nine heard the silence where the reading should have been.

---

"One hundred and thirty," said Orren Pike, and put his pencil back behind the boards.

That was the whole of what he said about it and he had said it every morning this week and about four people at the top of eleven feet had started saying it after him, which is a thing that happened in that yard without anybody deciding that it would.

---

At about a quarter to eleven the clerk was at the boards with her hand on the page at the back of the lot book and she wrote, and she did not read it out, and about four people at the top of eleven feet looked over at the hand on the page and then looked somewhere else.

Orren Pike came round the near end of the boards.

"You did not read it out," he said.

"No."

"That is two."

"That is the second of them," said the clerk.

---

"You read every one of the others out before you wrote it."

"I read twenty-one of them out before I wrote them and the twenty-second one I wrote on the one hundred and first morning and did not read out, and this morning you are asking me about a habit, and a habit is not a figure, and I am not going to answer it with a figure."

---

"Then tell me how many you are going to leave," said the boy. "You said you were going to leave about nine more. I am not asking which ones. I am asking how many, because about nine people in this yard are going to be thinking about it every morning and one of them is me and I would rather be thinking about a number than about you."

The clerk looked at him for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

"Three," she said. "I told this yard about nine more and I have written two and I am going to write one more and that is the last of them."

"You said about nine."

"I said about nine, and I did not know what I was talking about, and I have had eight mornings to find out and what I have found out is that a line I do not read out only means something if somebody asks me about it afterwards, and about nine people in this yard have not asked me about one of them, and I am not going to write nine lines that nobody in this district is going to come back and ask me about."

---

Then the boy counted them.

He counted them out loud, standing at the near end of the boards of the second table with the page not in his hands because the page was in Mara Quill's hands, and he counted them off the edge of the table with his finger going along the ruled lines that were not there.

"Twenty-six," he said. "There are twenty-six lines on the page at the back of that book and she has written one since I started counting and it is twenty-seven, and the twenty-seventh one is the one she did not read out, and it is mine to count because I am the only person in this yard who counts things that nobody has asked him to count."

---

"That page has nothing ruled on it," said Mara Quill.

"That is what I am saying," said Orren Pike. "It has nothing ruled on it and nobody has ever counted it and it has been in that book for fifteen years and it has twenty-seven lines on it this morning, and about four people in this basin who were not in this yard this morning would tell you it was not there. A thing that nobody has counted is not a thing. It is a floor."

"You are seventeen."

"I am seventeen and I have kept a sheet for four years and I have never once counted it either," said the boy, "and I have known in about four seconds standing here why nobody could get hold of it, and I am not saying that to be clever, I am saying it because you counted them and I did not and you are the clerk."

---

At about eleven Adrian Vale stood up off the rail at about four feet off the near end of the first table, which he had not done in about nine days, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stopped what they were doing.

"I am going to say a thing out loud in this yard now and then I am going to do it in about three days, and I am saying it now so that it is a morning on which somebody in this yard said a thing, and not a thing that a man of thirty-eight did quietly at the foot of a wall about four hundred yards off."

He put his hands flat on the boards of the second table.

"I am going to ask the man of fifty-six what the four figures on that wall are for. It is a question and it is not a figure. It is not going on that sheet that came down that hill and it is not going in that book and it is not going in a column and nobody's name is going anywhere with it. I have read the heading on that sheet and I know exactly what it pays for and it does not pay for that, and the room nine miles up that hill has got nine places and no names on it and I am not on it and neither is he."

---

"So you are going to do a thing that does not count," said Mavis Dorr.

"Yes."

"In a yard."

"Yes."

"In the ordinary voice."

"Yes."

"Then you have made it matter by saying it out loud and there is nothing in this district that matters except what about nine people heard at these boards, and you have just given yourself a morning." She was not being hard on him. That was the trouble. "I want you to know that I do not think that is true and I would like you to tell me why I am wrong, because I have been wrong once this week about a road and I would rather be wrong twice."

---

"I am not making it matter," said Adrian Vale. "I am making it checkable. That is the whole difference and I did not know it was the whole difference until a man of about forty-one read a heading out loud at these boards three mornings ago."

He took his hands off the boards.

"If a thing is said out loud in this yard then about nine people can be asked about it separately, one at a time, and if nine people say the same thing about it then it is a thing and not a man talking. And if I do not say it out loud then it is a man of thirty-eight who asked a man of fifty-six a question at the foot of a wall with about four people watching us do it, and there is no way on this earth for anybody to be asked about either half. And there is no sheet and there is no column and there is no room nine miles up that hill that is ever going to know that any of it happened."

---

"Are you asking me what it is worth," said Sabra Holt, from about four feet off the near end with her hands in her sleeves.

"Yes," said Adrian.

"Then I will tell you what I think, and I have been standing in this yard for three mornings not saying what I think. I think it is worth the light, and not one other thing. It is worth about nine people being able to say that a man of fifty-six was asked what his four figures were for and that he answered, in about four years, when there is nothing else at all in this district anybody can put a name to. And I am telling you that because you are going to go and do it and there is nothing at these boards that can stop you, and I would like it said that I said no to being the clerk of it."

---

"What do you think it is worth," said Adrian Vale.

"I think you have not got an answer to that," said Sabra Holt, "and I have not got an answer to it either, and I am the woman who has been an appointed administrator for nineteen years and has never once been asked what any of it was for, and I have just watched a man of thirty-eight be asked for nothing and say nothing, and that is the correct answer and it is the first correct answer anybody has given me in about nineteen years, and I am not going to be the one who tells you that you are right."

---

The man of about twenty-nine who drives the cart came up out of the yard with the shafts in his two hands and said that he drove nine miles of that lane twice a week and that if anybody in that yard was going to walk up it with a thing in a coat then he would put it in the cart and take it up at about six in the morning and would not say one word about it at either end.

"No," said Adrian.

"You have not asked me," said the man who drives the cart.

"I know that you have not been asked and that is why I said no before you asked," said Adrian. "And if I put a thing in your cart then it goes up nine miles in a cart and comes back down in a cart, and in about four years there is a page in this basin that says a district sent a thing, and the page will not say who put it in the cart, and you will have been the man who took it and you did not ask to be that and you did not get to say no."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about nine of those nine had heard a man of thirty-eight stand up at about eleven and say in the ordinary voice that he was going to do a thing that was not going to count, and the twenty-seventh line on the page at the back of that lot book was written and not read out, and four hundred and eleven has been on the sheet at that gatepost every morning of this run of mornings and its size of error has not been computed, and the fifth of the five is not paid, and the column has one name in it and the heading over it still says whoever.
