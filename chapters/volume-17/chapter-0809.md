# Chapter 809: The Ninety-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Fifty-Six Was Asked By The Man At The End Of The Second Table In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People To Say The Four Figures He Has Been Reading Off That Wall Since The Middle Of The Winter Into The Column In That Book And He Said No Out Loud And Gave The Reason Out Loud In Full And The Asking Was Entered And The Column Has One Name In It And The Heading Over It Still Says Whoever

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and forty-eight days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and twenty-three days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and thirty-seven days. Six hundred and ninety-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and twelve days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and seventeen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and eighty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and ninety days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and twenty-six days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and sixty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and fifty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man was asked this morning to go in a column and said no, and the count did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and twenty-three mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and twenty-two. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for seventy-three mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is ninety-three, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-five and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and nobody in this district knows who ruled it, and a man said no out loud this morning before he was asked a second time.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and thirty-fourth of those mornings, and it is the first of the five hundred and thirty-four on which somebody in that yard has asked him for a thing and he has said no out loud and asked for the asking to be entered.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and forty-eighth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and forty-seven of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the thirty-eighth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of about sixty-four said four words about the wire and would not say a fifth.

The record has stood eight days, and the column has one name in it and did not get a second, and the man at the end of the second table has been at that table for fifteen years and made a rule about who may be entered in it about twenty-one mornings ago and it has now refused one person and the rule has still never once been tested properly.

---

He did it standing up, in the light, at the near end of the boards, at about eleven, and he said the words out loud before he asked, and about nineteen people heard all three parts of it.

"I am going to ask you for a thing and I am going to say the thing out loud before I ask it, and I am not going to pretend I have not rehearsed it, because I have had two mornings to rehearse it and it has got worse.

"I am going to ask the man at that wall to say the four figures he has been reading off it since the middle of the winter into the column in that book that is headed for whoever reads a thing out loud in this yard.

"And I want it said that I made the rule about that column, and that the rule says a person goes in it on their own asking and out loud and in a yard and not at somebody's word, which I made in the light in this yard about twenty-five mornings ago, and that I am not asking him to break the rule, I am asking him to use it, and I have not been able to work out for about nine days whether those are the same thing."

---

The clerk had her hand on the cover of the lot book and she had had it on the cover for about nine days and about four people in that yard had stopped noticing it.

"I am not going to say no before he has answered her," she said, "because I said no to a man of thirty-eight on the eighty-second morning and I entered that I said no before he had answered me, and it is a bad thing to do to a person, and I have had nine days of it, and I am not doing it again this morning."

---

The man of fifty-six looked at about four of them and then at the book and then at the wall, and about nine feet of bank went up between him and the boards.

"No."

He said it in the ordinary voice and it took less time than the ask.

---

"I am going to say the reason and then I am going to stop and I am not going to be asked again, and if I am asked again I am going to say no again and I would like that entered as well.

"A column with a heading over it is for a person who reads a thing out loud, and I read four figures off that wall in this yard twice a morning and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have heard me, and one woman with a key and one woman with a beam scale and one man of about forty-one have been named in that yard as people I have said them to.

"And that is not what a column is for. A column is for somebody being asked. I have said them to about four thousand people in five hundred and thirty-four mornings and not one of them ever asked me for one, and the two mornings anybody asked me are the seventy-seventh, when a man of about forty-one asked and a woman with a key ruled it was not to happen twice, and the eighty-seventh, when a clerk asked and I said them out loud in about nineteen people's hearing and they are on a page with nothing ruled on it, and the page has nothing on it about who was standing next to me."

"And I am not going into a column with a heading over it for a thing I did standing up on my own for five hundred and thirty-four mornings without being asked once, because the day I go into that column the four figures on that wall belong to whoever holds the book and not to me, and I have got them and I would like to keep them in the same place."

---

"Four hundred and eleven is the figure on the sheet at that gatepost," said Sabra Holt, from the boards.

"That is not one of my four," said the man of fifty-six, "and I have never once been asked which of the four is wrong and I am not going to start this morning, and I would like the yard to notice that a man of fifty-two has just said a number at me and I have said no and neither of us was talking about the same number and about four of you heard both halves of it and none of you said anything, and that is about nine days of this yard in one morning."

---

Mara Quill entered it. She entered it on the page at the back of the lot book with nothing ruled on it, in her own hand, and she read it out loud before she wrote it, and it is the sixteenth line on that page and none of the sixteen is in the lot book, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard the whole of it read out and about nine did not.

---

Orren Pike wrote the morning in the box on the back of his own sheet and under it he ruled a line and left it empty, because the box is headed for mornings on which a thing was said out loud in this yard and was not entered anywhere, and this morning a thing was said out loud in this yard and entered.

Then he wrote under the empty line, in pencil, without ruling a space for it: *asked and no. asked to say the old four into the column. a reason, given out loud, in full. nobody entered.*

---

"I left out the reason," he said, to nobody in particular. "I have left out the whole of a reason and I have done that three times this week and I am not going to stop and I am not going to pretend I have not done it, and about four of you know why I do it and about nine of you know I do it, and if anybody wants to say that I am building a record with holes in it they can say so this morning and I will write it down and it will go in the box and not in the columns, because that is what the box is for and because a hole is a fact about a morning and not a sentence about a person."

---

The man at the end of the second table did not say anything about the wire, and about four people in that yard noticed that he had come within about four inches of saying something about it at about two o'clock, and Mavis Dorr noticed it and has said since that she watched him get about four inches from it and turn round, and that about nine mornings ago a man said in that yard that he was not going to ask a man of thirty-eight what a length of fencing wire in his coat was for, and that this morning he was not going to, and that is two data points and she does not have a sentence yet.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and the man at the end of the second table heard them say it and did not say anything, and eleven feet of fencing wire was still across a mile and a half of nothing at the culvert about four miles down that lane, and nobody had taken it down, and nobody in that yard had proposed anything about it in a mouth or in a page, and the boards of the second table were bare where the stone had been, and a man of about thirty-eight who had carried that wire in his coat since the middle of the winter had nothing in either pocket this morning and had told nobody in that yard which way he was going, and the man at the end of the second table had watched him go down the lane at about half past six and had said nothing.
