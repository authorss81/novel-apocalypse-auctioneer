# Chapter 836: The One Hundred And Twentieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month Nothing At All Happened At Those Boards Before Four O'clock In The Afternoon For The First Time In This Run Of Mornings And About Nine People Stood On The Ground At The Foot Of That Bank And A Man Of About Sixty-Four Came Onto It And A Man Of Fifty-Six Asked Him A Question For The First Time In This Run Of Mornings And Not One Word Of It Came Up That Bank

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and seventy-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and thirty-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and fifty days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and sixty-four days. Seven hundred and twenty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and thirty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and forty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and fourteen days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and seventeen days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and fifty-three days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and ninety-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and seventy-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and nothing at all happened at those boards this morning for the first time in this run of mornings, and about nine people stood on the ground at the foot of that bank at about four in the afternoon, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and fifty mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and forty-nine. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for one hundred mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and twenty, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-seven and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-two lines on it and none of the thirty-two is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and sixty-first of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and not one of those nine was standing on the ground he was standing on.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and seventy-fifth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seventy-four of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the sixty-fifth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and about nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank and a man of about sixty-four arrived on it and a man of fifty-six asked him a question and about four feet of stone and eleven feet of bank carried all of it and not one word came up, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood thirty-five days, and the ground at the foot of that bank has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there is a bell on a nail inside the hut where the man who keeps a tally keeps his number, and nobody in that yard has rung it.

"One hundred and forty-one," said Orren Pike.

Nothing else happened at those boards this morning.

It is the first morning in this run of mornings on which that is true, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stood around the boards from about ten to eight until about half past two waiting for something to arrive, and Mara Quill had the lot book open with her hand not on it, and about four people said out loud, one after another and not to anybody in particular, that they had thought there would be something.

---

There is a bell on a nail inside the hut where the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally keeps his number.

It is a hand bell and it has been on that nail since before he began keeping the number, and nobody in that yard has rung it in this run of mornings, and there is no rule in that yard about it and no name over it.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four came round the end of the low wall at the foot of that bank.

He came onto ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and he stopped, which he has not done in this run of mornings, and he stood about four feet off the flat stone with his hands at his sides and looked down the length of the strip of ground as if he were checking that it was still the length it had been.

---

The man of fifty-six was on the flat stone at about a quarter to four.

He saw the other man stop and he came about four feet along the wall, which he has done twice in this run of mornings, and he stood there and looked at him, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet had their heads over that bank.

---

"You can stop here," he said.

Four words, up eleven feet, in the ordinary voice.

The man of about sixty-four did not move for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

"Since the one hundred and seventeenth morning," he said.

"Since the one hundred and seventeenth morning."

"Then I will stop here," said the man of about sixty-four, "and I want to say that I have stood at the foot of this wall at about half past three every afternoon for nine years and I have never once been told I could, and I have never once wanted to stop, and I am not going to pretend to you that I wanted to stop. I am going to stop because it is allowed, and there is a difference between the two and I have thought about it for about two minutes."

---

"What is the difference," said the man of fifty-six.

The man of about sixty-four looked up at the bank and then back down.

"You stop because you want to and you move on when you have had enough of it," he said. "You stop because you are allowed and you move on because somebody might notice. I have done the second one my whole life and there is not one other person in this basin doing it on a road."

---

Then the man of fifty-six asked him something else, and it is the first question anybody in that yard has asked him in this run of mornings.

He asked it in the ordinary voice, standing about four feet off, in about nineteen people's hearing, with about nine people at the top of eleven feet leaning over that bank trying to hear it.

"Do you go out by the gate," he said, "or the long way round."

The man of about sixty-four was quiet for a moment.

"The long way round," he said. "Always the long way round. I go out at about half past four and I come back by the gate because the gate is a gate and not a shortcut, and I have never once been late and I have never once been early."

"Why do you go out at about half past four."

"Nobody in this yard has ever asked me a question," said the man of about sixty-four, "and you are the first one who is not a person with a sheet."

---

"Because it is the hour I go out at."

"No it is not."

---

The man of fifty-six looked at him for a while.

"Then why," he said.

"Because the first morning I ever did this road I got up at about half past four because it was the earliest I could get up without waking anybody in that house, and I have never had a reason since then and I have said so to nobody, and you are the first person in about nine years to ask me a question that I cannot answer with a figure and I would like it noticed that I answered you honestly."

---

About eleven feet of bank and about four hundred yards of air carried all of it and not one word came up.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet had been leaning over that bank for about four minutes and they had heard a voice and the shape of words and nothing else, and about four of them said afterwards that they could tell there had been more than one of them down there and could not have told you one word.

---

Adrian Vale did not go down.

He stood at the top of that bank from about half past three until about four and a half and he did not go down the eleven feet, and about four people at the top of eleven feet noticed him not going down and none of them said one word about it, and later one of them said, privately, to the man who keeps a tally, that she had thought he would.

She was wrong about that and she knew she was wrong about it inside about four seconds of thinking about it, because there was nothing at the bottom of that bank that he had ever wanted.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six came off the wall and said the four, and he got all four of them.

About nine people were standing on the ground at the foot of that bank at that moment and every one of them heard it, and not one of them had ever stood on that ground before this morning, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it at the same moment and said *as sent* out of step with each other as they have said it every morning since the wall changed.

---

And then he said, in the ordinary voice, that he does not know what any of them is for, and about nine people heard it for the ninth time running, and about nine of them were on the same ground with him, and none of the nine said one word about it afterwards and none of the nine wrote it down, and the woman who keeps a scale was still on her step about forty feet off and did not come down, and she said one thing to nobody at about half past four.

---

"I have been on that step nine years," she said, "and that ground has been under me the whole of it and I have never once stood on the middle of it with anybody else, and on the one hundred and seventeenth morning a man of sixty-one gave it away and this morning about nine people stood on it and read the four off that wall with him, and I am going to be on that step at the same hour tomorrow and I am not going to say one word about any part of it, and I would like it noticed that I said nothing."

---

At about half past four the man of about sixty-four went out on nine miles of road, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the sixty-fifth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of sixty-one did not go up the eleven feet at about four this afternoon because he had nothing left to give away and he had said so himself in that yard on the one hundred and seventeenth morning.
