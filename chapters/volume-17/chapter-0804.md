# Chapter 804: The Eighty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man At The End Of The Second Table Was Asked In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People To Say What His Fifth Line Was For And He Said He Was About Thirty-Two When He Wrote It And That He Had Not Told Anybody In Fifteen Years And That He Was Not Going To Start And A Boy Of Seventeen Was Asked A Second Question And Said No

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and forty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and eighteen days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and thirty-two days. Six hundred and ninety-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and twelve days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and eighty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and eighty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and twenty-one days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and sixty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and forty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man at the end of a table was asked what a line of his was for this morning and answered in about nine words and the count did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and eighteen mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and seventeen. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for sixty-eight mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is eighty-eight, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-four and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the fifth of the five lines was described out loud in that yard for the second time in fifteen years this morning and is not on any page anybody can walk up to and read.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and twenty-ninth of those mornings, and about nine people in that yard had heard one set of four figures said out loud inside four days and about nine had heard the other, and one clerk had written the second set down and one boy of seventeen had deliberately not written either.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and forty-third night of that run, having slept on four hundred and forty-two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the thirty-third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a road keeper came up that lane at about half past four with a book in his coat and did not stop and went back down it.

The record has stood three days, and a page came down that lane on the eighty-fifth morning and one has come down it on no morning since, and the lane count is twenty-four and does not move for anybody going up that hill.

---

Sabra Holt asked it, standing at the near end of the boards at about ten, and she said the question out loud before she asked it, which is a thing she has done in that yard about four times and which about four people in it have learned to wait for.

"Ask him what the fifth line is for."

---

That is all she said and about nineteen people in that yard had heard the question and nobody answered it for about the length of time it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

---

"I am not going to do that the way a thing is done," said the man at the end of the second table. "I have been asked things in this yard on the first morning I came up it and I answered them and I have been careful in this yard for nine of the fifteen years I have been at this table, and I have not once been asked what the fifth line is for, and I am not going to find out what I say now that I have been asked.

"I will tell you the reason, because the reason is the whole of what I have got and it is short, and then I am going to stop and everybody can go back to what they were doing.

"I was about thirty-two. I have been at this table for fifteen years and I was about twenty-two when I wrote it, and the fifth line is four lines long and it is in a clerk's hand and it is in that book, and I have not said out loud in this yard what it is for since the day I wrote it, and I did say it out loud once, on the seventy-first morning, in front of about nineteen people, and about four of you heard me describe it and not one of you has asked me since, and I have been waiting seventeen mornings to see whether any of you was going to, and this is the morning, and it is not the question I would have chosen."

---

"Then let me put the other one," said Mavis Dorr, from about four feet off the boards, and she had not moved and her voice had not gone up. "What I want to ask him is what he thought it was for when he wrote it, and that is a different question, and I have been carrying it since the seventy-first morning and I am putting it down this morning because if I do not put it down this morning I am going to be the woman who asked it once."

---

The man at the end of the second table looked at her for a while and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched him do it and nobody said anything.

"I thought it was for stopping a person," he said. "That is the whole of it and it is eight words and I have got about four years of things I have been meaning to say and that is the one that matters.

"I was about twenty-two. I had watched a thing go wrong at the end of a table for about two years and I wrote a line into a book that said a person could stand up and say no and that the book could not carry it, and I built it on purpose to be impossible for a book to do, and it has been impossible for a book to do for fifteen years, and it has cost me the ability to want anything at all since about the winter after I wrote it, and I would rather that line were the end of this book than have anybody put a name in a column for me.

"That is the fifth line. It is a thing I made when I was about twenty-two and it has been the reason this district is the way it is for fifteen years and the four of you at the near end of these boards have been standing under it every morning since, and one of you has been a clerk for all fifteen of them, and I have never once said any of that out loud in this yard, and I have said it this morning because a woman with a key asked me a different question than the one I was ready for."

---

The clerk had her hand on the cover of the lot book and she did not lift it.

"I am not going to enter that," she said. "I am going to tell you exactly why and then I am going to stop, and then somebody is going to hand me a pencil in about four minutes and I am going to have to do something with it.

"If I enter that, then the lot book has a line in it about the man who wrote the fifth line, and the line will be about a person, and in about four years a stranger is going to read a page of this district that says a man made a rule because he watched a thing go wrong, and the stranger is going to know more about the rule than the man who wrote it, and the man who wrote it is on no page in this district and I am not going to be the one who puts him there. That is the whole of my trade and I have known it since the second morning of this run of mornings and I have not had to be told it once."

---

"You have had to be told it about nine times," said Sabra Holt.

"Yes," said the clerk, "and I have said no nine times, and I would like the ninth of them entered, because the first eight were mine."

---

She entered it, and it is the eleventh line on the page at the back with nothing ruled on it, and she read it out before she wrote it, and about four people in that yard heard her say the words *a man who wrote a rule*.

---

Orren Pike had been at the near end of the boards with his sheet against his chest for about half an hour and had not written anything, and about nine people in that yard had noticed that, and about four of them had decided that it was none of their business, and about four of them had not.

"Orren," said Adrian.

---

"Do not," said the boy. "I know what you are about to do and I have had three mornings of practice at stopping it and I am going to do it in a different way this morning, so listen to it.

"You want me to write down that a man at the end of a table said eight words out loud in this yard and that about nineteen people heard him say them, and you want it in the body of my sheet, in the columns, where a person could be looked up by anybody who ever holds this.

"And I am not going to, and the reason is not that I am protecting him. It is that you and I have been arguing about this for three mornings and the argument is not about him.

"A man says a thing out loud in a yard and about nineteen people heard it and about nine of them can be asked about it separately and would give the same answer, because they were all standing there and they all heard the same words, and that is a fact about this morning and about those nineteen people and not a sentence about that man, and it goes in the box, and the box is the only place in this district where a fact like that can live without becoming a thing with a name at the bottom of it."

---

He wrote the morning in the box on the back of his own sheet and under it he wrote the fact, and under the fact he wrote the number of people who had heard it, which was nineteen, and he did not write the man's age and he did not write the fifteen years and he did not write the winter.

"I am going to be honest about what I have just done," he said, "because four people in this yard have taught me to be honest about it and I would like to keep it up. I have left out the two things that man said which are the two best things he has ever said in this yard, and I have left them out on purpose, and if anybody in this yard thinks I did that to protect him they can say so this morning and I will take it."

---

Nobody said anything, and the man at the end of the second table said one thing.

"You have left them out because they were about him," he said, "and I would have left them out too, and I have been leaving things out of that book for about fifteen years, and the first thing I ever left out of it was the reason I wrote the fifth line, and I have just said the reason out loud in a yard, and you have written down that I said it, and between the two of those there is now a gap in this district about the size of a winter, and I am not going to close it, and I am not going to leave it open in a speech either."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and the clerk's pencil went into a book, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, as they have about four times in nine years, and the boy of seventeen wrote the morning in the box on the back of his own sheet and wrote one more line under it and did not read it out, and the boards of the second table were bare where the stone had been, and the boards at the near end of the first table had a stone on them this morning that is nine years old and has a thumb worn in the middle of it that nobody made, and Adrian Vale has been in that yard one hundred and nine mornings and has not been given a bed, a wage or an office and has not asked for any of the three.
