# Chapter 834: The One Hundred And Eighteenth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of About Forty-Eight Who Keeps A Tally Said His Number Out Loud At About One And Then Said Out Loud That He Would Not Put A Toll Of Ninety-Eight On It And A Woman Who Keeps A Scale Said Nineteen Fives Is Ninety-Five Out Loud At Those Boards And Then Said That The Toll Is Not A Round Number And Three Of The Nineteen Give Six

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and seventy-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries seven hundred and thirty-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and forty-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and sixty-two days. Seven hundred and twenty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Four hundred and thirty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and forty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Five hundred and twelve days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is six hundred and fifteen days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and fifty-one days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and ninety-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and seventy-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a woman who keeps a scale divided a toll of ninety-eight by nineteen out loud at those boards and said that three of the nineteen give six, and nothing was entered on the back of that book this morning, and the count of things this district has made did not move and the five things are still not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and forty-eight mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and forty-seven. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for ninety-eight mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is one hundred and eighteen, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-seven and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever, and the page at the back has thirty-one lines on it and none of the thirty-one is in the lot book.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fifty-ninth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and a woman who keeps a scale said ninety-five out loud at those boards and then said a figure that is not a round number.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and seventy-third night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seventy-two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the sixty-third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a woman who keeps a scale did a division out loud at those boards and came at it the wrong way round and came at it anyway, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood thirty-three days, and the toll is ninety-eight and it is paid out of the number the man of about forty-eight keeps, and sixteen of about nineteen people give five and three give six, and nobody has entered any of it anywhere.

"One hundred and thirty-nine," said Orren Pike.

He said it at the near end of the first table and then he said, without being asked and about as loudly, that a figure is a thing two people can have a different opinion about, and that he had got up at about half past five on purpose and had nothing to do with himself until ten to eight.

---

At about one the man who keeps a tally said the number out loud.

He said it into the middle of that yard in the ordinary voice and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down, and he stayed where he was when he had finished, which he has never done in this run of mornings.

---

"Ninety-eight," he said again.

About four people at the top of eleven feet turned round.

"I said it yesterday afternoon in about nine people's hearing and about nine people heard it and nobody wrote it down and I want it said twice in two mornings in the same yard before a figure of mine goes anywhere, and that is the whole of why I have said it again and it is not a second opinion."

---

The woman who keeps a scale put the beam down across her knees and came up about four feet of the bank and stood at the boards.

"Nineteen," she said. "There are about nineteen of you and the toll is ninety-eight and I am going to divide it out loud because nobody in this yard is going to be told a share by a man who has just given away a piece of ground."

"Nineteen fives is ninety-five," she said. "That is what it comes to if you divide it as round as you can and there is no more than that and I have said it and I would like it written down that I said ninety-five first."

Mavis Dorr made a small noise.

"Ninety-five is not the toll," said the woman who keeps a scale. "The toll is ninety-eight. The remainder is three and the remainder has to go somewhere and it goes on three of you, and those three of you give six."

---

"Sixteen fives and three sixes," said Adrian Vale.

"That is sixteen fives," said the woman who keeps a scale, "and three sixes, and that is eighty and eighteen, and that is ninety-eight, and I would like about nine people to have heard it add up."

Nobody said one word.

"Now the part that is not arithmetic," she said. "I did not choose those three. I want that said before anybody in this yard thinks I stood on that step for nine years and picked three people out of nineteen. Nobody in this yard picks anybody this afternoon. The three are the three of you who can each lose a thing this afternoon that is not a figure."

---

"Say them," said Mavis Dorr.

"The boy of seventeen," said the woman who keeps a scale. "He has kept a sheet for four years so that it could not be got hold of, and he is giving six out of a number and putting a line on a page he has kept out of about four people's reach. That is a thing he has had and not a figure."

"The man of fifty-six," she said. "Nobody in this basin can prove the four figures on that wall changed except him, and every morning he is right is one of them, and there is no page and there is no second copy and if six comes out of that number then six mornings of it stop being anybody's proof."

"And the clerk," said the woman who keeps a scale, "who is the only person in this district who enters things, and who entered a question on the one hundred and twelfth morning because a man asked her to, and who is going to go on being the only one who can, and I do not know what six is going to take off that and neither does she."

---

"Nineteen people," said the man of sixty-one, from about four feet off, "and three of them picked by what they are holding."

"I did not say you picked them," said the woman who keeps a scale. "I said they are the three who can lose something that is not a figure, and that is not a picking and it is not a merit, and the sixteen of the rest of them lose five, and five is nothing anybody has ever heard of, and I am going to say the next part out loud because nobody else in this yard is going to say it."

---

"Sixteen of us give five," she said. "None of the sixteen can be asked about six."

---

That was the whole of what she said and she went back down about four feet of bank and sat down on her step and put the beam across her knees again.

---

"I am not saying one word about that," said Mavis Dorr.

"You are not obliged to," said the woman who keeps a scale, from about forty feet off. "I have said it and about nine people heard it and that a figure has now gone out of that yard in the ordinary voice on three mornings running and that I did not choose one of the three, and that the one before mine was the twenty-six that the boy said on the one hundred and sixteenth morning, and the one before that was the ninety-eight that the man who keeps a tally said on the one hundred and fifteenth, and the one before that was mine."

---

Orren Pike stood at the near end of the second table with his hand flat on the sheet that was lying on it and did not say anything for about two minutes.

Then he said: "I have been keeping that sheet for four years so that it could not be got hold of, and I handed it over on the one hundred and thirteenth morning to a man who has not walked up that hill, and I gave away the only copy of anything in this basin when I did it. I would like it noticed that I am now paying six out of a number as well, and that this is the first time in my life that a person I have never met has asked me for something and I cannot check whether the asking is correct, and I am going to pay it, and I am saying it out loud so that about nine people in this yard can be asked about it separately tomorrow."

---

The man of fifty-six was on the flat stone at the foot of the low wall and he had come up about four feet along it, which he does not do, so that he could be heard from the top of the bank.

"Do I pay the six," he said.

It came up eleven feet and it came up clearly and about nine people at the top of that bank heard it and nobody had asked him.

---

"Yes," said the woman who keeps a scale.

---

"Nobody in this basin has asked me anything since before the middle of the winter," said the man of fifty-six, "and a woman who keeps a scale has just come up four feet of that bank and told me to pay six, and I am going to do it, and I am going to say why and then I am not going to say anything else about it."

He set his palm down on the stone.

"I have been charged for the ground I stand on," he said. "That is the first time anything in this basin has put a figure on the place where I read four figures off a wall twice a morning, and I am not going to say it was worth it and I am not going to say it was not worth it, because I do not know and because about nine people can be asked about it separately tomorrow and I would rather be asked than told."

---

About nine people at the top of eleven feet were extremely still.

The clerk had her hand on the page at the back of the lot book and she did not open it.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and seventy-third night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seventy-two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the sixty-third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and about nineteen people said five and three said six out loud in that yard two hours before it and none of the three had ever said a figure out loud in that yard in their lives.

---

At four o'clock the man of fifty-six read the four off that wall, got all four of them, and then said, in the ordinary voice and in the same breath, that he does not know what any of them is for.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet heard it for the seventh time running and said *as sent* at the same moment and out of step with each other, and about four of them had heard him agree to a figure eleven feet above them not two hours before, and not one of the four put the two together out loud, because he had asked them not to, and because he had been right about what a figure costs since before the middle of the winter.
