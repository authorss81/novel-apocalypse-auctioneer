# Chapter 635: A Person Came Down That Lane With A Page In Their Hand At About Ten On The Eleventh Of The Sixth Month And A Man Of About Thirty-Four Who Mends Fencing Got Down On One Knee At That Table And Put Two Staples In A Leg Of It And Nobody Asked Him Why

The boards of that second table were dry by about seven on the eleventh of the sixth month and about four people in that yard had come up that bank before eight and had gone to the top of that lane, and by ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen. Two pages were lying along the end of that second table and the copy at the near end of them has now been on that wood for a hundred and thirty-three mornings.

A clerk of nineteen years entered at the near end of that first table at about a quarter to eight that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered three hundred and seventy-four days as the age of that figure, and entered that the number of pages on the end of that second table is two and did not move, and entered that the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move. Entered that the number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand since the sixth of the fifth month is five at about a quarter to eight on its thirty-fifth morning, and entered that she has not said out loud what she will enter if that figure moves, and entered that the figure is six and that it moved later in the day with a person in the room and the reason in the room before the hand, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them without looking at the wall, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that this is the three hundred and sixtieth of those mornings. About four of them have said that a man got down on one knee at that first table this morning with a hammer and two staples in his own mouth and that about four of them have said that nobody in that yard asked him to and that about four of them have said that he said the reason out loud first, which nobody had noticed happening until he had already said it.

"**The board carries five hundred and thirty-three, the train on that siding has stood eight hundred and forty-nine days, nobody has entered anything for five hundred and sixty-three, and five hundred and twenty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing had his own hammer and his own tin of staples on the boards at the near end of that first table at about half past nine, and he said one thing out loud, in about 39 seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what he said and got one hundred and three and read the number back to himself in a low voice, and then he got down on one knee in the grit and put the two staples in the wood with the flat of his own hand on the back of the hammer and did it in about a minute.

"**That leg has been moving under about four people's heels since before the frost and a man put a piece of slate under it three days ago and marked the slate in chalk, and the slate is a stone, and about four of you have worked out that I am going to tell you why before I do this and not after, and the reason is that a leg is a leg and that it is the only thing in this yard that nobody is being paid for and that is going to come loose, and I am not doing it for anybody.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he put two staples in a leg of that first table at about half past nine with his own hands and a hammer, and entered that she did not ask him why, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up came and looked at the two staples for about ten seconds and then went and got his own piece of slate out from under that leg and carried it down to the far end of that yard and put it on the low wall there, and said nothing at all to anybody, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a man took a piece of slate out from under a leg of that first table and carried it to that wall and entered that nobody asked him what for, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

And then a man of about thirty-eight came down that lane at about ten with a page in their hand and about nine people at the top of that lane turned round, and he came up that bank and stopped at the far end of that second table and said one thing out loud, in about 45 seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what he said and got one hundred and twenty and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have had this for twelve days and I have been down that lane twice and I came back up it both times, and about four of you have worked out that I came up a bank I had been able to see from about a mile off since yesterday afternoon, and about four of you have worked out that I could see this yard from the top of that lane and that I did not know it was a yard until I was in it, and that I have been thinking about that for about a day and a half and I have not worked out whether that is a thing about this place or a thing about me.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a person came down that lane with a page in their hand at about ten on the eleventh of this month and entered that they came down that lane and that they had a page in their hand and the hour, and entered that she is not entering his name, and entered that the number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand is six and moved this morning with a person in the room and the reason in the room before the hand, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up that bank at about half past eleven with mud to the thigh of his own right leg and stood at the far end of that second table and said one thing out loud, in about 48 seconds, to about nineteen people, and the boy counted what he said and got one hundred and twenty-four and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**A man in this yard said the reason out loud before he put a hammer to that leg this morning and about four of you have worked out that nobody in this yard has given a reason out loud before a hand went in five weeks, and about four of you have worked out that I have watched that man say a great deal this month and have not heard him give one reason for any of it, and about four of you have worked out that I noticed, and I am not going to make anything of it, and about four of you have worked out that I am saying that on purpose because there is about four of you who would have.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he is not going to make anything of it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

About nine people at that low wall have said since that a person came down that lane with a page in their hand at about ten this morning and that it is the sixth of them, and that about four of them have said that the six of them have all said something out loud in that yard on the morning they arrived except the one on the fifth of this month who said something about where she chose to stand, and that about four of them have said that a man put two staples in a leg of that table at about half past nine and that the man who put tables up took his own slate away afterwards and carried it to that wall and did not say one word about it.

The road keeper came up that lane at about half past one, read the four figures off that wall as he went past, and was not asked about the eleven miles, and about four people at that wall have said since that a man got down on one knee at that table this morning and put two staples in a leg of it, and that the road keeper came up that lane afterwards and read the four figures off that wall and was not asked about the staples or the leg.

The cart came up at about three with the near wheel still dragging and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and went on up that bank and went on up that lane past the wall without stopping. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given, and about four people near that wall have said since that a man was in that yard this morning doing a job nobody asked him to do, and that the man on those stones has been at that wall through every morning of it, and that about four of them have said that nobody has ever put two staples into anything on his account. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his two hundred and seventy-fourth night of that run, that he has slept on two hundred and seventy-three of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

Two hundred and eighty-three days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Two hundred and thirty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man, and the fourth line says that he is not named here. Two hundred and forty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Three hundred and thirteen days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is four hundred and sixteen days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is two hundred and fifty-two days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched it.

A hundred and ninety-two marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are a hundred and seventy-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither moved this morning. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move, and two staples in a leg is not a fourteenth thing, and a clerk entered the reason out loud with the count, which is that a leg was loose before this morning and is not loose now, and that a repair is not a making. Two of the four figures on that wall are out and neither of them was altered this morning, and the figure on the wall stands at five hundred and twenty-four.

The number of pages on the end of that second table is two and did not move, and the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and the number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move, and a copy has now been on the end of that second table for a hundred and thirty-three mornings while three pages lie on a shop counter up that bank with a door propped open, and a page brought down that lane this morning is not a fifth of them.

The fifth of those five lines is one condition with no end on it and the count of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and a man got down on one knee at that table this morning and that is not a condition with no end on it either. The reading of that lot is the fourth of the five, the fourth of them is a person, the fifth of them is a remedy, and it is unpaid, and six people down a lane did not advance it, and a clerk entered the reason, which is that a remedy is what happens when that person says no and that the man at the end of the fifth line has been answered six times and has said no to nobody. About three things were said out loud in that yard today and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and it has been named on every one of these mornings and it is empty on every one of them, and no person has been appointed to it, and a person who came down that lane this morning is not in it.

The fifth of the five things this district does not have is not paid, the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and a man who has been answered six times and has said no to nobody is not a payment and did not pay it, and the count of the five is still five.

Nobody in that yard went up that bank after the buckets. The buckets went down that bank empty at about half past five and were carried back up it full, and the woman of about fifty-eight read three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and about four people at the foot of that bank have said since that a person came down that lane this morning with a page in their hand and got as far as that second table, and that the woman who reads three lines in that book every evening came up past the same tables at about ten to six and did not stop and did not look, and that about four of them have said that a stranger got to those tables in a morning and a woman who has been reading the same three lines for four hundred mornings did not.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall for the last time that day, got all four of them, and about nine people near that wall have said since that this is the three hundred and sixtieth morning, and that a man was on one knee at that table with a hammer in his own hand at about half past nine and that the man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and was not asked about either of the two.

A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four in the afternoon in the yard of Lot Seventeen on the eleventh of the sixth month that a man put two staples in a leg of that first table with his own hands and that she did not ask him why, and the number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand is six and moved this morning, and a man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was at the near end of that first table at about half past nine on one knee in the grit with his own hammer in his own hand.
