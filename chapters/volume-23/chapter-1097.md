# Chapter 1097: The Three Hundred And Eighty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Six Feet Along That Wall

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and no one in this district has ever done anything with it. It is eight hundred and thirty-six days old.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety-five marks. One thousand three hundred and eleven days is how long that train has stood on that siding without a wagon off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and twenty-five days. Nine hundred and eighty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was not run and it is not a lot and not open this morning. That book is seven hundred days ahead of this yard on its second line and nobody in this district has ever read the rest of it. Seven hundred and five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and seventy-five days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and fourteen days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-eight days back. Six hundred and fifty-four marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and forty marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing here that holds it up. Those four documents have never once been got into this district. Those three lines are readable in four places here and in no fifth place. Four conditions stand in this district with no end upon any of them. This basin has made fourteen things and no others. Not one of the five of those fourteen ever arrived here and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book was not able to take a line for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning came, so that nothing written on it before that morning was ever a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that page and that page stands at sixty-five lines and it took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Four hundred and eleventh mornings is how long that second table has stood at the foot of that bank, and three hundred and sixty-first is how long the near rail has stood on the near side of it, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and eighty-first mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and eightieth morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and thirty-sixth night of his run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and thirty-five of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twenty-second of those mornings and had all four of them. That is the two hundred and thirty-seventh morning running with nobody having asked him anything. What he said to that stone after the four of them was the two hundred and seventieth time running.

That lot book has nothing written at the back of it, nothing ever having been put there, and it lies open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after that carries one name under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form and nobody in this district has ever been asked to put anything in it. Behind that lane are thirty pages in the hands of those who brought them and it brought nothing down it in the evening. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-six days. About nine people were stood on that ground at ten to eight and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-sixth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. No one in this district has ever had either of these two given to them, and nobody has ever gone up to look at either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll came to ninety-eight and it is paid and spent and none of it is left. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and nobody in this district has ever read one of them out.

---

He came through the gate at about ten to nine with the day still on him and stood a minute at the foot of that low wall deciding which part of it to put his back against.

What he wanted off that evening was the hour.

---

That wall holds the day the way a floor holds a fire, for about an hour, and then it has nothing left in it.

He has worked this out over a long run of nights without ever once putting it into words, because he has never needed words for it and because the people who come to that wall in the evening are not people who would understand what he is doing there with his back.

The part of that wall he likes is a stretch of about four feet near the gate end. The sun comes over that bank and down the face of that wall at an angle in the late afternoon and it lies on those four feet until the light has gone about a quarter past seven.

Six feet further along, the wall is a different wall as far as the evening is concerned.

---

He was not asked anything about any of it by anybody, and he did not ask anybody anything, and that has been the arrangement between him and that yard for as long as he has been coming to it.

At about six the man of about thirty-two came and stood at the other end of that wall with his hands behind him and waited for the cart, and he talked without waiting to be answered.

"That stone will give you an hour after the sun has left it. I have had my back on it in the afternoon and it is like leaning on a door that has just been stood in the sun. Everybody in this yard thinks that wall is cold and it is the warmest thing on this ground from about six until it is not."

He had his back on four feet of it for the best part of an hour and he told nobody what he had found out about the other six.

---

About half past seven the shadow of that bank came over the top of it and started down the face.

He felt it come. There is no other way to know that a shadow has got onto a wall you have your back against, and he has never once been wrong about it.

He stood at the foot of that wall for about another ten minutes out of pure habit, and then he went along the ground to the place about six feet further on and put his back against that instead.

---

He had it at about twenty past to eight and he could feel straight away that it was a different piece of wall.

That six feet of stone had the sun on it until about half past seven and it gave that back until nearly half past eight, and he stood against it for about three quarters of an hour and it was worth the walk.

That is the whole of what he got out of that evening and it took him eleven years to find out and about two minutes to use.

---

Nobody in that yard put a question to him about it at any point of it, then or at any other time.

About half past eight the man of about thirty-four came along that wall on his way to the boards at the top of that bank and stopped a little way off and stood with his cloth over his arm.

"You are further along than you were last week."

"I am."

"The heat is better there in the evening and it is not worth the walk back."

"You have noticed it."

"I have walked past that wall every morning and evening for nine years," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have never once thought about where the sun lies on it, and I am not going to start this evening."

Then he went on down his own boards and did not say anything else about it, and about four people at the top of those eleven feet were standing about with a foot on a board and none of them said one word to any other.

The boards come down off that cart face down at about half past four and go up that bank on the shoulders, and about nine people go up with them, and about ten come back down for the second load, and that is the whole of what that eleven feet of ground is for on an evening.

---

About ten past eight the turner came out of that wheelhouse and stood in the gap at the top of that bank and said the thing he says on most evenings, which is where the boards are going and what time they will be off that ground, and nobody asked him a question about any of it and he did not wait to be asked.

"I want that load off that cart and up before the wind gets into it," he said out loud, "and I want the near wheel kept off the place where those boards were lying yesterday, and that is all I have said and it is all I am going to say."

Then he went back in.

The wind came up out of the north-east about nine and found that wall the way it finds everything at that hour, and about four people at the top of those eleven feet put their feet on the boards and stayed there for about an hour with nobody arranging any of it.

Seven hundred days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands.

Nobody in that yard looked up at it, and that wheel is not going up.

---

At about ten past ten he took his back off that wall, which had nothing in it left by then, and stood a moment in the dark with his hands in his pockets.

He did not go up that lane and nothing came down it.

He had come out of that yard with nothing in his hand and he was going back into it with nothing in his hand, and that was the whole of that evening and he had got the three quarters of an hour he came for.

---

That page is at sixty-five lines and it is at sixty-five lines now. There are thirty pages behind that lane and none of them came down it in the evening either. There is no voice in this district that has ever read one of those three. That fifth of the five is five and it is still unpaid. Seven months has been counted here and nothing in this district carries it. That gatepost sheet is carrying four hundred and eleven and it will be carrying it in the morning.

Six feet further along that wall is where the evening is, and it is worth the walk, and nobody asked him a single thing about any part of it.
