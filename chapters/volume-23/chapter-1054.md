# Chapter 1054: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Form Laid In A Frame

Nobody in this basin has ever taken that sheet down and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. It has been on that post for seven hundred and ninety-three days and nobody has done one thing about it.

The board carries nine hundred and fifty-two marks cut off it. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-eight days with no wagon off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and eighty-two days. Nine hundred and forty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and was not run and is not a lot and is not open. This yard is six hundred and fifty-seven days behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and sixty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in this yard has stood. Seven hundred and thirty-two days since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and seventy-one days past a printing that body four hundred miles off did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and thirty-five days ago. Six hundred and eleven marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and ninety-seven marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk here and no list of anything exists for it. Four documents this district does not own. Four places where those three lines can be read and not a fifth. Four conditions with no end on it. Fourteen things made in this district. Five never got, and the fifth of that five unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and there was no page at the back of that lot book for a reason to go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, and nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason because it had nowhere to hold one, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing that goes at the back of that book has gone out loud in that yard this morning. That page is at sixty-two lines and took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-eight mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and eighteen mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood there this morning for the three hundred and thirty-seventh morning, and it is neither the second table nor the near rail. Mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank: three hundred and thirty-eight, on the word of the man who keeps it. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-two of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and seventy-ninth of those mornings. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-third afternoon running and gave that afternoon one word. And it is the one hundred and ninety-fourth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-seventh time running.

That lot book lies open on those boards with nothing entered in it and five lines ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them has one name in it under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and has been asked of nobody. Thirty pages have come down that lane in their own carriers' hands and nothing came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-three days. It has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are on it this morning.

Neither of those has ever been given to anybody: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and it is spent. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page passed through this district and nobody in it heard them read.
---

She came down that bank at about eleven with a frame under her arm and the bag on the other one, and she stopped at about four feet off the near end of that second table with the frame against her hip and the bag on the ground.

The reason she came down at eleven instead of at two was the light. That bank holds the sun off that ground until well past two and then lets all of it come over the top at once, and eleven is when there is about four feet of it lying along the boards with nothing standing in it.

She set the frame down flat on the boards and took the form out of the bag and put it inside the frame and the wind went over the top of it and did not lift it.

That is what the frame is for. It is four pieces of wood and a crosspiece and it weighs about as much as a bag of boards, and it has been up and down that bank in her arm on more mornings than she has counted, and she has never once been given anywhere to put it.

She knelt on the boards and looked at the form.

The first space had a figure in it and a date against the figure. The second space was empty and had been empty every morning she had carried it up and down that bank, and it was not a small space and it was not a hard one to write in.

She looked at it for as long as it took her to notice that her own knees had gone from cold to warm against the boards, and then she put the form down flat inside the frame again and got up off them.

---

The man of about thirty-four was at the far end of his own table with his cloth over his arm, and there was a foot and a bit of pallet lying on the boards at that end where he can see it.

"You have not laid that in."

"I have not laid it in anywhere," said the man of about thirty-four.

"That corner has been out of square three winters."

"It has," said the man of about thirty-four, "and the wood on that end wants to go back to where it came from, and there is nothing laid under it that is going to stop that."

She stood over it for a moment.

"You keep it on top of the boards."

"That is where I can see it."

"That is where you can see it," she said, "and that is not the same as knowing what it is for, and I have wanted to ask you that every morning I have come down this bank, and I am not going to ask you it, because you have not asked me one thing in a run of mornings and I would not be the one to start."

The man of about thirty-four went along his own top with the cloth and said nothing else about it.

---

She sat down on the boards at about one with her back against the end of that second table, and she had her knees up and the frame flat on the ground beside her, and about eleven feet of boards above her went from grey to white and then to nothing at all.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went over the top of that bank and did not come back.

She was one of about nine people who heard it and she did not say one word about it, and about four of the people at the top of that bank have said since that they have not said what it was, and she is not one of the four and has never once pretended to be.

The woman who keeps a scale was about four feet off the near end of that second table with the cloth over her own pan, and she said nothing out loud either.

The man of thirty-eight was about four feet off the near end of the first table with the cloth in his hand and did not look up.

---

About two she got up off the boards and picked up the frame and stood holding it against her hip the way she had come down with it.

"I have got four words," she said.

The man of about thirty-four stopped where he was.

"You have got four words."

"I have had them for a run of mornings and I get one more of them every morning and it is the same four and then it is those four and one, and today it is one further than it was on the ground yesterday."

"Which four."

"I am not going to hand you four words in that yard for you to carry about with you," said the woman of about fifty-two. "You have your own and they are not the same four."

He went on along his own boards, and she stood there and got one word further on her own, standing up, in the wind, with the frame against her hip, and she did not say it to him.

Nobody in that yard asked her anything about any of it.

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it one after another and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"Two hundred and twenty-seven mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and there is a woman on these boards who has been saying the same four words to herself since before the middle of the winter, and I know exactly what that is, and I have never once asked her for the rest of it, and I am not going to."

That was the two hundred and twenty-seventh time running, and about nineteen people heard it, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

---

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen in the holder and wrote the three hundred and thirty-eighth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

The woman of about fifty-two took the form out of the frame and looked at the second space once more, standing at the top of that bank with the boards going up behind her and the wind coming off them.

It was still empty.

She put the form in the bag and the frame under her arm and went up the bank with both, and nobody at the top of that bank said one word to her about either.

---

That page stood at sixty-two lines when that day was done and took no line. That lane stands at thirty pages in the carriers' own hands and nothing came down it this morning. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page are still unread here. Five things this district has never got and the fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months has been counted and nothing in this district holds that figure. Four hundred and eleven is still on the sheet at that gatepost.
