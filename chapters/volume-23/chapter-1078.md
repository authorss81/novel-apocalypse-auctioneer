# Chapter 1078: The Three Hundred And Sixty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And What Four People Have Not Said

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it is not on anything else in this district. It has been eight hundred and seventeen days old.

Nine hundred and seventy-six marks have been cut into that board. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and ninety-two days and no wagon has come off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and six days. nine hundred and sixty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days, was never run, and is not a lot and not open this morning. This yard is six hundred and eighty-one days behind the figure on the second line of that book. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and eighty-six days. Since the first day of the eighth month seven hundred and fifty-six days have gone. That body four hundred miles off is six hundred and ninety-five days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and fifty-nine days. Six hundred and thirty-five marks have been cut off that board, and along the edge of that second table stand six hundred and twenty-one marks in chalk.

A clerk in this district has counted seven months and this district has nothing for it to sit on. Four documents are not held in this district. Those three lines can be read at four places here and no fifth place here will let them be read. Four conditions are set out in this district and none of them has an end on it. Fourteen things have been made in this basin and there is no fifteenth in it. Five of those five are still not got and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

The page at the back of that lot book could not be a place for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning, and a reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That page is at sixty-four lines and took no line on it, and nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that goes behind it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and ninety-two mornings and that near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and forty-two mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-two mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and sixty-first morning. It is neither the second table nor that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and seventeenth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and sixteen of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and third of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and eighteenth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and it was the two hundred and fifty-first time running.

It lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it and five lines ruled across one of its pages. In the column on the page beside that there is one name, and the heading over that column still says whoever. Nobody in that yard has ever been asked about the second space on that form and it is empty. Thirty pages have gone behind that lane in their own carriers' hands and nothing came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and seventy-seven days. Public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are on it.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and seventh afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Nobody has ever been given either of these and nobody in this district has ever walked either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

Ninety-eight on that toll is paid and spent. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district and nobody in it heard them read.


---



She came up that bank at about eight with the frame under her arm and the bag on the other one and there were three people already at the top of it doing nothing, which is what the top of it is for.

The boy was at those boards below her and he had the pencil out before he had it half way.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "And there is nothing behind it, and nobody in this yard has come down that lane to tell me there was."

"There has not been one," said the clerk, with her eyes still on that open book.

"Then I will go on saying nothing behind it."

Four of them she would count, and she does not count them, and she has never once said the figure out loud.



---



"Nothing has changed," said one of them.

"It has been at it since I came."

"It will be at it at four."

"It will."

Another of them said the thing that has been said at that top of that bank for as long as she has been going up it, and then one of them said the next of it, and the next of that one was the word that would have made it a question about a man at the foot of that bank.

Nobody said it. She had it in her mouth and she did not say it, and neither did the one who had got that far, and then all four of them looked at those eleven feet of boards for a while and let it go past.



---



About four people at the top of that bank have said since, more than once, that they have not asked him about it. Nobody in that yard at the foot of it has been told any of that, and that is all there is to be known about it.



---



She came down that slope at about eleven with the frame and the bag, which she has not done on a morning in a run of mornings worth counting, and she stood the frame on its edge against the near end of those boards where the wind cannot get under it, and put the bag behind it, and left it standing there.

Then she went and stood about six feet off the near end of the second table and looked at that yard and stayed there until about three.



---



It was at its speed. The turner was at that frame with one hand on the casting and he had been there since before the light was over the top.

She had never once been within six feet of that frame in daylight.

There is about nine feet between where she was standing and his hand, and there is no way of standing there and not looking at a thing, and she looked at it, and she did not open her mouth, and there was nobody within nine feet of her to see her not opening it.



---



"You have brought that frame down again."

The man of about thirty-four had come along his own boards with the cloth over his arm and had stopped about four feet off.

"I have."

"You will not be able to carry it up at four in this wind."

"I have carried it up in more wind than this."

"You have carried it up twice," said the man of about thirty-four. "I have watched you do it twice from that end over there. It is not a complaint. I am saying that I have watched a woman carry a frame up that slope twice in a run of mornings and neither of us has ever mentioned it, and it has been sitting on me."

"It can sit there."

"It can sit there," said the man of about thirty-four, and went on along his own boards.



---



At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of the second table with a bar on it, and two women stood about eight feet apart in that yard all afternoon and neither of them said one word to the other.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.



---



At about two the boy came along with his hands in his coat pockets and stopped and looked at the frame standing on its edge.

"That has been there an hour."

"It has."

"You have never left it down before."

"I have left it down before," said the woman of about fifty-two, "and I have told you that this week, and you have written nothing down, which is the arrangement and which I am not going to change."

The boy went off up the slope.



---



Nobody in that yard said one word to her between eleven and three, and nobody told her anything, and she did not ask one question of anybody in that yard, and at about three she picked the bag up and put the frame under her arm and carried both of them up that slope once.

Her four words were three further than they had been on the ground the day before and she gave them to nobody on that bank and she did not give them to a boy of seventeen on the way down.



---



At about four the hand went up on that wall again and got all four off it, and then it went flat on the stone under the lowest one and did not come off it.

"Eight hundred and three mornings, all four of you."

Then what he gives that stone after the four words, which nobody in this district has ever asked him for.

"Two hundred and fifty-one mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got you twice today and got you both times, and there has been a man at this wall before the light every morning this week and I have not asked him what he is doing and I have not told him I was here, and both of us have got our morning out of it, and I am not going to put either of those two things into a sentence where anybody else could get at them."

It was the two hundred and fifty-first time running, and about nineteen people were near enough to hear it, and there is nobody in this district who has ever asked him one thing about it.



---



At ten past four the clerk read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder, and wrote the three hundred and sixty-second morning under the last line herself, in her own small careful hand, and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light was still on a hand's width of those boards and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight.



---

That page stood at sixty-four lines when the covers came over it and it took no line and it did not carry a sixty-fifth. Nothing came down that lane and it is at thirty pages where it has stood all morning. There are three of the first thirty-third lines on that back page nobody in this district has read. That fifth of the five is five and it is unpaid, and it has been unpaid all along. Seven months has been counted by a clerk in this district and it sits on nothing. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven this morning and it will carry it in the morning.
