# Chapter 1086: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Th Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And No Wagon Off That Siding

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it has been left alone in this basin since the day it was put there. Eight hundred and twenty-five days is the age of that figure.

That board carries nine hundred and eighty-four marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred days without a wagon coming off it. At that rail nothing has been entered for one thousand and fourteen days. Nine hundred and seventy-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open and it was not run and it is not a lot and not open this morning. Six hundred and eighty-nine days is how far this yard is behind the figure on the second line of that book. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and ninety-four days. It has been seven hundred and sixty-four days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and three days past a printing it did not make.

That ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and sixty-seven days. Six hundred and forty-three marks have been cut off that board. Along the edge of that second table there are six hundred and twenty-nine marks in chalk.

Seven months has been counted and it is on nothing at all in this district. Four of those documents are missing from this district and not one of the four is in it. Four places here let those three lines be read and nowhere else in here is any of them. Four conditions stand here with no end set on any of them. Fourteen is what has been made in this basin and there is nothing else in it. Five things were never got here, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason went out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book had no room for one before the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it in that yard.

Nothing at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning, and that page stands at sixty-four lines and it took no line on it this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Four hundred mornings is how long that second table has stood at the foot of that bank, and three hundred and fifty mornings is how long the near rail has stood on the near side of it, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and sixty-ninth morning, and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and twenty-fifth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and twenty-four of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and eleventh of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and twenty-sixth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and fifty-ninth time running.

Nothing has ever been entered in that lot book and it lies open along those boards with five lines ruled across one of its pages at all. In the column on the page after that there is its one name, under a heading which still says whoever. A second space on that form stands empty and nobody has ever been asked to put a name in it. That lane is at thirty pages behind it and nothing came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and eighty-five days. About nine people are on that ground and it has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and fifteenth afternoon running and one word was the whole of it. Neither of these has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody in this district has ever been up to look at either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight, paid and spent, and there is nothing left of it. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page went through this district and nobody in it heard one of them read aloud.

---


It was at its speed by six and a half.

The stove had been in since before the light and the turner had banked that frame at six and a half and had put it up himself at six and a half, and he was at the near end of that frame with one hand on the casting and he stayed there.

Nobody in that yard had come near him at that hour and nobody in that yard said one word about anything, and the light came over the top of that bank at about seven and lay along those boards.


---


What he wanted off that morning was to find out how much of the twist in his own top he has to live with.

He had known about that twist for three mornings. The near end of that top goes down about the thickness of a thumb and the far end comes up about the thickness of two fingers and his top is not flat and has not been flat since before the middle of the winter.

He had spent those three mornings trying to get that top flat. He was not going to try any more, because he could not and because it was beginning to take the morning off him.


---


He got to his own table at about half past nine and did not put the pallet down anywhere, and put the cloth on the boards at the near end, and worked from the near edge of that top, which is how he has worked that table for years, and got about six feet of it in a pull and the near two feet would not go.

"That is nearly two feet," said the boy, who had come along and stopped at the end of that table at about ten.

"It is."

"I counted it from here two mornings back and I counted it yesterday and it is the same two feet," said the boy, "and you have not asked me what I am going to do about that and I am not going to tell you."

"Nobody has asked me what I am going to do about it."

"You have not asked me," said the boy, "and I noticed, and I have got nine things I write down every morning and one of them is whether anybody has asked me anything, and that one has been the same every morning and I am not going to say what it is."

He put the pencil behind his ear, went off up the slope, and was gone.


---


At about one the man of about thirty-two came out from among his own boards with a straight edge in his hand and stopped where he stops.

"You have been standing at your own table looking at the middle of it for about an hour."

"I have been standing at my own table working the middle of it for about an hour."

"You have been standing at it twice the time it takes to work it," said the man of about thirty-two. "And I have watched you do it for three mornings now, and two mornings back you had that edge on it the other way up and you got a different answer, and I have not asked you about that and I am not going to, and I am telling you I noticed because a man who has been in this yard as long as I have knows what a man stands and looks at is doing."

"Then you know more than I do about what I am doing."

"I know a man stands and looks at his own top when the top has beaten him," said the man of about thirty-two, "and I have stood and looked at my own corner for a week and a half, and I got a wedge out of it, and you are not going to get a wedge out of a twist. That is all I came down for."

He went back in among his own boards again.


---


"The boards go up while the light is on them," said the turner, from the near end of that frame.

"They do."

"That is the whole of what I have come down this morning to say," said the turner, "and I have said it, and I am going back."

"You have not said a word to me about my own table in three mornings."

"I have not," said the turner, "and I am not going to, and I want to tell you why, and the why is that a man who has been told what is wrong with his own table by three other people in one week will go and do something stupid to it to get his own back, and I have seen that done in this yard and I am not saying it happened here."

He went back along to that frame.

At half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of the second table with a bar on it, and she let it want the wind until it stopped wanting it, and nobody in that yard put a word to her at any point of it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and did not come back.


---


At about four the hand went up on that low wall, took all four of them off it from the top down, and laid itself flat on the stone under the lowest one and lay there.

"Eight hundred and eleven mornings, the four of you."

Then the flat answer.

"Two hundred and fifty-nine mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had you twice today and got you both times, and the light has been on this stone for about as long as a man can stand and say it, and I have got nothing to put in there this afternoon, and I am not going to make something up to fill it."

That was the two hundred and fifty-ninth time running, and nobody in this district has ever asked him a thing about any part of it.


---


At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud, with the pen still in the holder.

"The three hundred and seventieth morning," she said. "One man stood at his own table for about an hour working the middle of it and got six feet of it in a pull, and the near two feet are the near two feet, and nothing goes at the back of this book for it."

Then she wrote the three hundred and seventieth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards, and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came down off that cart face down and went up the bank.


---


He went up that slope behind the last board with the cloth over his arm and the foot and about four inches of sound pallet still in his hand, and he worked that table from the near edge of it all afternoon and got about fourteen feet of it done, and the twist was in it the whole time.


---

Nothing was added to that page on that morning and it stood at sixty-four lines when the light went. That lane stood at thirty pages all morning and nothing whatever came down it. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have never been read out by anybody here at all. The fifth of the five is five and it is unpaid. That count of seven months has been entered and nothing in this district sits on it. That sheet at that gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and it carries it in the morning too.

It was at its speed at six and a half that morning and the turner was at that frame, and nobody asked him anything about any part of it.
