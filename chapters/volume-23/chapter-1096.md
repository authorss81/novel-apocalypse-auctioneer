# Chapter 1096: The Three Hundred And Eightieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Frame And A Bag

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and nobody in this basin has ever put that sheet to a use. It has been there eight hundred and thirty-five days.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety-four marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and ten days and no wagon has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and twenty-four days. Nine hundred and eighty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid was open and it was not run and it is not a lot and it is not open. Six hundred and ninety-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and four days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and seventy-four days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and thirteen days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-seven days back. Six hundred and fifty-three marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and thirty-nine marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing here to hold it. Four of those documents have never come into this district at any time. Those three lines can be read in four places here and nowhere else in it. Four conditions stand in this district and no end has been set on one of them. This basin has made fourteen things and nothing beyond them. Five of those fourteen things never came into this basin and the fifth of them is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and nothing on the page at the back of that lot book before the two hundred and eighth morning could have been a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been said out loud in that yard for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that page and that page is at sixty-five lines and it has taken no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and tenth mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and sixtieth mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and eightieth mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventy-ninth morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and thirty-fifth night of that run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and thirty-four of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twenty-first of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and thirty-sixth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the two hundred and sixty-ninth time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

There is nothing at the back of that lot book and there has never been anything put there, and it lies open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after that has one name in it under a heading which still says whoever. There is a second space on that form, it is empty, and nobody has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in the hands of the people who brought them and nothing came down that lane this morning. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-five days. There were about nine people on that ground at ten to eight and it has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-fifth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of these, and nobody here has ever been up to see one of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight and it has been paid and spent and nothing of it is left. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and not one of them was ever read out in it.

---

She came down that bank at about a quarter to nine with a frame in one hand and a bag over the other arm, the way she has come down that bank before, and the light was still on the top of it when she started.

What she wanted off that morning was that frame square.

---

A frame is four sticks and four corners and it is square if the two diagonals are the same length, and that is the whole of the trade, and the difficulty is that you cannot measure a diagonal with a stick without a fourth hand to hold one end of the stick still.

She has been coming down that bank with a frame and a bag for long enough to know which of the four corners will be wrong before she has the thing open, and today it was going to be the one nearest the wall, because that is where the ground comes up about an inch over two yards.

---

The man of about thirty-two was at his own trestles at the far end of that ground and he saw her come down and he got up off his heels and stood with his cloth in his hand about nine feet off.

"You have got a piece out," he said.

"I have got a piece out before I have it on the ground."

"It is always the one by the wall on this side."

"I know which one it is," she said, "and I know what it is before I have got it open, and it is the same one it was the last four times, and if I put the frame on that ground as it is I will have to shim it, and if I shim it I will have to come back and take the shims out in about a week when that ground has settled."

He put the cloth over his shoulder.

"Then set it out away from the wall by about a foot and a half and take your square with you."

---

She did that.

She set the frame out on ground that was about level and she got both diagonals the same with a length of string and a knot in it, and it took her about half an hour, and then she stood looking at it.

The bag was on the ground at her feet and she had the form in it, and the second space on that form was empty where she could see it from where she was standing, and she did not fill it and nobody asked her to.

---

About four feet of that yard was a different kind of quiet, and about four people at the top of those eleven feet had said since that they had not said what it was, and none of them said it this morning either.

---

Half past twelve and the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table, her basket on the ground at her feet.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four voices in that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another.

The turner stood at the frame at the near end of that first table the whole of that morning and put his hands in his pockets at about two and stayed where he was for a while after that.

---

The man of thirty-four came along about one with the cloth over his arm and a foot and about four inches of sound pallet in his other hand, which he has carried for years and laid in nowhere.

She got her own measure out of the bag and stood the two diagonals again on the ground before she lifted a stick of that frame, and got the same answer twice, and then she sat down on her own heel beside it for a while and looked at the light going along the top of that bank.

"You are set out a foot and a half off the wall," he said.

"He told me to."

"Then he was right and I am saying so where he can hear me," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have never once said that about a thing he said and I am not going to make a habit of it."

She did not answer him and he went on down his own boards.

---

At about four that hand went up on the low wall and brought four figures off it from the top down, and then it lay flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"The four, and the fourth one last again."

Then the other thing he says.

"Two hundred and sixty-nine mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had the two of you twice and got them both times, and there is a woman at the middle of that ground this morning standing four feet and a bit from where she stood the last four times, and I have not asked her one thing about it and I am not going to, and this stone gets the whole of it and not one word else."

That was the two hundred and sixty-ninth time running and there is nobody in this district who has ever asked him a question about any of it.

---

The clerk came along those boards at about four with the pen still in the holder and read that morning out loud in the way she reads it, and then she wrote the three hundred and eightieth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

She has read a morning number out loud four hundred mornings and more and this one is a round one and she read it the same as the rest of them, because there is nothing to do with a morning number except say it.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards, and the boards went up that slope while the light was still on that ground, and about four of that nineteen said the goodnight and the boards came down off that cart face down.

She folded her frame into itself and put it under her arm and got the bag up off the ground, and the second space on that form was still empty when she put it away.

She went back up that bank with the frame and the bag and nobody at the top of it gave her anything and nobody at the bottom of it had asked her for anything at any point of the whole of that day.

---

Nothing was put on that page this morning and it stands at sixty-five lines. Thirty pages is what is behind that lane and not one of them came down it this morning. Those three lines have never once been read out in this district. That fifth of the five stands at five and nobody here has paid it. That count of seven months sits on nothing at all. Four hundred and eleven will be on that gatepost sheet in the morning.

That frame is square and it is standing on ground about a foot and a half further off that wall than it was the last four times, and nobody in that yard asked her why she moved it.
