# Chapter 1084: The Three Hundred And Sixty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Nothing In His Hand

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and nothing has been done with it in this basin since it was put there. That figure is eight hundred and twenty-three days old.

That board carries nine hundred and eighty-two marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and ninety-eight days and no wagon has come off it. At that rail nothing has been entered for one thousand and twelve days. Nine hundred and seventy-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Nobody ran that bid in four hundred and fifty days and nobody has run it since, so it is not a lot and it is not open this morning. This yard is six hundred and eighty-seven days behind the figure on the second line of that book. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and ninety-two days. It is seven hundred and sixty-two days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and one days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and sixty-five days. Six hundred and forty-one marks have been cut off that board. Along the edge of that second table stand six hundred and twenty-seven marks in chalk.

A clerk in this district has counted seven months and it stands on nothing whatever. Four documents have never come into this district. Four places in this district will let those three lines be read and a fifth will not. There are four conditions and no end is set on any of them. This basin has made fourteen things and nothing else. Five of those fourteen were never got here, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason went out loud in that yard, and that page could not be a place for one before the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on it before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That page stands at sixty-four lines and took no line on it, and nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that goes behind it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Three hundred and ninety-eight mornings is how long that second table has stood at the foot of that bank, and three hundred and forty-eight mornings is how long the near rail has stood on the near side of it, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-eight mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and sixty-seventh morning. It is neither that second table nor that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and twenty-third night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and twenty-two of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and ninth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and twenty-fourth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and fifty-seventh time running.

There is nothing at the back of that lot book and nothing has ever been put there. It lies open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and in the column on the page beside that there is one name, under a heading which still says whoever. There is a second space on that form and it is empty and nobody has ever been asked about it. That lane is at thirty pages and it brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and eighty-three days. About nine people are standing on that ground, which has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and thirteenth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of these and nobody here has ever gone up to look at either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight, paid and spent. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three came through this district and nobody heard them read in it.


---


He came along those boards at ten to eight on his way in, because he comes in past the boards every morning and has done for years, and he said nothing out loud to anybody at that hour, and nobody said anything out loud to him.

He got to that first table at about half past nine and put the cloth down flat on the boards where the frame is, so that he could see the casting without turning his head.


---


What he wanted off that morning was the near two feet of that top.

He had wanted them since before the middle of the winter. There are about fourteen feet of that top he can do and about two feet he cannot, and the two feet are at the near end, and he has never once told anybody how long he has wanted them or why he has not got them.


---


He got his weight over the middle of that top, where the ground is sound, and reached for the near section with no turn of that wheel anywhere in it, and pulled.

It came about two inches and stopped.

He did it again in his own time and it came about two inches more and stopped, and he did the whole length of that near section four times over about half an hour, and at the end of it the boards under that near end had gone down the width of two fingers.

That is what happens when a man reaches across the sound part of a thing to get at the part that has nothing under it, and he knows that now, and he did it anyway this morning because he wanted to see whether it would come out the same way twice.

It came out the same way twice.


---


At about eleven he went back to working on the turn of that wheel and did the middle of that top in about fourteen minutes, six feet in a pull, waiting each time to see whether it would go again.

"That wheel is at its speed all morning," said the man of about thirty-four, going along his own boards with the cloth over his arm, "and I say that to that frame most mornings and it has never once said anything back."

The man of thirty-eight did not say anything.

"You have not said a word in this yard that I have heard," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have got four of my own and I have said all four of them to a piece of pallet. That is not a complaint. I am going along my own boards."

He went along his own boards.

Nobody asked the man of thirty-eight anything at any point of that day, and nothing has ever been asked him anything, and he has got his own hand back on that cloth and is working on the turn of it.


---


At about two he went up that lane.

There is a lane at the far end of that ground, going up and out of the top of it, and it has been a lane longer than anybody in that yard has been asking what is at the top of it.

He went up it with nothing in his hand. Not a page and not a pencil and not a cloth and not a tool, and his coat pockets were empty because he emptied them at that table before he started, the way a man does who is going to work with both hands.

There was nobody at the top of that lane.

That is the whole of what was there. No doorway and no light in a doorway and no voice and nothing said to him from anywhere, and nobody put anything into his hand, and there was nowhere on that top for a man to sit and nothing on it for a man to be given.

He stood up there for about as long as it takes to look at four things.


---


The second gap.

There is a gap at the top of that bank that the light comes through in the morning, and every man at the foot of that yard has looked at that gap ten thousand mornings running without ever once looking along that bank.

There is a second one further along, about a hundred and forty feet further along, and it is not a hole. It is a place where the stone has come down out of the face of that bank in one piece and gone into the ground, and the ground behind it is lower than the ground in front of it, and the light goes through that gap in the afternoon and not in the morning.

He had never seen it because from the foot of that yard it is behind the second table and behind the man who puts tables up and behind a stack of boards, and from the top of that lane it is the second thing on that bank and it is as plain as anything.

He came back down that lane at about half past two with nothing in his hand, which is what he went up it with, and nobody in that yard saw him go and nobody in that yard saw him come back.


---


At half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of the second table with a bar on it, and she was there for the rest of that day.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another.

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and took all four off it from the top down and laid itself flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Eight hundred and nine mornings, the four of you."

Then the flat answer.

"Two hundred and fifty-seven mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had you twice today and got you both times, and there was a man in that yard go up that lane at two o'clock this afternoon and come back down it at half past, and I have not looked to see what he came back with, and there is nothing at the top of that lane that I know of, and I am not going to go and find out."

That was the two hundred and fifty-seventh time running, and nobody has ever asked him a thing about any part of it.


---


At about four he took a straight edge out of his coat and laid it the whole length of that top, near end to far end, and looked down the length of it, and then lifted it and put it back in his coat.

He does that at that hour and nobody has ever seen him do it.

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder, and wrote the three hundred and sixty-eighth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came off that cart face down and went up the bank.


---


He went up that slope behind the last board with his own hands empty, and the near two feet of that top is where it was this morning, and he is going to be working on the turn of it tomorrow.


---

That page stood at sixty-four lines at the end of that morning and it took no line on it. That lane has been at thirty pages all morning and it brought nothing down it, and a man going up that lane is not a page and a person not asking is not a page either, and it stands at thirty. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three are not read by anybody here and never have been. That fifth of that five is five and unpaid. Seven months has been counted and nothing whatever in this district holds it. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet and it will be on it in the morning.

Nobody in that yard asked him one thing about any part of that day.
