# Chapter 1056: The Three Hundred And Fortieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Gap In That Bank

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. It has been on that sheet for seven hundred and ninety-five days and no one has been near it.

That board carries nine hundred and fifty-four marks. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventy days with nothing off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and eighty-four days. Nine hundred and forty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, was not run, and is not a lot and not open. Six hundred and fifty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and sixty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and thirty-four days since the first day of the eighth month, and six hundred and seventy-three days past a printing that body four hundred miles off did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and thirty-seven days. Six hundred and thirteen marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and ninety-nine marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk and there is no list of anything for it here. Four documents are not owned in this district. Those three lines can be read in four places and not in a fifth of them. There are four conditions here with no end on any of them. Fourteen things have been made here and five were never got, and the fifth of that five is unpaid this morning as well.

Out loud in that yard, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, a reason was said, and the page at the back of that lot book had nowhere to hold a line for one until the two hundred and eighth morning, and so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing that goes at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning, and that page is at sixty-two lines and took no line this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and twenty mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirty-ninth morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. Mornings a man has stood at the foot of that bank: three hundred and forty. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-fifth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-four of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eighty-first of those mornings and got all four. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-fifth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. And that is the one hundred and ninety-sixth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer went to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-ninth time running.

That lot book lies open on those boards with nothing entered in it, five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and has not been asked of anybody. That lane carries thirty pages in the carriers' own hands and brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-five days. That ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are standing on it.

Nobody has ever been given either of those and nobody has walked them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

Ninety-eight was the toll and it is paid and spent. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district with nobody here hearing them read.
---

He had been at that far end of his own table since half past eight, and the foot and the bit of pallet were on the boards in front of him where he could see them, and he had a piece of it in his hand for the last half hour and had not done anything with it.

The bad end of it was the length of two fingers. He could feel where sound wood stopped and rubbish wood began, and you could not use any of it and you could not leave it on, because nothing will lie on a sound length with a soft length hanging off the end of it.

There was one saw in that yard that he wanted and it was not in that yard. The man who drove the cart had one under the boards of that cart and had had one under the boards of that cart for as long as the man of about thirty-four had been working at that table.

He got his coat off the back of the leg and found the cord he keeps in it, and he put the cord round the bad end and pulled it up against the frame of his own table and put his shoulder into it.

The pallet did not break. Pallet is not wood. Pallet is what they put a load on and then throw away, and it is built to be stood on.

---

The cart came through the gap in that bank at about two.

The man of about twenty-nine drove it and it was loaded high with pale stuff and a strap over it, and he came through that gap at the speed he comes through it, and he did not stop, and he did not put his head round at anybody, and about nine people at the foot of that bank stepped aside the way they step aside.

The man of about thirty-four was standing at the gap with his hand up and his palm open.

The cart went past his hand at about the pace of a man walking.

There was a saw under that cart with the handle standing up out of the boards where anybody in this yard could see it, and the man of about twenty-nine looked at the hand, and then at the man, and then at the road.

He said nothing out loud. He has not said one word out loud in that yard on any morning of that run and there is nobody waiting to hear him start.

The cart went through the gap and went up the bank and the strap and the pale stuff went with it, and about four people stepped back in and went to what they were doing, and nobody in that yard said one word behind that cart.

---

He went at it again with the frame of his own table.

It took him the best part of three goes and the third one put a splinter down his forearm as long as his hand and he stood there afterwards with his arm hanging while about four people in that yard found a reason to be somewhere else.

The bad end came off in one piece and went about four feet and stopped under the second table.

The sound end was a foot and a bit long and about four inches wide, and it was square at the cut, and there was nothing wrong with it anywhere.

He held it up in the light and turned it over and looked at both faces of it the way a man looks at a thing that has cost him something.

That is the shape of it. That is the shape of the thing a man needs when a corner is out of square and the ground under it has gone.

He put his hand out flat over his own boards where the piece had been lying and there was nothing on them.

He looked about four feet along the boards and about four feet back and there was nowhere in that yard to lay a foot and a bit of sound pallet that would hold a corner, and he had known there was not since before the frost.

So he put the sound end back down on top of his own boards at the far end where he can see it, and squared it with two fingers so that it lay straight, and went and got his cloth.

---

The man of about thirty-two came along his own boards about three with the cloth over his arm.

"You have had something off that pallet."

"I have had the rubbish end off it."

"That is a job for a saw, not for your own table."

"That is a job for a saw," said the man of about thirty-four, "and the saw in this yard is under the boards of a cart that does not stop."

The man of about thirty-two stood and thought about the gap in that bank.

"He has gone up to the top of it with the load," he said.

"He has."

"He will be coming down empty at about four, at a walk, with nothing on that cart, and he will go through that gap at the same speed he goes through it with a load."

"He will," said the man of about thirty-four, "and he will not stop for a saw."

The man of about thirty-two went along his own top with the cloth.

The boy came down off the slope at about half past three and counted the nine spaces in those boards twice and got nine twice and did not ask anybody what the piece of pallet on the far end of that table was for.

---

About four, the hand went up on that wall again and got all four of them off it, and it came down flat on the stone under the lowest one and lay there.

"Two hundred and twenty-nine mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and there is a man in this yard who has spent his morning getting a thing ready and has nowhere to put it, and he will do it again tomorrow, and I have been the man who got a thing ready and had nowhere to put it since before the middle of the winter, and I have not once got to the end of saying so."

That was the two hundred and twenty-ninth time running, and about nineteen people heard it, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen in the holder and wrote the three hundred and fortieth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and they came off the cart face down and went up the bank, and about four people said the goodnight, and the turner banked the wheel and stopped it with his hand on the frame the way he does at the end of every day of that run.

The piece of pallet was on the boards at the far end of that table, square at the cut, where he could see it.

---

That page stood at sixty-two lines and took no line this morning. That lane stands at thirty pages and nothing came down it this morning. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have gone through this district unread. The fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months has been counted by a clerk and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet at the gatepost and nobody has done anything with it.
