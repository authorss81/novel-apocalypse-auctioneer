# Chapter 1095: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Nine Spaces In A Stack

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and no part of this basin has ever found a use for it. That figure has been on that sheet eight hundred and thirty-four days.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety-three marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and nine days and no wagon has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and twenty-three days. Nine hundred and eighty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open and it was not run and it is not a lot and not open. Six hundred and ninety-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and three days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and seventy-three days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and twelve days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-six days back. Six hundred and fifty-two marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and thirty-eight marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and nothing in this district carries it. Four documents have never once been in this district. Those three lines are readable in four places here and in no fifth place in it. Four conditions are set out in this district and no end has been set upon any one of them. This basin has made fourteen things and no more than those fourteen. Five of those fourteen never came here and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book did not take a line for a reason until the two hundred and eighth morning came, so that nothing on it earlier than that was a line for one, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been said out loud in that yard for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that page and that page is at sixty-five lines and it took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and ninth mornings and that near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and fifty-ninth mornings, and the man who fitted it is not to be thanked. Three hundred and seventy-ninth mornings is how long a man has stood at that foot of that bank on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood there for the three hundred and seventy-eighth morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and thirty-fourth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and thirty-three of them. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twentieth of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and thirty-fifth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. The two hundred and sixty-eighth time running was this morning for what he says to that stone after the four of them.

Nothing is at the back of that lot book and nothing has ever been put there, and it lies open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after that holds one name under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form, it is empty, and nobody here has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane are thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and nothing came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-four days. About nine people were on that ground at ten to eight and it has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-fourth afternoon running and one word was all that afternoon had in it. Neither of these two has been given to anybody in this district, and nobody here has ever gone up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and nothing of it is left over. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and no voice in this district has ever read them out.

---

The stove was going before the light came over the top of that bank and the turner was at that frame at about six and a half and did not put his heel on it, and nobody in that yard started it.

What the boy wanted off that morning was nine spaces in a stack and not eight and not ten.

---

He has measured those spaces out on the boards himself, once, with a stub of pencil he keeps behind his ear and a knotted string he made himself, and the nine of them are the width a board lies on and no more than that, and they have been the right width for as long as he can remember and they are the reason a day's boards go up that slope in one run instead of two.

The stack comes down off the cart face down at half past four and he lays it out from the bottom, and by about seven he had seven of the nine spaces filled and the eighth filling.

The ninth board was a foot longer than the others.

---

He had it on his knee at about half past seven when he found it out, because a board that is a foot longer than the one under it does not lie down on a stack.

It was not a bad board. It was the same width and the same thickness and it had been in the same load as the rest of them, and it was about a foot longer at the end nearest his own left knee, and there was no way on that ground to shorten it.

"Nine spaces," he said out loud to himself, and then, because there was nobody in that yard at that hour to say it to, he said it again. "Nine spaces and a foot of board over the end."

---

At about ten to eight he was at the near end of that stack with the light lying along the top of it.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "There is nothing behind that number and I have not been asked anything this week."

The man of about thirty-two was putting his own frame up at the far end of that ground and he heard the boy.

"You have said that number at this hour every morning I have been in this yard."

"I have."

"What is it for?"

"It is the number of pages behind that lane and it is all I am asked for at this hour," said the boy, "and you have never asked me for one more thing and I have got used to that, so do not start today."

Then he went back down his own boards to the foot of that stack and left the man of about thirty-two where he was.

---

The turner came along that ground at about a quarter past nine and said the thing he says.

"The boards go up while the light is on them and not one minute after," he said. "And that ninth space is not a space if a foot of board is hanging over the end of it."

He said it to the ground and not to anybody and then he went back to that frame and put his hands in his pockets.

---

The boy wanted that ninth space to hold what went into it and he wanted the whole stack to go up in one run, and both of those were still standing at about eleven, and the only thing standing between them was a foot of board that was a foot too long.

He tried it three ways.

He tried it with the long board on the bottom of the ninth space, which lifted the whole end of the stack up about two inches and left the eight spaces above it all a little bit out of line.

He tried it at the back of the ninth space, which put the long end out over the drop behind the stack where a man coming up the slope in the dark would have his foot on it.

He tried it on the top, which covered six of the eight other spaces and made a stack that was not a stack.

---

"You are trying to make a space hold a board it has not got room for."

The man of about thirty-four was coming along with the cloth over his arm and a foot and about four inches of sound pallet out in the other hand, which he has carried for a long time and has laid in nowhere.

"I have tried it three ways and I have run out of ways."

"Then move the space," said the man of about thirty-four. "It is a mark on a board and not a rule."

"That is the whole of it," said the boy. "It is a mark on a board. I have been treating it like a rule for about three years and nobody ever said so out loud and you have said it out loud and I do not know what to do about that."

"Measure the ninth one wider," said the man of about thirty-four. "You have got a knotted string and a stub of pencil behind your ear and you have got about four hours before the light goes off that ground. Do the ninth one to the board and leave the other eight where they are."

---

He stood there with that for a minute and then he got the string out and he did it.

The ninth space is about a foot and a bit wider than the other eight, and it does not look like the other eight, and it is the ninth space and it is the right size for the ninth board, and the other eight are where they have always been.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table with her basket on the ground at her feet.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four voices in that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.

At about two the boy got his knotted string back behind his ear and the stub of pencil in his pocket and went to look at the ninth space again from the other side, and it was still right.

---

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and brought four figures off the top of it one after another, and it lay flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"All four of you, and no more of you."

And after that came the other part of it.

"Two hundred and sixty-eight mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had the two of you twice and got them both times, and there is a boy at that stack this morning who has just found out that a mark on a board is not a rule, which took me the best part of a morning and him seventeen years, and I am not going to say one word about that either."

That was the two hundred and sixty-eighth time running and nobody in this district has ever asked him a thing about any part of it.

---

The clerk came down that row of boards at about four with the pen in the holder and read that whole morning out loud before her hand went near any part of it, and then she wrote the three hundred and seventy-ninth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards, four of that nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards went down off the cart face down and up that bank.

The whole of that stack went up in one run, including the ninth space with the foot of board on it that is a foot wider than the other eight, and the boy went up behind the last of them.

He did not write anything down that afternoon and he did not tell anybody at the top of that bank what he had worked out about the marks on that board.

---

That page was still at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it had taken no line on it. Nothing came down that lane all morning and it is at thirty pages behind it. Not one of those three lines has ever been read out in this district. The fifth of those five is five and unpaid still. Seven months is counted here and there is nothing in this district for it. That gatepost sheet will be carrying the same four hundred and eleven in the morning.

Nine spaces went up that slope in one run this morning and the ninth one of them is a foot wider than the other eight, and not one person in that yard had heard the yard go quiet.
