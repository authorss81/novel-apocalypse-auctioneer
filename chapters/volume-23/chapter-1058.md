# Chapter 1058: The Three Hundred And Forty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Same Nine

Nobody has come at that sheet with a thing to do, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. It has been on that post for seven hundred and ninety-seven days.

That board carries nine hundred and fifty-six marks cut off it. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventy-two days and has not had a wagon come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and eighty-six days. Nine hundred and forty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, and never once in all of that time. It was not run and it is not a lot and it is not open. Six hundred and sixty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and sixty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and thirty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and seventy-five days past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and thirty-nine days ago. Six hundred and fifteen marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and one marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk in this district and nothing in this district holds that count. Four documents are not owned here. Those three lines can be read in four places and not in a fifth place. Four conditions stand here with no end on any of them. Fourteen things have been made here. Five were never got and the fifth of that five stands unpaid.

Out loud in that yard, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, a reason was said, and the page at the back of that lot book could not hold a line for one until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was ever a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing of the sort that goes at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning. That page was at sixty-two lines and took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-two mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and twenty-two mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and forty-first morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-two mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-seventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-six of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eighty-third of those mornings and got all four. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-seventh afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. And that is the one hundred and ninety-eighth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and thirty-first time running.

That lot book lies open on those boards with nothing entered in it and five lines ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them carries one name under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has been asked to fill it. That lane carries thirty pages in the carriers' own hands and nothing has come down it today. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-seven days. Public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, that ground, with about nine people on it this morning.

Nobody has ever been given either of those and nobody has ever gone up them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and it is not paid twice. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district without anybody in it hearing them read.
---

He counted the nine spaces at half past six, before there was any light on them, and got nine, and then he counted them backwards at about a quarter to eight and got nine again, which he had done once before in two years and about three weeks and had not repeated since.

At ten to eight he said the lane count out loud at those boards with the light still flat on the top of the bank.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "And there is no figure after it this morning."

The clerk had the book open against the rail and shut it again.

"You have got no figure after it."

"I have got no figure after it," said the boy, "and I have said that out loud, so that nobody in this yard has to sit there wondering."

"You are seventeen."

"I am."

"Two years and about three weeks on these boards."

"Yes," said the boy. "And I have got nine spaces and thirty pages and I have got nothing that comes out different, and I want one."

The clerk did not ask him what he wanted one for, and he did not tell her.

---

He spent the morning trying to get a number out of that yard that came out different twice in one day, which is the one thing he has never once managed.

He counted the times a hand went up on that wall. Four, at about a quarter to eight, and four again at about four, and it is always four because there are four figures on that wall.

He counted the boards the light came over first. One, and it is the same board every morning, and it is the third board from the end where the bank is lowest.

He counted how many times anything came through the gap in that bank. One, and it came through once with a load, and it will come through again empty at about four at the speed it comes through.

He put his boot heel against the bottom board of that stack and marked it with the side of the pencil and walked up the bank and came back down and counted from the mark. Ten boards. He counted them again from the mark. Ten.

He did it a third time starting at the other end and counted nine and then corrected himself to ten, because the short one at the bottom is not a space, and he has known that about a year, and knowing it did not make the number different.

He counted the spaces between those boards again at about eleven with the sun on them and got nine, and again at about two with the sun off them and got nine, and the only difference was that he was warmer.

---

At about half past three he was standing about nine feet off the far end of the man of about thirty-four's table looking down at a foot and a bit of pallet lying on those boards with a square cut at one end of it.

"That is a new cut."

"That is a new cut," said the man of about thirty-four.

"You broke the rubbish end off it against your own frame."

"I did."

"Why have you not laid it in."

"Because there is nothing in this yard for it to go under," said the man of about thirty-four, "and you have asked me that question twice this week and I have given you the same answer twice and it is the same answer both times."

The boy looked at it for a while.

"I count things all day," he said. "I have never once been asked what it is for, and you have never once asked me what I get, and I have decided that is a fair arrangement and I am not going to be the one to break it by asking you something you have already said no to."

He went back to his boards.

---

The turner came through the gap at about ten past four with his hand off the frame and stopped at the near end of the boards.

"That mark on the stack is not yours to make," said the man of about thirty-two, from about nine feet along.

"It is on my boards."

"It is on the boards that the cart stands against, and if that mark moves four inches in a morning the boards have gone and nobody in this yard will know how far they went."

"Then it is a good mark," said the boy. "It will be wrong on some morning and then we will know."

The man of about thirty-two went along his own top with the cloth and did not say anything else about it, and the turner did not either, and neither of them has said anything about it since.

"They will go up at about half past four today."

"They went up at about half past four yesterday."

"They went up at about half past four the morning before that," said the turner, "and the morning before that one, and I have been in this yard thirty-one years and I have never once known them to go up early, and I have never once known them to go up late, and there is nobody in this yard who ever came here to find that out."

He went up the slope and did not say anything else about it.

The boy stood at the end of the boards with the pencil still in his pocket and did not count anything at all for longer than he had ever gone without counting, and the light came off the top of the bank while he was standing there doing it.

That is a thing about the yard that was the same this morning as it was yesterday, and he had wanted one thing in it that was not.

He went and stood at the end of the boards and got the pencil out of his pocket and put it back in his pocket again.

At half past four the light had already gone off those boards, and they came off the cart face down and went up the bank, and about four people said the goodnight, and the turner banked the wheel where it stood and went on standing at the frame until there was no light left on that ground at all.

Orren Pike said the count out loud one more time before he went up the slope.

"Thirty," he said, "and nothing came down that lane, and there is no figure after it."

Then he went up the bank and counted the nine spaces on the way up and got nine, and he said them out loud on the slope where about four people at the top of it could hear him and nobody did.

---

That page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines at the end of that morning and took no line. That lane stands at thirty pages and came down with nothing this morning. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page are still unread in this district this morning. The fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months has been counted and it is on nothing here. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost and no one has been near it.
