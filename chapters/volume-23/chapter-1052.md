# Chapter 1052: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Straight Edge Laid Across

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and has been four hundred and eleven since before that rail went in. It is seven hundred and ninety-one days old and nobody has ever taken it down to see.

The board carries nine hundred and fifty marks. Nothing has come off the train on that siding for one thousand two hundred and sixty-six days, and nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and eighty days, and nine hundred and forty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and was never run and is neither open nor a lot this morning. Six hundred and fifty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line this yard stands. The rule said out loud in this yard has stood six hundred and sixty days. It has been seven hundred and thirty days since the first day of the eighth month, and six hundred and sixty-nine days past a printing that body four hundred miles off did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights lies eight hundred and thirty-three days back. Six hundred and nine marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and ninety-five marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk and no list of anything in this district exists for it. Four documents are not owned here. Those three lines can be read in four places in this district and not in a fifth. There are four conditions in this district and an end has never been put to any of them. Fourteen things have been made here, five were never got, and the fifth of that five is unpaid and is not going to be paid this morning.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book did not have anywhere to put one until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on it before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That page is at sixty-two lines and no line went on it this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Three hundred and sixty-six mornings is how long that second table has stood at the foot of that bank, and three hundred and sixteen mornings is how long the near rail has stood on the near side of it, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground this morning for the three hundred and thirty-fifth morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-six mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and seventy-seventh of those mornings, and got all four. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-first night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-first afternoon running and gave that afternoon one word. And that is the one hundred and ninety-second morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer went to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-fifth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing in it and five lines ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds a single name under a heading that still says whoever. Nobody has been asked what goes in the second space on that form, including by being asked to name it. Thirty pages have come down that lane in their own carriers' hands and not one of them came down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-one days. The ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are standing on it.

Both of those are distances and nobody in this district has ever gone over either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight was paid and is spent and is done with. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have gone through this place with nobody here hearing them read.
---

He had the table up before the light was on the boards, and it stood for the three hundred and thirty-fifth morning, and he put his hand on the near leg and pushed and knew again what he had known since the spring.

The corner was out of square. He had got the frame itself square three mornings back with a wedge cut out of the fire-split and it was still square, and the leg was still in the right place, and the whole thing still sat down to one side at that corner.

That is what a man gets for putting a square frame onto ground that is going.

He got his straight edge out of the wheelhouse, the long one with the painted end, and laid it across the top of his own boards, and where it lay along the length it touched at two ends and had a hollow in the middle of about half an inch, and where he laid it across the width it lay flat on the same two boards and stood off the third.

That was the whole of the trouble and it was not the frame and it was not the ground and it was not the corner.

The boards of that top had cupped. They had taken a bow across their width somewhere in a stack they had lain in before he ever had them, and the weather in this basin does what it likes to a board that has done that, and the only thing a man can do about it is turn a board over and see whether the cup went with it.

He got his heel under the near end and pushed up on the frame while his shoulder was against the wedge, and it stood, and he left it standing and went up the slope for his coat.

---

At ten to eight the boy was at those boards with the light still flat on the top of the bank.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and there is nothing after it."

"You say that every morning and it has never once been different."

"It has never once been different," said the boy, "and I am not going to be the one who finds out what it means when it is."

He went about four feet along the boards and stopped at that table and looked at the far end of it.

"Your leg is up on something."

"One I cut out of the fire-split myself."

"That is not holding it up right. The leg is up and the top is off the frame at that end. Look at the light on it."

The man of about thirty-two looked at the light on it.

"The wood has cupped," he said. "That is all that is. There is no wedge in this world for a board that has bowed, because the board pulls itself back and it pulls the wedge out with it."

"Can you turn them over."

"I can turn them over if I take the top off, and I cannot take the top off on my own in a morning, and I am not standing here telling you that to anybody, I am telling it to you because you counted nine spaces on those boards this morning and I have noticed you are the only one out here who looks at a thing before he touches it."

The boy thought about that.

"You want me to say the number out loud," he said.

"I do not want you to do anything at all."

"It is your table and not my table," said Orren Pike, and went up the slope, and got to the top of it before he turned round and said down, "It is off by about half an inch and that is not a figure, that is a thing I saw."

Then he was gone.

---

She came down that bank at about half past twelve with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of that second table and put a bar on her own pan and let it want to follow the wind until it came still.

The man of about thirty-eight went past within four feet of her and of that bench and made no sound at either pass, and that is a thing about that table and not about him.

At about one the man of about thirty-four came along his own boards and stopped at the far end of his own table, and took a foot and a bit of pallet out of his coat and put it down flat on top of those boards at the far end where he can see it from where he works, and squared it up with two fingers so that it lay straight.

He stood looking at it.

He did not say anything about it. The man of about thirty-two was four feet along with a straight edge in his hand and did not ask him what it was for, and the boy was up the slope, and the turner was at the frame, and about four people in that yard went on with what they were doing.

The man of about forty-eight put his number up into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one in the voice he keeps for it and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went over that bank and did not come back.

---

About three he took the top off his own table.

There is a length of about six feet of it and the two men at the ends of it carry it like a board is carried, which is one at each end and both of them knowing what the other is doing.

"Set it down on the boards face up," he said.

"It is going to cup the other way and you will have done it for nothing."

"It will cup the other way for about a week and then it will lie down," said the man of about thirty-four, "and the seven hundred and something boards in that stack at the back will not, and that is the difference between them."

The boards came over one at a time. He had known what he would find before he had the first one turned, and he found it anyway, and the middle one of the five was the worst of them and had a dark band across it where the water had stood in the stack.

"That one is not worth having," said the man of about thirty-four.

"That one is the one that was carrying the corner," he said. "That is why the corner is down. Everything else in this yard has been telling me it is the ground."

They laid them back face down and it took longer than he had thought it would, and they got the frame up again about ten minutes before the light came off the boards, and he put his hand flat on the middle board and it was flat, and the corner was square, and he stood there and looked at the light going off it.

"A day," said the man of about thirty-four, picking up his cloth.

"It is a day and I have had the morning."

"You have had about half of it."

"I have had the half that tells me what the day is for," he said, and that was the whole of what he said out loud on that morning, and he did not say it to anybody in particular, and it went into that air and up that bank and nothing came back down out of it.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four of them off it and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"Two hundred and twenty-five mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got all four of you off you this morning and not one of them is going to tell me whether I read them right, and I have stopped minding, and it has taken me every one of those mornings to stop minding."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and twenty-fifth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen in the holder and he heard every word of it from his own table, and then she wrote the three hundred and thirty-sixth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and they came off the cart face down and went up the bank. About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that is said at the end of a day in that yard, and the turner banked the wheel and stopped it with his hand on the frame the way he does at the end of every day of that run.

---

That page stood at sixty-two lines and took no line this morning and there is no sixty-third of it. That lane stands at thirty pages and nothing came down it. Three lines out of the first thirty-three on that back page have never been read in this district. The fifth of that five is unpaid. A clerk has counted seven months and there is no list anywhere that holds that figure. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet at that gatepost.
