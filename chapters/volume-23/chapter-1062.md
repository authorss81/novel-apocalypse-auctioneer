# Chapter 1062: The Three Hundred And Forty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And One Line On The Page

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. It has been on that sheet for eight hundred and one days, and nobody in this basin has ever gone near that post.

Nine hundred and sixty marks have been cut into that board. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventy-six days without a wagon coming off it. At that rail nothing has been entered for nine hundred and ninety days. Nine hundred and fifty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open before it was not run, and it is not a lot and not open. This yard is six hundred and sixty-five days behind the figure on the second line of that book. The rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and seventy days. It has been seven hundred and forty days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is six hundred and seventy-nine days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and forty-three days back. Six hundred and nineteen marks have been cut off that board. Along the edge of that second table there are six hundred and five marks in chalk.

Seven months is counted by a clerk in this district and is on nothing here. Four documents are not owned here. There are four places here where those three lines can be read and never a fifth. Four conditions stand on the list and not one of them has an end on it. Fourteen things have been made in this district. Five were never got, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and there was no page at the back of that lot book for a reason to go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

One reason was said out loud from the top of that bank this morning and it did not come down in anybody's mouth after it. One line went on the page at the back of that lot book, and that line carries this morning and not what was said in it. She was not thanked for it and nobody answered her. That page stood at sixty-two lines from the first board up to the last one off and stands at sixty-three lines.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-six mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and twenty-six mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-six mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and forty-fifth morning, and it is neither the second table nor it is the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and first night of that run, having slept on seven hundred of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eighty-seventh of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and second morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and thirty-fifth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and has never been asked of anybody. Thirty pages have gone down that lane in their own carriers' hands and nothing came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and sixty-one days. It has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and about nine people are on it.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones this afternoon for the two hundred and ninety-first afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Nobody here has ever been given either of these and nobody here has walked either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district without one person in it hearing them read.

---

She was at those boards before the light with the book open against the rail and the pen in the holder, and she did not write anything, because there was nothing yet to write.

She has two forms of words. One of them is for a morning when nothing has gone out loud in that yard that goes at the back of that book, and it is the common of the two and she has had it ready since she was twenty-six. The other one is for a morning when something has, and she has used it four times in fifteen years and she could say every word of it.

What she has never had is words for the third kind, and she has never met one either.

---

At ten to eight the boy said the count out loud at those boards.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and no figure after it, and I have said no figure after it every morning this week."

"You have."

"And nobody has ever asked me what either of them is for," said the boy, "including you."

"I have not asked you that."

"You have not," he agreed. "It is the arrangement, and I am the one who would break it, so I do not."

He went off up the slope with the pencil in his pocket and she shut the book and did not write anything in it.

---

At about eleven the woman of about fifty-two came down that bank with a frame under her arm and the bag on the other one, and set the frame flat on the boards at the near end and took the form out of the bag and put it inside the frame, because the wind goes over the top of that frame and lifts anything that is not in it.

She knelt down on the boards and looked at the second space on that form.

The clerk stood at the rail about nine feet off with her hands in her sleeves and let her look, because there is nothing a clerk can do with a space on somebody else's form, and she has never once said so out loud, and she is not going to start.

At about half past twelve the woman of about fifty-two got up off the boards, put the form back in the bag and the frame under her arm, and went up the bank with both.

---

At about one a reason came out of the top of that bank.

It came out of a woman standing on the top of that bank in the ordinary voice, at the speed a person uses for a thing they mean, and it went down the face of that bank and out across that ground and about nineteen people heard every word of it, and it did not come back into that yard in anybody's mouth.

The clerk heard all of it from the rail. The boy heard all of it from about nine feet along the boards. The turner heard it from the frame and could not have told anybody afterwards what any of it had been, because a voice across that yard comes in on a man who has stood beside a wheel in it for thirty-one years as a shape and not as words.

Nobody answered her.

Nobody said one word back up that bank. Nobody thanked her. She did not come down, and she was not on that ground again that day, and about four people at the top of that bank said afterwards that it came out of nowhere, and said nothing else about it to anybody.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off the top of that bank and did not come back.

About four feet off the near end of that first table the man of thirty-eight had the cloth in his hand and had not put one word out loud into that yard all morning.

The wheel was at its speed the whole of that morning, and the turner was at the frame, and nobody in that yard put anything to him at any point of it.

---

At about two he came along those boards and stopped at the end of them.

"That book is open on the same page again."

"It is open on the same page because nobody has written on the other one."

"You could write in it."

"I could," said the clerk. "There has not been one morning come along that bank in fifteen years where I could not have put something in it."

"Then why is it still open."

"I have a pen on me," said the clerk, "and you have known that for fifteen years, and I am not going to stand here at two o'clock in the afternoon and explain my own hand to you."

"I was not asking you to."

"No," she said. "You were not."

He looked at her for a moment, and then he went away up the slope with his rag over his shoulder.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and took the four off it in the order they come and set his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Seven hundred and eighty-seven mornings, the four of you."

Then the flat answer, which is what he gives that stone at that hour and has given it since before the middle of the winter.

"Two hundred and thirty-five mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had you twice today and got you both times, and I have never once asked anybody in this district to say a thing over again so that I would hear it better, and I am not going to start on a morning when somebody up that bank has said one thing out loud and gone away."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and thirty-fifth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

---

At ten past four she came along those boards with the pen still in the holder and read that morning out loud before her hand had been near any part of it.

"The three hundred and forty-sixth morning," she said. "One reason was said out loud from the top of that bank at about one and it did not come down in anybody's mouth after it, and one line goes on at the back of this book, and the line carries this morning and leaves out what was said in it, and I have given you all of that before I have put the nib down."

Then she wrote the three hundred and forty-sixth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and below her own hand she took one line.

She could not have told anybody afterwards what was in that reason. She has gone back over the whole of it twice since and it has not come back into her head as a thing with a subject in it. What she has is that it was said, and the sound of that woman's voice on the top of that bank saying it, and a line on that page.

The line went over the worn place in that paper about four inches in from the foot of the page and it did not sit level with the lines above it, and she has never once tried to correct for it in fifteen years and she did not today.

Nobody thanked her. Nobody at the foot of that bank said one word to her about any part of it.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight. The boards came off the cart face down and went up the bank, and the turner shut that wheel down with his palm on the casting and waited for it before he followed them up.

---

That page stood at sixty-three lines when the covers came over it, having stood at sixty-two when the morning began, and it took that one line and no other. That lane stands at thirty pages and nothing came down it this morning. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page are still unread in this district. The fifth of that five is five and it is unpaid. Seven months has been counted and there is nothing in this district for that count to sit on. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet at that gatepost and it will be there in the morning.
