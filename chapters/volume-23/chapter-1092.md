# Chapter 1092: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Six Feet And Then Two

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it has been left exactly there in all that time. Its age on that sheet this morning is eight hundred and thirty-one days.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and six days and no wagon has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and twenty days. There are nine hundred and eighty-one days between the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days, was not run, and is neither a lot nor open this morning. The figure on the second line of that book is six hundred and ninety-five days ahead of this yard. The rule said out loud in that yard has stood seven hundred days. It has been seven hundred and seventy days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and nine days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-three days back. Six hundred and forty-nine marks have been cut off that board. There are six hundred and thirty-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted here and it is on nothing at all. Those four documents have never once been in this district. Those three lines stand in four places in this district and in no fifth place. Four conditions are set out in this district and no end has been set on one of them. This basin has made fourteen things and nothing outside the fourteen. Five of those fourteen were never got here at all and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the back page of that lot book had no business taking a line for one until the two hundred and eighth morning came, so that nothing written on it earlier than that was ever a line for a reason, and it stands counted nowhere, and the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in that yard has never been given out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that page and it stands at sixty-five lines and took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Four hundred and sixth mornings is how long that second table has stood at the foot of that bank, and three hundred and fifty-sixth is how long the near rail has stood on the near side of it, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-sixth mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventy-fifth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and thirty-first night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and thirty of them. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and seventeenth of those mornings and had all four of them. That is the two hundred and thirty-second morning running with nobody having asked him anything. His flat answer went to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and sixty-fifth time running.

That lot book lies open on those boards and there is nothing in it. One of its pages carries five ruled lines, and the column on the page after that carries one name under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form and it has stayed empty and nobody has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane are thirty pages in the carriers' own hands, and it brought nothing down here this morning. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-one days. About that many people as ever are stood on that ground, which has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-first afternoon running and one word was all that afternoon came to. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of these two and nobody here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight and has been paid and spent and none of it is left. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have come through this district unread by anybody in it.

---

A table that has stood at the foot of that bank as long as that one has stood wants two things on a morning and cannot have both of them.

What he wanted off that morning was to have the whole of that top off the trestles and square to the near rail before the light went off the eleven feet of boards behind him.

He worked it in two-foot lengths, the way it has to be worked, and for two reasons. The middle of that table is over ground that has not moved since anybody can remember, and out on the sound ground a two-foot length comes up in one pull. The near two feet are over the place where the ground was opened up years ago and filled in again badly, and there a two-foot length has to be persuaded.

Six feet of that top came up in one pull at about seven.

---

The near two feet did not.

He set them down and squared them against the rail with the back of his heel the way he has set down about four hundred tops, and the end of that top stood about the thickness of a coin proud of the line the rail ought to be on.

He did it again with a straight edge.

He did not do it again with a straight edge, because there is one in that yard and he is not going into that frame to get it.

He picked the top up, turned it round a quarter turn, put it down the same way and got the same coin.

---

"You are putting your top down against that rail," said the man of about thirty-four, coming along his own boards with the cloth over his arm, "and that rail is not on a straight line over the last two feet."

"I know it is not."

"Then what are you doing?"

"I am putting my top down against it," said the man of about thirty-two, "and getting a coin out of it every time, and I have just turned it a quarter turn to see whether that end is the fault of the top or of the rail."

"Which was it?"

"The rail," he said. "I could hear it. There is a hollow worn in the top of it about a foot and a half back from the end and everything from that hollow forward is lower than the rest of it, and whoever cut that hollow did it with something that was always on the same line."

He put his hand flat on the top of the rail, well back, and the rail was up level with the heel of his hand, and he put it flat on the rail at the end and the rail was down.

"There is nothing wrong with that rail," he said. "It is the only thing in this yard that is not a person."

---

At about ten to eight the boy was at those boards.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike.

"You have said that every morning I have been in this yard and I have never once known what it is for."

"It is a number of pages," said the boy, "and it is behind that lane, and I have told you that every morning as well."

Then he went off up the slope and put the pencil behind his ear.

---

The turner came along the far end of his boards at about a quarter past nine with the rag in his hand and did not stop, and said the same thing he says on most mornings, which is where the boards go up and when.

"They go up while the light is on them and not one minute after," he said, "and the ground under that cart has had about nine feet of boards lying on it since before the light and you can feel where they were."

"I have felt it," said the man of about thirty-two.

He did not say anything else and went back down to that frame, and nobody in that yard put a question to him about anything at any point of the morning.

---

Seven hundred days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood.

Nobody in that yard looked up at it, and the wheel went at the speed it goes at and it did not stop for it.

---

He got six feet of that top squared and set and wedged by about eleven, and then he came back to the near two feet, and he put them down, and the coin was there.

Then he took the top off the two trestles at the near end and shifted the whole thing out by the width of his hand, so the near two feet of that top ran on good ground instead of the ground that had been opened, and he got four feet of the near end square in one pull.

That left two feet at the very near end with nothing to sit on.

---

He stood looking at it.

What he wanted off that morning was in the top and not in the ground, and he had it in the top, and the two feet at the end that had no ground under them were the price of it.

Then he put his hand under the corner of that top and lifted it and let it down on its own weight four times.

It did not rock. It did not move at the near end and it did not move at the far end, and the two trestles under it were both down and both level, and there was about a finger's width of daylight under the middle of it where the ground had gone in.

"Leave it," said the man of about thirty-four. "It has been up a long while and a finger of daylight under the middle of a table is not a fault."

"A table that rocks is a fault."

"A table that rocks at the end of a day when the light has been on that ground all afternoon is a different thing," said the man of about thirty-four, "and that one is not up to you and not up to me and we can both stand here and watch it."

---

He got the bar off at about half past twelve and stood at the far end of it with his hands behind him.

The woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan at half past twelve, about four feet off the near end of that second table, holding the bar still until it had stopped wanting the wind.

The man of about forty-eight put his number out at half past one into about four hundred yards of air, and four voices in that yard carried it on out of step with one another.

---

The five boards with the notches cut across their undersides were in his stack at the near end of that table, face up, in the order they had come out of it, and they had been that way since he turned them over and he did not touch them.

He had not told anybody what was on the underside of them and he had not worked it out either, and there was about as much chance of his working it out before the light went as there was of that two-foot length coming square to that rail.

---

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and took four figures off it from the top down, and laid itself flat on the stone under the lowest one, and lay there.

"The four of you, first one and last one."

Then what he said after that.

"Two hundred and sixty-five mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had the two of you twice and got them both times, and there is a man at the end of that yard who has been told what is wrong with his own top by three people in one week, and I am not going to be the fourth of you, and I have not asked him one thing about it and I am not going to."

That was the two hundred and sixty-fifth time running and there is nobody in this district who has ever asked him a question about any of it.

---

The clerk came down those boards at about four with the pen in the holder and read the whole of that morning out loud before she put her hand near any part of it, and then she wrote the three hundred and seventy-sixth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

Nobody said anything to her about it before or after, and nobody has ever asked her what a line on that page is for.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and four of the nineteen said the goodnight and the boards came down off that cart face down and went up that bank.

He took his top off the two trestles at the near end and carried it to the stack, and the two feet at the very near end went down on the ground with a sound in them that the other ten feet of that top do not make.

He put the bar up and squared the trestles and left the frame of that table standing empty, and about a finger's width of daylight was under nothing at all by then.

---

That page stood at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it took no line on it. Nothing came down that lane all morning and the count behind it stayed at thirty pages. Nobody in this district has ever heard one of those three lines read. The fifth of those five is five and unpaid and nobody here has paid it. Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing in it that sits on that count. That sheet at that gatepost will carry the same four hundred and eleven in the morning.

Six feet of that top came up in one pull. The near two feet are two feet of a table and they are square now to four feet of rail that was never straight, and no man in that yard has said one word out loud about any of it.
