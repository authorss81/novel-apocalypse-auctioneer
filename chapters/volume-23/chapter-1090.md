# Chapter 1090: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Cart Back Through The Gap

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it has been left on that sheet in this basin since the morning it went up. That figure is eight hundred and twenty-nine days old.

That board carries nine hundred and eighty-eight marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and four days without a wagon off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and eighteen days. Nine hundred and seventy-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was not run, so it is not a lot and not open this morning. Six hundred and ninety-three days is how far this yard is behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and ninety-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. It is seven hundred and sixty-eight days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and seven days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-one days back. Six hundred and forty-seven marks have been cut off that board. Along the edge of that second table there are six hundred and thirty-three marks in chalk.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing in this district that holds it. Those four documents are not in this district and never were. Those three lines can be read in four places in this district and in no fifth. Four conditions stand in this district and none of them has an end set on it in writing. This basin has made fourteen things and nothing besides them. Five of those fourteen were never got in this basin at all, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book could not take one before the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning, and that page stands at sixty-four lines and took no line on it this morning at all. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Four hundred and four mornings is how long that second table has stood at the foot of that bank, and three hundred and fifty-four mornings is how long the near rail has stood on the near side of it, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-four mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventy-third morning. That is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and twenty-ninth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and twenty-eight of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and fifteenth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and thirtieth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and sixty-third time running.

There is nothing at the back of that lot book and nothing has ever been put there, and it lies open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page beside that has one name in it, under a heading that still says whoever. On that form there is a second space, it is empty, and nobody has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down this morning at all. That record has stood two hundred and eighty-nine days. About nine people are standing on that ground, and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and nineteenth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Neither of these has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody in here has ever gone up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and not one of it is left over. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district and not one of them was heard read in it.

---


He came through the gap with the cart at the hour the cart comes through, and he did not stop, and he has never once stopped in that yard.

What he wanted off that morning was to get the whole of it up the slope in one run.

The boards that came down that slope the day before had been stacked at the near end of those eleven feet instead of going up, because there had not been room on the cart for all of them and because the wind had come up at one. So there was a load lying on the ground and a load behind him in the gap, and one cart, and a morning, and he had worked out the day before that between him and the light on those boards there was room for the whole of it once and not twice.


---


At ten to eight the boy was at those boards with the light still lying along that stack in front of him.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "And that is all I am going to say at that hour this week, and I have not stopped because I have settled anything. I have stopped because I am tired of the shape of it."

The cart came on past him and the near wheel went over the place where the boards had been lying and where the ground under them has not come back.

He did not put a word out loud into that yard about any part of that.


---


He roped the load up to about the height of the axle and stood at the off side of it with his hip against the shaft while he took the slack out of the rope, and then he got up on the footboard and got the off wheel off that soft ground by the only route there is, which is the long way round the near end of the boards where it is still hard.

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale set up at her own pan about four feet off the near end of the second table with her basket on the ground at her feet.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices out of that yard carried it on after him, out of step with one another.

The wheel was at its speed all morning and the turner was at the near end of that frame.


---


"You take that up at that load you are going to come back down that turn on the near wheel every time," said the man of about thirty-four, who had come along his own boards with the cloth over his arm.

He did not turn his head. He put his right thumb up off the rail, which is the whole of what he has ever given anybody in that yard in the way of an answer, and the man of about thirty-four looked at the thumb and went on along his own boards.

"He has not asked you a question and you have not given him a word," said the man of about thirty-four, to nobody in particular, "and both of those are how we do things here."


---


He went up it.

A roped load to the height of the axle on two wheels comes up a slope in a straight line or not at all, and the whole of the business of it is deciding before the cart is moving where it is going to stop, because there is nothing on that slope to stop against.

The near wheel found the soft place at the second bend, the same soft place the long way round was for, and dropped about an inch into it and stopped dead and took the whole load over with it.

He got his shoulder into the shafts and walked it four times and got it out, and lost about a minute and a half doing it, and there was about a hundred and twenty yards of that slope in front of him after that.


---


At the top he got the boards down off the cart the way he always gets them down, which is one at a time on the near end and the rest of them riding on the two far wheels until the last one comes off, and about four people at the top of that bank were standing well back out of it because that is where you stand.

Then he stood at the top with the cart and looked down at that yard for the length of time it takes the eye to go over a thing once.

The lot book was open along those boards below him. He could read the heading on the column from up there, because a man who cannot read a heading is a different sort of man and he is not one. It still said whoever. There was one name under it. The second space on that form was empty.

He looked at it for about as long as it takes a man to look at a thing he has looked at before.

He has come through that gap with that cart since he was a boy in it, and he has not been on anything, and he has never wanted anything out loud, and nobody had ever come to that top of that bank and put anything into his hand, and nobody at the foot of it had ever thought to ask him for anything either, and both of those are the way that yard has been with him the whole time he has been coming through it.


---


He went back down the near wheel at the second bend and it went through the same soft place and this time he had it right, because he had watched it go in that morning and a man who has watched a wheel go into a place knows where the place is.

That is the whole of what that morning gave him and he got it out of it.


---


At about four the hand went up on that low wall and took all four figures off it from the top down and laid itself flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Eight hundred and fifteen mornings, the four of you."

Then the flat answer.

"Two hundred and sixty-three mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had you twice today and got you both times, and a man went up that bank this afternoon with twice the load he goes up with and came down that bend on the near wheel and did not put a foot down, and I have not asked him how and he has not told me and I am not going to say one word about it."

That was the two hundred and sixty-third time running, and nobody in this district has ever asked him a thing about any part of it.


---


At about half past four the light came off those boards, four of the nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came down off that cart face down and went up the bank.

The whole of that day's boards went up in one run, and there was not a second run, and there was about nine feet of those boards left on that cart at the end of it and he took those back down with him through the gap.

He did not stop on the way through, and he did not stop at the top of that bank either, and there was nobody standing at the top of that bank to give him anything.

The length of belting is still lying flat on those boards at the near end of that frame and the straight edge is still lying on the top rail of it, and the nail is still in the wall of that wheelhouse with nothing on it, and none of that moved on this morning.


---


That page was still at sixty-four lines when the covers came over it and it took no line on it. That lane stood at thirty pages for the whole of that morning and not one page came down it. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page are unread in this district and always have been. The fifth of those five is five and unpaid. That count of seven months is on nothing whatever in this district. That sheet at that gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and it carries it in the morning as well.

That wheel was at its speed all morning and the turner was at that frame, and nobody in that yard put a word to him about anything.
