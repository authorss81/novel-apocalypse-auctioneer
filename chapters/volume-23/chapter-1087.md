# Chapter 1087: The Three Hundred And Seventy-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And One Thing About His Table

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and that sheet has never once been the subject of a morning here. That figure is eight hundred and twenty-six days old.

Nine hundred and eighty-five marks have been cut into that board. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and one days without a wagon off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and fifteen days. Nine hundred and seventy-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, was not run, and is not a lot and not open this morning. That figure on the second line of that book is six hundred and ninety days in front of this yard. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and ninety-five days. Seven hundred and sixty-five days have gone since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and four days past a printing it did not make.

That ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and sixty-eight days. Along the edge of that second table there are six hundred and thirty marks in chalk, and six hundred and forty-four marks have been cut off that board.

A clerk in this district has counted seven months and it sits on nothing at all here. Those four documents are not in this district. Those three lines are readable in four places in this district and not in a fifth. There are four conditions in this district and no end is set on any of them. Fourteen is what this basin has made. Five of those fourteen were never got here, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and that page could not be written on before the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on it before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing at the back of that book was said out loud in that yard this morning and that page stands at sixty-four lines and took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and one mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and seventy-one mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-one mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventieth morning, and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and twenty-sixth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and twenty-five of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twelfth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and twenty-seventh morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and sixtieth time running.

Nothing has ever been entered in that lot book and it lies open along those boards. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and one name stands in the column on the page after that, under a heading that still says whoever. There is a second space on that form and it is empty and nobody has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and eighty-six days. It has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are standing on that ground.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and sixteenth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Nobody here has ever been given either of these and nobody in this district has ever gone up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll of ninety-eight has been paid once and there is no paying it twice. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district and nobody here heard them read.


---


He had the cloth on that top and the straight edge in his coat and a wedge in his coat and a strip of bale tie in the rib at the near end, and that is the whole of what he has on a table.

What he wanted off that morning was to see it with nothing on it.


---


He came in past the boards at ten to eight on his way to that table.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, with the light still lying along that stack. "And there is nothing behind it and you have not asked me what I settled and I have not told you and I am not going to tell you this morning either."

"Nobody is going to make you."

"Nobody makes anybody do anything in this yard," said the boy. "That is what I have worked out and it has taken me a long time to work out."

He put the pencil behind his ear and went off up the slope.

The turner was at the near end of that frame with his hands in his pockets and the wheel was at its speed, and nobody in that yard put a word to him at any point of it.


---


He got to that second table at about half past nine and worked the far end of it first, which is the way he has always started, because the far end of that table is the end that has never given him anything to do.

At about ten past twelve the woman who keeps a scale set up at her own pan about four feet off the near end of his own table with her basket on the ground at her feet, and she was four feet off that near end for the rest of that day.

He did not pick the mallet up. There is a mallet in this yard and it carries about nine feet, and it has been within nine feet of his own near end every morning for a run of weeks, and he has not used it and is not going to.


---


At about two he stood in the middle of that ground with his own hands behind his back and said a thing out loud.

He said that he had been at that table for the best part of his working life and that he had never once seen it with nothing on it.

He said it in the ordinary voice, at the speed a person uses for a thing they mean, and it went out across that ground and up the face of that bank.

About nineteen people were close enough to take every word of it.

Nothing came back to him out of that yard. Nobody thanked him. Nobody asked him a second question about it and nobody asked him the first one either, and about four of the nineteen went on doing what they were doing, and the rest of them were a long way off. He stood there for about as long as a man stands when he has said something out loud in a yard and nothing has come back, and then he went and put his hand on his own cloth where it was lying and carried on.


---


At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and did not come back.

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and took all four figures off it from the top down and the palm went flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Eight hundred and twelve mornings, the four of you."

Then the flat answer.

"Two hundred and sixty mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had you twice today and got you both times, and a man stood in the middle of that ground at two o'clock this afternoon and said a thing out loud about his own table and there has been nothing come back to him out of that yard yet and it is half past four and there is not going to be."

That was the two hundred and sixtieth time running, and there is nobody in this district who has ever asked him a word about it.


---


At about half past three he took the cloth off that top and folded it into his coat, and then he took the straight edge out of his coat and put that in his coat, and then he took the wedge out, and then he took the strip of bale tie out of that rib at the near end and put that in his coat too, and the strip came out of that rib about as easy as it went in.

That table was bare.

He stood at the near end of it and looked at it and then he went round it and looked at it from the far end, and then he stood at the middle of it and looked along its whole length, which is fifteen years of looking along one thing and the only way he knows to look at anything.

He could not remember it.

That was not a feeling and it was not a sadness and it was not one of those things a man says in a yard to be interesting. It was that he had no picture of that top at all, from any hour, from either side, with nothing on it or with everything on it, and that a man who cannot picture his own table has to go and stand and look at it, and that he had been looking at it for fifteen years without once having to.


---


What he found, when he stood there, was that the part of that table he could picture was the near end.

Not the far end and not the middle. The near end, with the ground gone under it and the rib lifting and a strip of his own in it that would not hold.

He had spent a fortnight with his face on that two feet of wood and had not once had a picture of the other twelve.

He put his hand flat on that bare top in the middle and left it there for a while, and then he took his tools out of his coat one at a time and put them back where they go, which is the cloth flat on the boards where the frame is and the straight edge along the far edge and the wedge in the coat, and he put the strip of bale tie back into that rib at the near end because the wind gets under a strip and a strip keeps the rain out of a rib for a while.

That is not the same as it holding.


---


At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder.

"The three hundred and seventy-first morning," she said. "One man said one thing out loud about his own table at about two and nothing came back to him out of this yard, and at about half past three he took every tool off that top and looked at it with nothing on it for the first time, and nothing goes at the back of this book for any of it."

Then she wrote the three hundred and seventy-first morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came off that cart face down and went up the bank.


---


He went up that slope behind the last board with his own tools back where they go and that top bare behind him, and he did not say one word out loud about it to anybody on that slope.


---

That page stood at sixty-four lines at the end of that morning and it took no line on it. That lane stood at thirty pages all morning and brought nothing down it. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have never been read out by anybody in this district. That fifth of the five is five and unpaid. That count of seven months has been entered and nothing whatever here sits on it. That sheet at that gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and it will carry it in the morning.

That wheel was at its speed all morning and the turner was at that frame, and nobody in that yard asked him anything about any of it.
