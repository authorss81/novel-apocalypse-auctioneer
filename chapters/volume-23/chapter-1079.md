# Chapter 1079: The Three Hundred And Sixty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Four Feet Of Sun

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and no person in this basin has ever taken it off that sheet. It is eight hundred and eighteen days old.

That board carries nine hundred and seventy-seven marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and ninety-three days without a wagon off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and seven days. nine hundred and sixty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days of that bid came and went and it was not run, so it is not a lot and not open this morning. The figure on the second line of that book is six hundred and eighty-two days ahead of this yard. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and eighty-seven days. It is seven hundred and fifty-seven days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is six hundred and ninety-six days past a printing it did not make.

That ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and sixty days. Six hundred and thirty-six marks have been cut off that board. Along the edge of that second table stand six hundred and twenty-two marks in chalk.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk here and it stands on nothing whatever in this district. Four documents have never come into this district at all. Four places in this district will let those three lines be read and there is no fifth. Four conditions stand in this district without an end on any of them. This basin has made fourteen things and nothing else. Five of those fourteen were never got here, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and there was no page at the back of that lot book that would take one until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that book and that page took no line on it, and it stands at sixty-four. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and ninety-three mornings and that near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and forty-three mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-three mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and sixty-second morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and eighteenth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and seventeen of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and fourth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and nineteenth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and fifty-second time running.

Nothing has ever been entered in that lot book and it lies open along those boards with five lines ruled across one of its pages. The column on the page after that has its one name in it under a heading which still says whoever. That form has a second space and it stands empty, and nobody in this district has ever been asked about it. That lane is at thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and seventy-eight days. It has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are on that ground.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and eighth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Neither of these has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and there is nothing left of it. Three of the first thirty-third lines on that back page went through this district and nobody in this district heard one of them read.


---



What he wanted off that morning was for the near end of his own table to stop lifting.

It has been lifting since before the middle of the winter. He put a fresh strip off the bale tie into it a run of mornings back, because a mallet in that yard carries about nine feet and a woman came down that bank and set up four feet off his near end and stayed all afternoon, and he got the mallet into his hand in forty seconds and did not use it.

That strip has been in there a fortnight and it came out of that rib this morning on its own, which he had expected and had not wanted.



---



At ten to eight the boy was at those boards.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and there is nothing behind it, and I have not said that out loud at this hour since the morning I told you I would stop, and that was two mornings ago, and I have stopped, and I am not going to start again on the same words."

"Then what are you going to say at this hour."

"Nothing yet," said the boy. "I have not got to the bottom of what I am going to say at this hour and I would rather say nothing at this hour than say nothing at this hour."



---



He got to his own table at about half past nine and had that rib open by ten, and the boards under the near end of that table had gone down about the width of a thumb.

That is the third of them in that yard. He knew about the near end of the first table because everybody has known about that for a run of mornings, and he had seen what the man of about thirty-two did to his own corner with a wedge and strips, and he had watched the corner stay square five mornings running and said nothing about it to anybody.

So he did not need to be told that his own rib was not the rib.

He got his own fingers under the near end of that table and pushed up, and the frame came about the thickness of a coin and stayed there while he held it, and when he let it go it went down and the rib lifted again about a finger's width.



---



He put his own fingers under it and looked at the ground along the whole length of that table, which is fifteen years of looking at one thing and the only way he knows to look at it.

Nothing had gone under the middle of it. Nothing had gone under the far end of it.

It had gone under the near end of it, and about the same as it had gone under the near end of the first table, and in the same ground.

He took his hand out and stood there and did not say one word about it to anybody, and nobody was near enough to hear if he had.



---



At about eleven the woman of about fifty-two came down that bank with nothing in her hands at all.

Not the frame and not the bag. That frame has gone up that slope in her arm on more mornings than she has counted, and this morning she came down with neither of them and stood in about four feet of sun that came over the top of that bank and lay on the boards and on the top of that gap.

She stood there about an hour.

Nobody in that yard said good morning to her. Nobody in that yard said one word to her. The man of about thirty-four went past her and looked at his own boards. The boy came down the slope at about ten past twelve and went round her. The turner was at that frame and he did not turn his head.

Her frame was standing on its edge against the near end of those boards where the wind cannot get under it, and it had been left there an hour. The second space on her form was empty, and it was empty for the whole of that morning, and nobody in that yard said a word to her about either of those two things.

He was the one who went by her, and he stopped about four feet off and turned back.

"You have not said good morning to her."

"I have not said good morning to her in three weeks and she has not said good morning to me in three weeks," said the man of about thirty-four. "If I say it this morning I have said it for the wrong reason and she will know that in about a second, and then neither of us will say it again for a month."

"Then you will not say it."

"I will not say it," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have not said a word to her in three weeks and I have not gone up that bank to find out whether she is all right, and I have told you all of that and I am going back to my own boards now."



---



At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of his own table with a bar on it, and she was four feet off his near end for the rest of that day.

He worked about two feet away from that woman for the rest of that day and he did not ask her for anything at any point of it, and there was nothing in that yard she could have told him that would have got that rib down.



---



At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air, and about four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another.

At about four the hand went up on that wall and got all four off it from the top down, and then the palm went flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Eight hundred and four mornings, all four of you."

Then the answer he gives that stone, and it is a different set of words every morning.

"Two hundred and fifty-two mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got you twice today and got you both times, and I have not put one word into that wall about the fact that a man in that yard found the same thing under his own end this morning that another man found under his, and I have not put one word into that wall about the fact that I know which of us said the first of it out loud, and I have not asked him and he has not asked me."

That was the two hundred and fifty-second time running, and about nineteen people in that yard heard it, and nobody has ever asked him about any part of it.



---



He put a strip of his own in that rib at about half past three and it did not hold either, and he left it in there anyway because the wind gets under a strip and a strip keeps the rain out of a rib for a while.

At ten past four the clerk read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder and put the three hundred and sixty-third morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight, and he said it with the other four, and he went up the bank with them.



---

That page stood at sixty-four lines at the end of that morning and no line went onto it. That lane stood at thirty pages all morning and brought nothing down it. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three are not read out by anybody in this district. That fifth of the five is five and unpaid it stays. Seven months has been counted and nothing whatever in this district holds it. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet, and it will still be on it in the morning.
