# Chapter 1069: The Three Hundred And Fifty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Nobody At That Wall

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and nobody in this basin has ever done anything about it. It is eight hundred and eight days old.

That board carries nine hundred and sixty-seven marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and eighty-three days and has not had a wagon off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for nine hundred and ninety-seven days. Nine hundred and fifty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was not run, so it is not a lot and it is not open. This yard is six hundred and seventy-two days behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and seventy-seven days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and forty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and eighty-six days past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off.

That ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and fifty days ago. Six hundred and twenty-six marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and twelve marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk in this district and it is on nothing here. Four documents have never come to this district. There are four places here where those three lines can be read and not a fifth place anywhere. Four conditions here and not one of them has an end on it. Fourteen is the whole of what has been made in this district. Five were never got and the fifth of that five is unpaid in this district.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book was not a place one could go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, and nothing on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the page at the back of that book, and that page is at sixty-three lines and took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and eighty-three mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and thirty-three mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifty-three mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and fifty-second morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and eighth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and seven of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and ninety-fourth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and ninth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and forty-second time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards and nothing is entered in it, five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and no one has ever been asked to fill it. Thirty pages have come down that lane, every one of them in its own carrier's hand, and it brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and sixty-eight days. It has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about nine people standing on it.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and ninety-eighth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Nobody in this basin has ever been given either of these and nobody has ever gone up to look at either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight and it has been paid and it is spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page went through this district and nobody here heard them read.

---

He was at that low wall before the light came over the top of that bank, the way he had been every morning of that run.

At that distance the four figures on that other wall were a grey shape and not four figures, and they had not been four figures to him for a long time and he had not told anybody that and was not going to.

He got his back against the stone of that wall and put his hands in his pockets and waited.

---

At about a quarter to eight a hand went up on that wall four hundred yards off, and stayed up, and came down, and went up again, and came down.

Then a palm went flat on the stone under the lowest of them and stayed there, and the man of fifty-six said his four words to it, and not one word of that came four hundred yards.

At about half past eight the boy came along the bottom of that slope and stopped next to him with his hands in his pockets and did not ask him one thing.

"There are nine spaces in those boards and I have got nine four mornings running."

"That is a good thing to have got four times."

"It is the only thing in this district that comes out the same twice," said the boy. "That is not the same as being right. I have been at those boards two years and about three weeks and nobody has ever told me what any of it is for, and I have a figure of my own that nobody has ever read and nobody is ever going to, and I am telling you that at half past eight in the morning because you are the only man in this basin who has never asked me a single question about anything."

Not one person in this basin has put a question to that man, that day or any other, and the boy standing beside him at half past eight had not put one either.

---

At about eleven he walked the four hundred yards.

That other wall was empty. It had been empty since the man of fifty-six went off it at about a quarter to eight and it would be empty again until about four, and he had known that and had come on the strength of it.

He got his hand up on that stone about four feet off the lowest of the figures and he read all four of them.

It took him the time it takes to get a cup of water drunk. He read them in the order they come and then he read them again from the bottom up, and both times he got four, and both times the four were not the same four they ought to be.

There is one of those four that is out and he knows which one it is.

That is as far as he has got and it is further than he was on the day he walked out here to find out whether his own eyes had started lying to him. They have not. They have read four figures off a wall four hundred yards off, from a mark cut by somebody else, twice, and got them.

He took his hand off that stone and put it in his pocket and stood about four feet off that wall and did not say one word to anybody, because there was nobody there, and because the man who comes to that wall twice a morning is not a man you hand a thing like that to at eleven in the morning.

Then he walked the four hundred yards back and was at his own wall before the light came off the top of that bank.

At about half past twelve a shape came down that bank and stopped near the end of that second table and stayed there all afternoon, which is what that shape does.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it came out over that basin and thinned and went away.

He heard all of that. He had heard all of that on a great many mornings and it was the only thing anybody says in this district at that hour that is any use to anybody, and he had never once been able to make out a figure in it.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall again and got all four off it and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Seven hundred and ninety-four mornings, all four of you."

Then the flat answer, which is the thing he gives that stone at that hour and has given it since before the middle of the winter.

"Two hundred and forty-two mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got all four of you off you and got all four, and I have been reading you twice a morning since before the middle of the winter, and I have never once walked off you and left you standing there for somebody else, and I am not going to start, and that is the whole of what I have to say to you this afternoon."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and forty-second time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

---

The man at that low wall could not hear one word of that and could tell from the shape of that man's mouth that he had said all of it.

He put his palm flat on the stone of his own wall in the dark after that and tried to feel the grain of it, and he could not tell what he was feeling.

He could have told what he was touching before the frost, and he could not tell it now.

---

That page stood at sixty-three lines that morning and it took no line on it. Thirty pages stand on that lane and nothing came down it this morning. Three lines out of the first thirty-three on that back page are still unread in this district and have always been. That five is five and the fifth of it is unpaid. Seven months has been counted and there is nothing whatever in this district for that count to sit on. That post at that gate carries four hundred and eleven and it will still be carrying it in the morning.
