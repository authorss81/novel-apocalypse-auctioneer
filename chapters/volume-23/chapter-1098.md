# Chapter 1098: The Three Hundred And Eighty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Four Figures Off That Wall

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it has been left on that sheet for this basin to get the use of. It is eight hundred and thirty-seven days old.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety-six marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and twelve days with no wagon off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and twenty-six days. Nine hundred and eighty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run and it is not a lot and not open. Seven hundred and one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and six days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and seventy-six days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and fifteen days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-nine days back. Six hundred and fifty-five marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and forty-one marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing in this district that it sits on. Those four documents have never been in this district at any point. Those three lines can be read in four places here and there is no fifth place in it with any of them. Four conditions have no end upon them here. Fourteen is what this basin has made and it has made nothing else. Five of those fourteen things never came in this basin at all and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book did not take a line for a reason until the two hundred and eighth morning came along, so that nothing written on it earlier than that was ever a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that page and it did not take a line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and twelfth mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and sixty-second mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and eighty-second mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood there for the three hundred and eighty-first morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and thirty-seventh night of his run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and thirty-six of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twenty-third of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and thirty-eighth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the two hundred and seventy-first time running for what he says to that stone.

Nothing is at the back of that lot book and it lies open along those boards with nothing in it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after that one holds one name under a heading which still says whoever. The second space on that form stands empty and nobody has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane are thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down here this morning. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-seven days. About nine people were stood on that ground this morning and it has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-seventh afternoon running and one word was the whole of it. Nobody in this district has ever been handed either of them, and nobody here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and none of it is left. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and no voice in it has ever read one of them out.

---

He had his hand up on that wall at about a quarter to eight and got all four of the figures off it, and he had it up again at about four and got all four of them off it.

What he wanted off that morning was the same four both times.

---

He reads them off the top down. That is not a preference, it is the only order in which the four of them are the right distance apart for a man who has one hand on the wall and is standing on the ground, and he has read them off the top down for so long that the order is in his arm and not in his head.

The top one is the one that costs him.

That figure is the oldest paint on that wall and the sun has been on it since before he came to this basin, and what is left of it is thinner than what is left of the other three, and if a man reads it with the sun on it he will read it wrong and will not know that he has read it wrong.

---

He had his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one by about a quarter to eight, the way he does it, and he read the top one, and he read the second, and he read the third, and he read the fourth, and he took his hand off the wall.

Then he stood back about two feet and looked at that wall with his head on one side and did not say anything and did not go to anybody about it.

He has been doing that since before the middle of the winter and he was doing it this morning.

---

The man of about thirty-four came along that wall at about half past eight on his way to his own boards and stopped at the other end of it and stood there for a minute with the cloth over his arm.

"That stone is the only thing on this ground that is still warm this morning," he said out loud. "I have had my hand on it going past and it is like the handle of a pail that has been in the sun. Forty years I have been coming past that wall and it has never once been cold in the morning."

Nobody said one word to the man of fifty-six about any part of that, and he did not answer it, and after a while the man of about thirty-four went on down his own boards with his cloth still over his arm.

That is the way that wall is used. A man says a thing at it and the wall takes it and the wall keeps it, and about four people at the top of those eleven feet have said since that they have not said anything about that either.

About half past eight he went along that wall to the gate end and came back along it and stopped at the same place again, and then he went and got a cloth out of his coat and wiped the stone under the lowest figure and wiped it clean, and that took him about two minutes and made no difference to anything at all.

---

At about ten to eight the boy was at those boards with the light lying along the top of that stack.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike.

Nobody in that yard said a word to the man at that wall about any part of that, and he did not look round, and he went along that wall about six feet and stood at the other end of it and looked at the four figures from there, and then he went back to where he had been.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table and let the bar want the wind until it stopped wanting it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air, and four voices in that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another.

At about two he was at the near end of that first table with his cloth in his hand and he did not put a word out loud into that yard at any point of it, and nobody put a question to him.

---

At about eleven the man of about thirty-two came past with a length of batten under his arm and stopped at that wall and looked along it.

"You are going to be up there again at four."

He said it to the yard and not to the man at that wall, and he said it out loud because he says things out loud.

Nobody took it up and nobody put a question to anybody about it, and the man of fifty-six went on with the fourth figure of the morning and then had his hand off that wall until the afternoon.

---

At about four he put his hand up on that wall again.

"The four of you, and I will be here."

After that one came the second one, the one that says how long it has been going.

"Two hundred and seventy-one mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had the two of you twice today and got them both times, and there is not one person in this district who has ever come to this wall to look at anything, and I have not asked for one and I am not going to, and this stone has my whole hand on it."

That was the two hundred and seventy-first time running, and nobody in this district has ever asked him a thing about it.

---

The clerk came down those boards at about four with the pen in the holder and read that morning out loud, and then she wrote the three hundred and eighty-second morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four of that nineteen said the goodnight and the day's boards went up that bank off the cart.

He stayed at that wall until the light had gone off it and then he put his hand off the stone, and the stone was cold, and he has been read by nobody and he goes on reading it.

---

That back page has sixty-five lines on it and took no line this morning. That lane is still at thirty pages and it brought nothing down it today. Nobody here has ever heard those three read. The fifth of that five stands at five and unpaid. That count of seven months is on nothing whatever. The sheet at that gatepost will carry that same four hundred and eleven into the morning.

He got the same four off that wall twice this morning and he was read by nobody while he did it.
