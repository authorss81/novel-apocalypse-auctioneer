# Chapter 1076: The Three Hundred And Sixtieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Back Of His Heel

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it is on that sheet and nowhere else in this basin. It is eight hundred and fifteen days old.

Nine hundred and seventy-four marks have been cut into that board. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and ninety days without a wagon off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and four days. nine hundred and sixty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

The bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, was not run, and stands this morning as a bid that is not a lot and not open. This yard stands six hundred and seventy-nine days behind the figure on the second line of that book. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and eighty-four days. seven hundred and fifty-four days have gone by since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is six hundred and ninety-three days past a printing it did not make.

That ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and fifty-seven days. Six hundred and thirty-three marks have been cut off that board, and six hundred and nineteen marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

There are seven months counted by a clerk in this district and nothing whatever here to sit on them. Four documents this district would want are not in it. In four places here those three lines can be read and there is no fifth place here. Four conditions, and not one of them has an end. Fourteen things have been made here and they are the whole of what has been made here. There are five things this district has not got, and the fifth of them is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and nothing at the back of that lot book could be a line for a reason until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing that goes at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning, and that page took no line and stands at sixty-three. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and ninety mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and forty mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and fifty-ninth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and fifteenth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and fourteen of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and first of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and sixteenth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the the two hundred and forty-ninth time running.

That lot book is open on those boards with nothing entered in it and five lines ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has been asked to fill that one. That lane brought nothing down this morning and it has thirty pages behind it in their own carriers' hands. two hundred and seventy-five That ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and there are about nine people on it.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and fifth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of these and nobody in this district has ever walked either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and it is spent. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district and nobody here heard them read.


---



He wanted that table done and square by about one, and every morning for a run of weeks it has taken him until about half past two and then wanted doing again.

So this morning he did it the other way round. He took his cloth off it first, before he started, and put it on his own knee.



---



At ten to eight the boy was at those boards and had the pencil out before he had it half way.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and there is nothing behind it, and it is the last morning this week I am going to stand here and say nothing, and that is not because anything has changed."

"What has changed."

"Nothing has changed," said the boy, "and I have got to the end of saying it every morning and I would rather stop while it is still nothing than keep going and find out."

He did the spaces and got nine and put the pencil away.



---



He laid his own table up at about nine and squared it with the back of his heel the way he has squared it every morning for years, and it went square and stayed square, and he got the heel on the second leg as well because one heel is not enough for a table this size.

Then he went along the top of it with the cloth, from the near end to the far, and took about twenty minutes over it and did it properly rather than the way you do it when you know you are coming back.

It came up white enough. He put his palm flat on it and it was cold and dry and it would hold for a day.

That is four days now that he has not had to come back to it, and it is four days of the whole of that morning being his, and he has not told anybody that and is not going to.



---



At about ten the man of about thirty-two came past on his way to his own boards and looked at the top of that table and then at the near corner.

"Square."

"It is."

"Still."

"It is," said the man of about thirty-four.

The man of about thirty-two went along about four paces and then turned round.

"You are welcome to come and look at mine."

"I know what you did to yours."

"Then you know it was the ground and not your boards," said the man of about thirty-two. "Everybody in this yard has been putting wedge under that corner for three winters and it is nothing to do with the wedge. Come and look at it."

"I will look at it after one," said the man of about thirty-four.

"You will not," said the man of about thirty-two, "and that is all right. I have told two people and you are the third and you will not be the last."



---



He had it done at about twenty past twelve and he had half the day and he did not want it.

He went along his own boards to the far end where that piece of pallet was lying flat where he can see it from where he stands, and he has not laid any of it anywhere.

It is a foot and about four inches long, with a square cut on one end of it and the other end broken off, and it is sound all the way through, and it is the shape of the thing a man reaches for when a corner wants holding.

He picked it up.



---



He carried it back along the length of his own table to the near end and held it against the corner in the air, about a finger's width off the wood, where it would sit if it were going to sit there.

It was the exact length of the play in that corner. It was square at the cut end. It would have gone in there perfectly.

And the wood on that end wants to go back to where it came from, and he said that out loud in this yard on his second morning at that table, and nobody has ever contradicted him, and it is as true this morning as it was then.

He took it off the corner and stood in the middle of his own table holding it.

Then he went to the far end and held it against that corner in the air.

Nothing there wanted it either. That end is sound ground and has been square for two winters and would go on being square with nothing under it at all.



---



So it was not for the near end and it was not for the far end, and he had carried it the length of his own table in his hand and found out both of those in about ninety seconds, and he had said nothing at all out loud while he did it.

He put it down flat on the boards at the far end in the same place it had been lying, with the square cut facing the way it had been facing, and it is still not laid in anywhere.



---



The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another.

At about four the hand went up on that wall and got all four off it and went flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Eight hundred and one mornings, all four of you."

And then the flat answer, which has never once been the same sentence twice.

"Two hundred and forty-nine mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got you twice today and got you both times, and there is a man in this yard carrying a foot of good pallet about with him and has carried it for a run of mornings, and he has said four words out loud in this yard and every one of them was to that wood and not to anybody, and I am not going to say a fifth word about him."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and forty-ninth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.



---



At ten past four the clerk read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder and wrote the three hundred and sixtieth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

The light came off those boards at about half past four and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight that is said at the end of a day there.

He picked his cloth up off his knee and put it over his arm and stood for a minute looking at that top, which was square and white and would hold until tomorrow, and then he went up the bank.



---

That page was at sixty-three lines at the end of that day and it carried no new line on it. Nothing has come down that lane and it has stood at thirty pages. Three of the first thirty-third lines on that back page nobody here has read and nobody here ever has. That fifth of those five is still five and still unpaid. Seven months has been entered and there is nothing in this district for it to sit on. Four hundred and eleven will be on that sheet at that gatepost in the morning.
