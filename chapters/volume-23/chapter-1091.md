# Chapter 1091: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And His Barrow In The Cut

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it is still nailed up there in the same weather, and no one in this basin has ever done one thing with it. Eight hundred and thirty days is the age of it.

Nine hundred and eighty-nine marks have been cut into that board. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and five days without a wagon being taken off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and nineteen days. Nine hundred and eighty days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was never run, so it is not a lot and it is not open this morning. Six hundred and ninety-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and ninety-nine days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and sixty-nine days have gone past the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and eight days beyond a printing it never made.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and seventy-two days. Six hundred and forty-eight marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and thirty-four marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and nothing in this district holds it. Four documents have never come into this district at all and not one of the four is here. Those three lines are readable in four places in this district and there is no fifth place in it that has any of them. Four conditions stand in this district and no end has been set on one of them in writing. This basin has made fourteen things and has made nothing else. Five of those fourteen never arrived here and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and there was no page at the back of that lot book that would take one before the two hundred and eighth morning came, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

One reason went out of that yard this morning from about four hundred yards off, and one line went onto that page for it, and that line carries the morning on it and not one word of the reason. That page stood at sixty-four lines when the morning began.

That page is at sixty-five lines now and it does not move again.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and fifth mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and fifty-fifth mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-fifth mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventy-fourth morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and thirtieth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and twenty-nine of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and sixteenth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and thirty-first morning running with nobody having asked him anything. The flat answer went to that stone twice this morning and it was the two hundred and sixty-fourth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it in any part of it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page beside that holds one name under a heading which still says whoever. On that form there is a second space and it is empty and nobody has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down here this morning. That record has stood two hundred and ninety days. That ground has been nobody's but the public's since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are stood on it this morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twentieth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Neither of these two has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody here has ever gone up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight and it is paid and spent and there is none of it left over. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and nobody in this district has ever heard one of them read.

---

The loam comes up at the north end in slabs about the thickness of a shoe sole and it has to be cut, not scooped, and the cutting is the whole of that job.

What he wanted off that morning was to get the barrow to stand in the cut without his hands on it.

He has wanted that for two seasons. The cut runs about nine feet and it goes into the ground a hand's width and a half at the shallow end, and at the deep end it goes in as far as a man's forearm can reach and come back up with clay on it. A barrow standing in that cut with nobody holding it does not roll, does not slither, and can be loaded from either side while the loader keeps both feet where they are. A barrow standing on the lip of it has to be held, and a barrow held is a barrow that has to be told to.

The trouble is the stone.

---

He had it open again by six and a half and the standing water from the night was lying in the bottom of yesterday's cut in a long grey lens, and he waited for it to go the way he has waited every morning for as long as there has been a cut here.

About twenty minutes later he was in to the elbow and the ground under his knees was like a wet heel and would not come off the blade in pieces, only in strings.

"You are going to get that stone out," said the man of about thirty-four, who had come down to the north end on his own way and was looking down into the cut from above.

"I am not going to get that stone out."

"It is the size of a door."

"It is the size of a door and it is under the middle of it," said the man who digs loam, "and if I lift it I have to put it back, and putting it back is a worse job than leaving it, and I have done that twice and it went down worse than it was before."

The man of about thirty-four stood at the lip a while and then went off up the slope without saying anything else about it, and what he said about it was the only thing he said about it.

---

He dug along the lip for about a yard and a half and then stopped and stood in his own loose ground with his hands on the handle and looked north, at the low place where the water comes off the bank and crosses.

He had been working on that thinking for four days and he had got to the front of it and he could not get past the front of it, and it was a reason and not a figure, and it belonged to him and to the ground and to nobody else in that yard.

He put his head up and shouted it out across about four hundred yards of open ground to the south.

"The water comes off that bank at the north end and it crosses here and it will cross here every wet season there will ever be, and that cut is in this line because of where the water goes and not because of where anything is put, and I have said it and it is out of me now."

Nobody answered him.

About nine people were standing on that ground between him and the wall and not one of them turned round, and the four voices that were going somewhere else at that moment went on going somewhere else, and the rest of that yard carried on carrying itself.

He went back into the cut and worked, and he did not say it again, and he did not expect anybody to say it back to him.

---

At about ten to eight the boy was at those boards with the light still lying along the front of that stack.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "Nothing behind it."

The man who digs loam was four hundred yards off and did not hear him and would not have answered him if he had.

---

The trouble with the stone was not the stone itself. The stone was the width of a door and about two hands thick and it sat under the middle of the cut where the middle of the cut had always sat.

The trouble with the stone was that the wheel of the barrow comes down into the cut about four inches in from the near edge, and four inches in from the near edge at the middle of that cut is the top of the stone, and a wheel that comes down on the top of a stone comes down on a slope and does not stop there.

He tried it four times and got it four times.

---

The man of about thirty-two came out to him at about eleven and stood at the end of the cut and looked down the length of it and then at his own table behind him and then at the cut again.

"You are digging it to hold a barrow," he said, "and you are digging it in a line and not in a hole, and if you ever fill it in, fill it in from the deep end."

"Why from the deep end?"

"Because you will not get to the deep end once you have filled the shallow one, and you know that, and I have watched you not know things for two days."

Then he went back to his own boards and picked up his cloth.

---

He had about four hours before the light came off those eleven feet and he used all of them.

He took the loose out of the cut in barrow-loads and put it on the spoil at the far side where it would not go back in when the wind came off the bank. Then he went back down into the cut with a short-handled spade and cut the floor of it on the near side, four inches below the lip, from the shallow end to within a foot of the stone, and then cut it across above the stone and down the other side, and that last foot and a half he cut by hand with the blade turned over, because the stone would not let the spade lie flat.

The clay from that cut went on the lip in a low ridge about the thickness of his wrist.

He packed that ridge down with the back of the spade and then with the heel of his boot, and he packed it along the whole nine feet and finished at the deep end, because the man of about thirty-two had told him to, and because it holds water better that way round.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale set up at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table and let the bar want the wind until it stopped wanting it. At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another.

The wheel was at its speed and the turner was at the near end of that frame.

He heard none of that from where he was, and he was four hundred yards off, and he dug.

---

He tried the barrow into it at about two.

It stood in the cut by itself.

He took his hands off the handles and stepped back and it stood there, and it did not roll and it did not walk down toward the drain, and after about as long as he was willing to stand and look at it, he took the handles again and tipped it out and dug out what he had tipped into it.

Then he dug it clean again for about an hour and stood and looked at it once more with nothing in it at all, and it stood, and that was the whole of what that morning gave him.

---

At about four the hand went up on that low wall and took four figures off it from the top down and laid itself flat on the stone under the lowest one and lay there.

"The four of you, again, and again."

Then what he says to the stone after that.

"Two hundred and sixty-four mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had you twice and got you both times, and a man has shouted a reason out of that yard this morning from four hundred yards north of it and there is not one person in this district who knows what he said, and I have not asked him and I am not going to ask him, and that stone has got my whole hand on it and it is not going to ask him either."

That was the two hundred and sixty-fourth time running, and nobody in this district has ever asked him one thing about any part of it.

---

At ten past four the clerk came down the row of boards and read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder, and then she wrote the three hundred and seventy-fifth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and then she took one line for the reason that had gone out of the yard that morning, and the line carries the morning and not the reason.

Nobody thanked her for it. Nobody said one word to her about it before or after.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of that nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came down off the cart face down and went up that bank.

He was four hundred yards north of all of it and he did not see any of it go.

He put his spade in the cut and lined the near lip up with the heel of his boot, because a lip with a ridge on it is a lip that will be walked on by nine people by noon tomorrow, and then he put the barrow in it one last time, and it stood there while he got his coat off the rail behind him, and he left it standing there, and that was where the whole of that day was for him.

---

That page was at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it took no more line on it that day. That lane stood at thirty pages the whole morning and not one page came down it. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have still never been read out in this district. The fifth of that five is five and nobody in this district has ever paid it. Seven months has been counted and there is nothing in this district that holds it. The sheet at that gatepost is carrying the same four hundred and eleven again in the morning.

One reason went out loud from the north end of that yard this morning and one line went onto that page for it, and the line carries the morning and not the reason.

Nobody answered him and nobody said one word of thanks to him for saying it.
