# Chapter 1085: The Three Hundred And Sixty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And His Heel Off That Frame

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and no use of it has ever been made in this basin. Eight hundred and twenty-four days is the age of that figure.

Nine hundred and eighty-three marks have been cut into that board. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and ninety-nine days without a wagon coming off it. Nothing at that rail has been entered for one thousand and thirteen days. Nine hundred and seventy-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was not run and it is not a lot and it is not open this morning. That figure on the second line of that book is six hundred and eighty-eight days ahead of this yard. That rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and ninety-three days. Since the first day of the eighth month it has been seven hundred and sixty-three days. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and two days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and sixty-six days. Six hundred and forty-two marks have been cut off that board, and six hundred and twenty-eight marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing at all in this district for it to sit on. Four of those documents have never been in this district at all. In four places here those three lines are readable and no fifth place here has any of them. Four conditions stand in this district and not one of them has an end. This basin has made fourteen things. Five of those fourteen were never got here, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book was not there to take one before the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That page stands at sixty-four lines at the end of this morning and took no line on it, and nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that goes behind it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and ninety-nine mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and forty-nine mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-nine mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and sixty-eighth morning. It is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and twenty-fourth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and twenty-three of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and tenth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and twenty-fifth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and fifty-eighth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards and nothing is entered in it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and one name stands in the column on the page after that, under a heading that still says whoever. There is a second space on that form and it is empty and nobody has ever been asked about it. That lane is at thirty pages behind it and it brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and eighty-four days. That ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and about nine people are on it.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and fourteenth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Neither of these has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight and it is paid and spent. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three went through this district and nobody in it heard one of them read.


---


He had the stove in before the light and the frame banked by six and a half and it was at its speed by ten to eight.

What he wanted off that morning was to know whether that frame is straight.

He has run that wheel at one speed for thirty-one years and it has never once come up short, and it has never once run away either, and he has never in all that time asked himself the plain question, which is that a frame can be straight and a wheel can be true and you can have either one of them and not know which one you have got.


---


He got his straight edge out of the wheelhouse at about a quarter to nine and laid it along the top rail of that frame, and then he took it and laid it along the boards at the near end of that frame, and then he took it and laid it back along the top rail of that frame again.

Both of them said the same thing.

He stood there and looked at the place where the straight edge was lying and decided that one answer is not an answer and that he would put it down on that rail the same way twice more in the morning and get the same answer, and then he left it lying there because a man who carries a straight edge about in both hands cannot do the next thing on his list.


---


At ten to eight the boy was at those boards with the light still lying along that stack.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "And there is nothing behind it, and you have not asked me what I settled and I have not told you."

"I have not asked you what you settled."

"You never ask me anything at this hour," said the boy. "You have never once asked me anything at this hour in all the mornings I have said it, and I have got used to it, and I am not asking you for it."

He put the pencil behind his ear and went off up the slope.

At about one the man of about thirty-four came along with the cloth over his arm and stopped where he stops, and looked at the straight edge lying on the top rail of that frame.

"You have left that out."

"I have."

"It is going to be under a boot before the light goes."

"Then a boot goes over it," said the turner, "and I will not have moved a thing at the end of a morning to keep a boot off a piece of steel, and that length of belting has been lying on those boards three days now for the same reason."

He did not pick the straight edge up and he did not pick the belting up.

"I have picked neither of them up," he said out loud, "and I am not going to, and if you want to know why I do not know why and I have stopped working it out, because there is nobody in this yard who could tell me and I would have to make it up, and I do not make things up."

The man of about thirty-four looked at him for a second.

"You have never once in this yard said a thing I did not believe," he said, "and I have been listening for it for years, and I am going along my own boards now."

"That is your business and not mine."

"It is my business," said the man of about thirty-four, "and you have not got anything to do with it."

He went along his own boards.


---


At half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about four feet off the near end of the second table with her basket on the ground at her feet.

He was at that frame all morning and about four feet off the near end of that first table the man of thirty-eight had his cloth in his hand and did not put a word out loud into that yard at any point of it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and did not come back.

Nobody in that yard looked up at it.


---


After the middle of the day he put his heel on that frame.

The wheel came off.

He took the heel off and stood there with his hands at his sides, and then he went along those boards and did the next thing on the list he has had for that morning for as long as he can remember.

He did not put that heel back on that frame again that day.


---


At about two he got to the front of his sentence.

He has got to the front of that sentence most days of his run and he has never once got past the front of it, and he has never told anybody that he gets that far, and he stopped there then the way he stops there and went and did something else.


---


At about four the hand went up on that low wall and took all four off it from the top down and laid itself flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Eight hundred and ten mornings, the four of you."

Then the flat answer.

"Two hundred and fifty-eight mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had you twice today and got you both times, and there is a length of straight steel lying on the top rail of that frame at the other end of this ground where a man left it this morning on purpose, and it will still be lying there tonight, and I am not going to go and look at it and I am not going to say a word about it."

That was the two hundred and fifty-eighth time running, and there is nobody in this district who has ever asked him a thing about it.


---


At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder.

He did not hear a word of it. A voice across that yard comes in on him as a shape and not as words, and it has done as long as he can remember.

Then she wrote the three hundred and sixty-ninth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came off that cart face down and went up the bank.

He stood at that frame with his hands in his pockets until the last of them was up the slope.


---


He went up that slope behind the last board with the rag in his coat, and that straight edge is still lying on the top rail of that frame where he put it at a quarter to nine, and that length of belting is still lying flat on those boards with its damp end under it, and the nail is still in the wall of that wheelhouse with nothing on it.


---

That page stood at sixty-four lines at the end of that morning and it took no line on it. That lane was at thirty pages all morning and brought nothing down it. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have never been read out in this district by anybody. That fifth of the five is five and unpaid. Seven months has been counted and there is nothing whatever in this district that sits on it. That sheet at that gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and it will carry it in the morning.

That wheel came off that frame after the middle of that day and it did not go back up that day.

Nobody asked him anything about it, and nobody thanked him for anything, and nobody in that yard said one word out loud about it.
