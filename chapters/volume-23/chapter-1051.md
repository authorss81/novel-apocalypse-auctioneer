# Chapter 1051: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Length Of Belting

Nobody has ever come at that sheet with a tool or a thought, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. That figure has stood on its post for seven hundred and ninety days.

That board carries nine hundred and forty-nine marks cut into it. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-five days without a wagon coming off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and seventy-nine days. Nine hundred and forty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open. It was not run, it is not a lot, and it is not open. Six hundred and fifty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and fifty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and twenty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and sixty-eight days is how far past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and thirty-two days ago. Six hundred and eight marks have been cut off that board. Five hundred and ninety-four marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months a clerk has counted and there is nothing in this district for that count to sit on. Four documents are not owned here. Four places here can read those three lines and not a fifth. Four conditions are on the list here and not one of them has an end on it. Fourteen things have been made in this district and five were never got, and the fifth of that five is open this morning and has been open every morning of this run.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and there was no page at the back of that lot book for one to go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, and nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason because before that morning it had nowhere to hold one, and that reason is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing has gone out loud in that yard this morning that goes at the back of that book. That page is at sixty-two lines and it took no line this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-five mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and fifteen mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-five mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirty-fourth morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and seventy-sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninetieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighty-nine of the nights of it. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eightieth afternoon running and one word is the whole of that afternoon. And that is the one hundred and ninety-first morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer is given to that stone twice a morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-fourth time running.

On those boards there is that book lying open on its face with its spine to the wind and nothing entered in it. Five lines are ruled across the first of its pages. Down the next of those pages runs a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has been asked about it. Thirty pages have gone down that lane in the hands of the people who carried them, and not one of them came down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty days. Public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, that ground, and about nine people are on it this morning.

He has never been given either of them and has never gone up to look: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district without one person here hearing them read.
---

He had the stove in before the light came up over the top of that bank, and he had the frame banked by six and a half.

The light does not come into that yard all at once. It comes down the bank in a slow wedge and finds the boards first, and the boards are what he sets the day by, and he had the boards before he had the sun.

There was a length of belting on a nail in the wheelhouse that had been on that nail for as long as he could remember, and every morning for a run of mornings he had looked at that nail on his way past, and every morning he had decided that today he would take it down and fit it, and every morning he had got as far as deciding it.

This morning he took it down.

It was the length of belting for a frame of that size and nothing else. He knew it by the feel of it in the hand before he had it out of the light, the way a man knows a thing he has carried for a long time, and it came away from the nail with the dry sound of a thing that has not been moved in a while.

He put it in his coat and went out and looked at the frame.

The frame had frost on the underside of the arm where the night had got at it, and he got his hand under there and cleared it. The wheel came round on top of that and did not sound like anything.

That is the whole of what happens when you fit a new length of belting to a frame that has been running at one speed since before you were in this yard. It sounds the same. He knew that before he had the thing in his coat, and he had known it every morning of a run of mornings, and being right about it did not put it back on the nail.

At ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, the way he says it, and said out loud after it that there was no figure behind it this morning.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and there is nothing behind it."

The clerk had the book open against the rail and shut it again without writing anything in it.

"You have said that out loud to me before," she said.

"I say it out loud to the boards."

"That is the same ground."

"It is the same ground and it is not the same ears," said the boy, and went up the slope with the pencil in his pocket.

That was the first sound of the yard. He could set a wheel by it, and had done for thirty-one years, and could have done it in the dark, and had done it on mornings when there was a fog on that ground you could not see six feet of.

He had the length of belting out of his coat about ten and looked at the frame with it in his hand for about as long as a man looks at a thing he is going to decide about.

Then he put it back in his coat.

The man of about thirty-two came along his own boards about two with the cloth over his arm and stopped where he stops, four feet short of where he wanted to be.

"Your corner is out of square again."

"My corner has been out of square since the spring," said the man of about thirty-two.

"You squared it with your heel on the boards behind you."

"That was the other one."

"That was the other one," said the turner, "and it went out again by morning."

"Then that tells you what you are dealing with," said the man of about thirty-two. "The boards go. You square a thing onto boards that are going and you have squared nothing. I have had a wedge of my own out of the fire-split for a fortnight and I am not going to pretend it is holding my end."

He went along his own top with the cloth and did not say anything else about it, and about four feet along from where the turner stood there was a foot and a bit of pallet lying flat on top of another man's boards where it had been put down.

"You still have not laid that in anywhere," said the man of about thirty-two.

"No," said the man of about thirty-four, from about nine feet further along.

"Three winters of that corner."

"It has got the grain the wrong way round that end," said the man of about thirty-four. "That is what it is. You cannot pack a corner out of square when the wood wants to go back to where it came from."

"You have worked that out and you have not done anything about it."

"I have had it in my pocket since before the frost and I have put it on top of my boards where I can see it," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I do not know yet what it is for, and you are going to want me to know."

He went along his own boards with the cloth and neither of them said another word about it.

---

The turner was at the frame at about half past twelve when she came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of that second table and put a bar on her own pan.

He had noticed her coming down the bank for nine years and he had never once noticed her going up it, and he could not have told anybody which was the harder to watch.

She said nothing out loud that morning that he heard, and she worked about three feet off her own pan and let it want to follow the wind until it came still, and about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and was gone.

Four feet off the near end of the first table the man of thirty-eight had the cloth in his hand and had had it since half past nine and had not said one word out loud all day, and there was nothing in the way anybody stood that morning that wanted him to.

The turner got as far as the front of it.

There was a sentence in him that he had been carrying for a long time and he came up to the front of it about one o'clock, the way he came up to the front of it every morning, and he stopped there, and nobody in that yard knew he had come up to it, and he did not say it.

Not one person in that yard knows that he gets as far as the front of it. It is the only thing in his life that has never been asked for, and he has not decided whether that is a good arrangement and he is not going to.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it one after another in the order they come and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

The turner heard that at four hundred yards and could not make out one word of it, and had never been able to, and about four people at the foot of that bank heard the shape of a man talking to a stone and none of them went over.

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards with the book open and gave the whole of that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder.

He could not hear the words from the frame. He could hear that she was giving the whole of it, which was the way she worked, and he went on oiling a joint that did not want oiling because there was nothing else in the yard that wanted doing.

At about half past four the light came off the boards.

About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight the way it is said at the end of a day in that yard. The boards came off the cart face down and went up the bank with the wind behind them.

He banked the wheel and put his hand on the frame to stop it, which is what he does at the end of a day, and had done at the end of every day of that run, and the yard went quiet at the near end of that ground the way it does.

---

That page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines when the covers came over it, and it did not carry a sixty-third line. That lane is still at thirty pages and nothing came down it this morning. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page are still unread in this district. Five things this district has never got, and the fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months a clerk has counted and it is on nothing. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost and it is still on it, and nobody in this basin has done one thing about it.
