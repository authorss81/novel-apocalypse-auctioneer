# Chapter 1063: The Three Hundred And Forty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Six Feet In One Pull

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. It is eight hundred and two days old, and nobody in this basin has ever come at that post with anything at all.

That board carries nine hundred and sixty-one marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventy-seven days without a wagon off it. At that rail nothing has been entered for nine hundred and ninety-one days. Nine hundred and fifty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was never run, so it is not a lot and it is not open. This ground is six hundred and sixty-six days behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and seventy-one days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and forty-one days since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and eighty days past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and forty-four days. Six hundred and twenty marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and six marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk here and it is on nothing. Four documents this district does not own. Four places here can read those three lines and not a fifth one of them. Four conditions have no end on any of them here. Fourteen things have been made in this district. Five were never got and the fifth of that five is still unpaid.

Out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning there was a reason, and the page at the back of that lot book had nowhere to put a reason until the two hundred and eighth morning, so no line on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing that goes at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning. That page is at sixty-three lines and took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-seven mornings and that near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and twenty-seven mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-seven mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and forty-sixth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and second night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and one of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eighty-eighth of those mornings and got all four. That is the two hundred and third morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer went to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and thirty-sixth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them carries one name under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has been asked about it. That lane carries thirty pages in the carriers' own hands and has brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and sixty-two days. That ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and about nine people are on it.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and ninety-second afternoon running and gave it one word. Nobody here has ever been given either of these and nobody here has ever gone up to look: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page have gone through this place without one person in it hearing them read.

---

He took his place at about four feet off the near end of that first table at half past nine with the cloth in his hand, and he heard the boy say his count out loud at the boards while he was still walking up to it.

He had the whole of that morning in front of him and about two feet of table he was allowed to do at a time, and that had been the arrangement for a run of mornings, and he had got used to it in the way a man gets used to a thing that has no alternative in it.

The boards under the near end of that table have gone down about the width of two fingers. Put his weight on that end and the table rocks and the cloth leaves a wave on the boards instead of lying flat. The middle of that table is over ground that has not gone anywhere.

So he stood on the middle and worked forward.

---

At about eleven he tried something and it came off better than he had any right to expect.

He took a length of about six feet of that cloth, laid it along the boards with the far end of it a foot past the middle of that table and the near end of it up against the leg, and got his weight in the middle where the ground is, and pulled.

The whole six feet came off in one go. It came off flat and it left the boards dry and it did not waver once, and where the table lifted about the width of two fingers at the near end it did not put a bend in the cloth.

Six feet, in one pull, on a table with a hole under one end of it.

He put his hand flat on the boards where the cloth had been and looked at the length of it and then went and did the next six feet the same way, which took him the better part of an hour because he kept waiting for it not to work.

It worked twice more.

He had done about fourteen feet of that table by half past twelve in lengths a man would not have believed in, and the near two feet were still on that table with the cloth on them, and he could not get those two feet at all in one pull and had not tried.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of that second table and put a bar on her own pan, and let it want to follow the wind until it came still.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went up over that bank and was gone out of that yard.

The turner was at that frame the whole of that morning. He had come down off the slope at about six and a half and he went up it again at about half past four, and in between he was the only man in that yard who never once sat down.

From where the man of thirty-eight stood, that frame was about eight feet off him, and he could see all of it without turning his head, and he pulled his cloth on the turn of it, because that wheel had gone round at that speed since before he could remember and a man takes his time off a thing like that without ever putting it into words.

The man at that frame put his weight on the boards all that morning and did not once put it on the casting, and the man of thirty-eight was not asked about that and did not ask about it, and there was nothing in the way anybody stood that morning that wanted it.

---

At about two the man of about thirty-two and the man of about thirty-four were about four feet apart along those boards and talking about their own tables.

"Has my corner gone again?"

"Your corner has not gone," said the man of about thirty-four. "Your corner has not gone because nothing has moved it. That end of your table is sitting in a hole and your top is flat and the two of those are telling you different things."

"Then I have a table with a flat top on a frame in a hole, and a frame in a hole is not a table."

"You have a table on boards," said the man of about thirty-four, "and the boards are not yours, and I have been telling you that for a run of mornings and I am not going to be the one who says it differently today."

The man of about thirty-two went along his own top with the cloth and did not say anything for a while.

"Have you still not laid that pallet in anywhere?" he said.

"I have not laid it anywhere."

"You have had it in your coat for a run of mornings."

"I have had it in my coat for a run of mornings and I have had it on top of my own boards for longer than that, and it is the wrong shape for the corner you keep talking about and I worked that out about two days ago."

"Then what is the wrong shape for it."

"A foot and about four inches of it," said the man of about thirty-four, "and that corner wants a foot and about four inches of it on each of two sides, and I have got the one and there is not the other anywhere in this yard."

The man of thirty-eight went past them with his cloth and neither of them stopped him.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it in the order they come and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Seven hundred and eighty-eight mornings, all four of you."

Then what he calls his flat answer, which is the same thing at that hour every morning and has been since before the middle of the winter.

"Two hundred and thirty-six mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and there is a man in this yard working his own table six feet at a time this morning out of a corner he cannot square, and he has not once said one word about why it is done that way, and neither have I, and neither has anybody else, and that is the arrangement and I am not going to be the one to break it."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and thirty-sixth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

---

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder, before she had her hand near any part of it.

"The three hundred and forty-seventh morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that belongs at the back of that book, so it goes down in my own hand alone with an empty space beside it, and I have said it here before the pen was anywhere near it."

Then she wrote the three hundred and forty-seventh morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight. The boards came off the cart face down and went up the bank, and the wheel was shut down with a palm on the casting and stayed down until it had done.

---

He had about four feet of that table left when the light went off it, and the near two feet were still the near two feet, and he had got fourteen feet of it done in one pull apiece where a fortnight ago he was doing it in two feet at a time and getting nothing done at all.

He folded the cloth over his arm and went up that bank with the boards.

---

That page stood at sixty-three lines when that morning was done and it took no line on it. That lane stands at thirty pages and nothing came down it this morning. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page are unread in this district as they have always been. The fifth of that five is five and it stands unpaid. Seven months is counted in this district and it is on nothing whatever. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet at that gatepost and it will be there tomorrow.
