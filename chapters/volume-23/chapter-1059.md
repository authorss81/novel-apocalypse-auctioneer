# Chapter 1059: The Three Hundred And Forty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Short Lengths

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. It has been on that sheet for seven hundred and ninety-eight days and no one has been near it.

That board carries nine hundred and fifty-seven marks. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventy-three days without a wagon coming off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and eighty-seven days. Nine hundred and forty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and was not run and is not a lot and not open. This yard is six hundred and sixty-two days behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and sixty-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and thirty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and six hundred and seventy-six days past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and forty days ago. Six hundred and sixteen marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and two marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk here and there is no list of anything for that count. Four documents this district does not own. Those three lines can be read in four places in this district and never in a fifth. There are four conditions here and nobody in this district has ended one of them. Fourteen things have been made in this district, five were never got, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book had no place for one until the two hundred and eighth morning, and nothing written on it before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing that goes at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning, and that page stood at sixty-two lines and took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-three mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and twenty-three mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and forty-second morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-three mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-eighth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-seven of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eighty-fourth of those mornings and got all four of them. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-eighth afternoon running and gave that afternoon one word. That is the one hundred and ninety-ninth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and thirty-second time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it, five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and has never been asked of anybody. That lane carries thirty pages in the carriers' own hands and has delivered nothing this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-eight days. Since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning that ground has been public lot, and about nine people are on it.

Nobody here has been given either of those: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid, and spent, and settled. Three lines among the first thirty-three on that back page went through this district and nobody here heard them read.
---

He took his place at about four feet off the near end of that first table at half past nine with the cloth in his hand, which is where he has taken his place every morning of that run.

He came along his own top, and it moved.

Not much. About the width of a finger at the near end and nothing at all at the north end, and the cloth came off the boards in a wave instead of lying flat, and there is a way of reading that wave that a man does with his knee and his hip without ever looking down.

He came back and put his heel on the boards at the near end of that table and pressed, and there was nothing under that heel at all, and the boards went down about the width of two fingers and stopped.

The ground under that corner has gone.

He stood there with the cloth in his hand for a minute and a bit and then he went and got a bar of iron from where the bars are kept and laid it along the boards at the near end of that table and got his weight on the end of it, and the table came up onto it and stayed there while he got his weight off, and when he took the bar out it went down again the same way.

He could not lift the near end of that table with the cloth and his back. He could not get a hand under the frame to find out how far the ground had gone, and he could not lift the top off it alone, and he could not ask anybody to help him lift it, because he has not asked anybody for anything in a long time and does not do it, and because nobody in that yard has asked him anything either and that is not going to change today.

---

The man of about thirty-two had his own table up about four feet along and was going along the top of it with the cloth.

"My corner has gone out again."

"Your corner went square four mornings back," said the man of about thirty-four.

"It went square for about two days," said the man of about thirty-two. "And now there is a hollow under the boards at that end and I have put my hand in it and my hand goes in to the second knuckle."

"That is not the wood then."

"It is not the wood, and it is not the frame, and it is the ground, and there is nobody in this yard who has ever put anything under the ground in this yard."

"You have not asked anybody."

"Ask who," said the man of about thirty-two. "There is nine people on that ground and every one of them is at something."

About four feet off the near end of the first table the man of thirty-eight had the cloth in his hand and said nothing out loud at all, and nobody in that yard put anything to him.

---

He worked his own top in short lengths.

That was the whole of what he had by about eleven and it took him the rest of the morning to do what he used to do before the middle of the winter in about an hour and a bit.

A short length is about two feet of table, and he would lay the cloth along it and pull it and it would come off clean and flat, and he would move along to the next two feet and not come back to it until the afternoon. The table could rock all it wanted with his weight on the middle of it, because the middle of it was over sound ground and the boards under the middle of that table had not gone anywhere.

He tried a long length at about half past twelve and it took him four goes to get it and the first go left a smear on the boards that he had to go back over.

So he went back to short lengths.

At about half past twelve she came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of that second table and put a bar on her own pan and let it want to follow the wind until it came still, and she said nothing out loud that he heard, and nobody in that yard said one word to her about anything.

The boy came down off the slope at about one and stood about nine feet off the near end of that first table with his hands in his pockets.

"You do it in pieces now."

"I do it in pieces."

"You used to do it in one go."

"My table is on the edge of a hole and I am one man," said the man of thirty-eight.

The boy stood there for a while.

"Everybody in this yard is doing something in pieces this morning," he said, "and I am the only one who counts things and I have got nothing that comes out different twice, and I am not going to ask you about your table, because you have never once asked me about the boards, and that is the arrangement between the two of us and I am not the one who changes it."

He went back up the slope with his hands in his pockets.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"Two hundred and thirty-two mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have read every one of you off that stone twice today, and there is a man in this yard who has worked his own table in pieces all morning because the ground has gone under one end of it, and I watched him do it, and I am not going to say one word to him about it, and I have been the man who watched."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and thirty-second time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen in the holder and wrote the three hundred and forty-third morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

At about half past four the light went off those boards and they came off the cart face down and went up the bank, and about four people said the goodnight, and the turner banked the wheel and held the frame until it was still.

He was still at about four feet off the near end of that first table with the cloth in his hand when the light went off it, and he had not put a word out loud in that yard between half past nine and the light going, and nobody in that yard asked him anything.

---

That page at the back of that lot book was at sixty-two lines at the end of that morning and took no line on it. That lane stands at thirty pages and nothing has come down it this morning. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page are still unread in this district, as they have been. The fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months has been counted and it is on nothing at all. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet at the gatepost.
