# Chapter 1055: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Four Hundred Yards Walked

Nobody has ever come at that sheet with anything at all, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it has said the same for seven hundred and ninety-four days.

Nine hundred and fifty-three marks have been cut off that board. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-nine days and no wagon has come off it. At that rail, nothing has been entered for nine hundred and eighty-three days. Nine hundred and forty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days, was not run, is not a lot and is not open. This yard is six hundred and fifty-eight days behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and sixty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in this yard has stood. Seven hundred and thirty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and seventy-two days past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and thirty-six days ago. Six hundred and twelve marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and ninety-eight marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk and no list of anything in this district carries it. Four documents are not owned here. Those three lines can be read in four places and not in a fifth. Four conditions stand open and not one of them has an end on it. Fourteen things have been made here. Five were never got and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book had no place for a reason until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on it before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning that belongs at the back of that book. That page was at sixty-two lines and took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-nine mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and nineteen mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. Mornings a man has stood at the foot of that bank: three hundred and thirty-nine, entered after he said it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirty-eighth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-fourth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-three of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eightieth of those mornings and got all four. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-fourth afternoon running and gave that afternoon one word. That is the one hundred and ninety-fifth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer went to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-eighth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing in it, and five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and has never been asked of anybody. That lane has thirty pages on it in the carriers' own hands and delivered nothing this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-four days. Public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, with about nine people on it.

Nobody has ever been given either of those and nobody has gone over them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight has been paid and is spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have gone through this district without anybody here hearing them read.
---

He was at that low wall before the light came over the top of the bank, the way he had been every morning for as long as there had been mornings at the foot of that bank.

From where he stood the wall with the four figures on it was a grey shape about four hundred yards off, at the bottom of a slope, and at that distance the four figures were not figures to him. They had not been figures to him for some time and he had not told anybody that and was not going to.

He had read marks off a board for a living, a tide board first and then a depth board on a stretch of water that has a different name now, and the whole of that trade was taking a thing off a distance and being certain of it. He was as certain as anybody alive that a man knows the exact moment his own eyes begin to lie to him, and he knew the moment, and he had known it for long enough that he had stopped being afraid of it and started being bored by it.

---

At about a quarter to eight, four hundred yards off, a hand went up on that wall.

He watched the whole of it. The hand went up to the wall and stayed there, and came down, and went up again, and came down, and he counted four without meaning to, because counting is what his hands and his eyes do when there is nothing else for them to do.

Then the man laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest of them and left it lying there.

At that distance he could not hear one word of it and he could not have told anybody what was being said, and he did not try.

---

The boy came along the bottom of that slope at about half past eight with his hands in his pockets.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "There is nothing down that lane this morning and I have said it out loud at the boards."

"You say it twice a day."

"I say it twice a day and it is thirty twice a day," said the boy, "and there is a man at the end of that second table with a piece of pallet on his boards that he has not laid in anywhere, and I have counted nine spaces in those boards four times since I got here, and I have got nine four times, and I do not know what any of it is for."

"Nobody has ever told you."

"Nobody has ever told me," said the boy, "and I have stopped waiting to be told and I have started watching, which is a different thing and is not better, and I know it is not better."

He went on up the slope with his hands still in his pockets.

Nobody asked the man at that low wall a single question, then or on any other morning of that run, and the boy had not asked him one either, and the boy had come four hundred yards out of his way to stand next to him and say four things he could have said anywhere.

---

He walked the four hundred yards at about a quarter to four.

It took him longer than it used to take him. The ground along the bottom of that basin is not level and there are stones in it that have not moved since before either of those walls was put up, and he went the whole way and arrived at that other wall with about four minutes in hand.

The man of fifty-six was already there with his hand up.

He read the four off one after another in the order they come, and the man of about sixty-four stood about four feet off and could see the shape of his own hand going along the stone and could see that the numbers were figures and not marks and could see that one of them was a figure he had never once been able to see properly from four hundred yards, and that from four feet it was no better, and that it was not his eyes.

That is what he had wanted. He had wanted to know whether it was his eyes.

It was not his eyes. There was a figure on that wall that was out, and he could not tell which of the four, and he was not going to stand there and work it out in front of a man who was reading them.

He went back the four hundred yards and was at that low wall before the light came off the top of the bank.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up again and got all four of them off it and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and said his four words to that stone.

"Seven hundred and eighty mornings, all four of you."

Then he said his flat answer, which is the thing he says at that hour and has said at that hour since before the middle of the winter.

"Two hundred and twenty-eight mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and not one of the four of them has ever needed anything out of me, and I have never once wanted one of them to need something, and a man walked four hundred yards to find out that his own eyes were all right, and he will come again and neither of us will ever say a word about it."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and twenty-eighth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen in the holder and wrote the three hundred and thirty-ninth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it. He could not hear one word of it from that low wall and could tell from the shape of her mouth that she was giving all of it.

At about half past four the light came off the boards and they came off the cart face down and went up the bank, and about four people said the goodnight.

He stayed at that low wall in the dark for a while after that, and put his palm flat on the stone of it, and found that he could not tell by his thumb what he was feeling, and he could have done that a year ago.
---

That page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines and took no line this morning. That lane stands at thirty pages and delivered nothing this morning. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page are still not read by anyone in this district. The fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months is counted and it is on nothing at all this morning. The four hundred and eleven on that gatepost sheet is still on it and nobody has done a thing with it.
