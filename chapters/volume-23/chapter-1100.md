# Chapter 1100: The Three Hundred And Eighty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Boards Down Face Down

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and nobody in this basin has done one thing with it. That figure is eight hundred and thirty-nine days old.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety-eight marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and fourteen days and no wagon has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and twenty-eight days. Nine hundred and eighty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and it was not run, so it is not a lot and it is not open this morning. Seven hundred and three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and eight days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and seventy-eight days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and seventeen days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and eighty-one days back. Six hundred and fifty-seven marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and forty-three marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing here that holds it. Four documents have never come into this district at all, at any time, in any part. Three lines are readable in four places here and in no fifth place in it. Four conditions are set out in this district and none of them has an end upon it. Fourteen things is what this basin has made and there has never been anything else made in it. Five of those fourteen never reached this basin and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and that page at the back of that lot book did not take a line for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning came, so that nothing on it earlier than that was ever a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

No figure went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that page and no line went onto it either. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and fourteenth mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and sixty-fourth mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at that foot of that bank three hundred and eighty-fourth mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood there for the three hundred and eighty-third morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and thirty-ninth night of that run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and thirty-eight of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and twenty-fifth of those mornings and all four of them were got. That is the two hundred and fortieth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the two hundred and seventy-third time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

That book lies open along those boards with the back of it as bare as it has always been. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after that has a single name in it under a heading which still says whoever. A second space is ruled on that form and nobody in this district has ever been asked about it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in the hands of the people who brought them, and none of them came down it today. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-nine days. There were about nine people on that ground at ten to eight and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-ninth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Neither of them has ever reached anybody in this district and nobody here has ever been up to either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

Ninety-eight is what that toll came to and it has been paid and spent and there is nothing left of it. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page came through this district and not one of them has ever been read out here.

---

The stove was in before the light and the light came over the top of that bank at about a quarter to eight and lay along the whole length of that ground.

What he wanted off that morning was to finish at that table at about four and to have the cloth off his hand before the light went.

---

He came down to the near end of that first table at about half past nine and did the ordinary thing there, which is a thing nobody has ever asked him about and which he has never once described out loud in that yard.

He does not hurry it and he does not talk through it, and the four feet between him and the near end of that table is the four feet where a man can work without being spoken to, and that is the whole of what those four feet are for.

---

The man of about thirty-four and the man of about thirty-two were at the far end of that first table by about a quarter to ten with their cloths and a short length of batten between them, and they went at the ordinary business of that ground, and they talked about it.

"That ninth space of the boy's is a foot wider than the other eight and I would not have believed it of him."

"It is a mark on a board," said the man of about thirty-two. "It was never a rule. He has had it three years and has never once tried it against a board that did not fit it, and that is no fault of a boy of seventeen. That is only what a boy of seventeen is when nobody has given him a reason."

"Move the mark and the board fits. That is the whole of it and it took him a morning."

"Then he has it now and he does not need me to tell him what it is."

They worked the far edge of that table from about a quarter to ten until about half past eleven, and neither of them said one word to the man at those four feet who had his cloth in his hand, because there has never been a reason to, and because that is the four feet.

---

At ten to eight the boy was stood at those boards with his hands in his pockets.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "And nothing behind it, and I am not going to keep saying the other part."

Then the boy went up that slope and did not come back down it again that day.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale set up at her own pan about four feet off the near end of that second table and put her basket down beside it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices in that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.

Nobody in that yard put one word out loud about it.

The turner said one thing out loud at about two and it was about the boards.

"The boards go up while the light is on that ground and not one minute after," he said. "That load is two days in one and the near wheel is still finding the soft place at the second bend, so it goes up slow and it goes up once."

Nobody asked him anything about that or about anything else.

---

At about three the man of about thirty-two put his own top across the two trestles and squared it with the back of his heel and went along the top of it with his cloth, and the man of about thirty-four took his own table to pieces at the far end the way he takes it to pieces now, near edge first, with the twist in it the whole way.

"That frame of yours is a foot out of square and it has been for years."

"It has a coin of daylight under the near end of it and I have stopped looking at the coin."

"You have not stopped looking at it. You looked at it about twenty times this morning and every single time you told yourself that you were not looking at it." 

---

At about four the hand went up on that low wall, took four figures off the top of it one after another, and lay flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"The four of you, one more time today."

Then the other thing.

"Two hundred and seventy-three mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had the two of you twice and got them both times, and I am not going to say one word about that wall while I am stood at it, because a man that talks about a wall is talking about himself and not about it, and this stone gets what I say and nothing else."

That was the two hundred and seventy-third time running, and nobody in this district has ever put one question to him about it.

---

The clerk came down that row of boards at about four with the pen in the holder and read that morning out loud, and then she wrote the three hundred and eighty-fourth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand, and she took no line for it.

She has written a morning number in that band four hundred mornings and more and she has never once corrected for it and she did not do so this morning.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of that nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came down off that cart face down and went up that bank.

About nineteen people went up that bank that afternoon, and the two who did not go with them were the man with the cloth at about four feet off the near end of that first table and the woman with the cloth knotted over her scale and her basket on her arm about four feet off the near end of that second table.

The turner was stood at the frame at the near end of that first table, with that belt sitting in the middle of the groove between the two marks on the casting.

---

The man of about thirty-four was the last of those men to come away from that ground, and he left the piece of sound pallet lying on his own table in the middle of it with the square cut end pointing at the near edge, and he left the foot of it on the boards at the far end of that table where anybody walking past can see it.

When the last of them had gone up that slope the boards came down off that cart face down and lay there with their own undersides to the sky, and that yard was empty.

The lane count was thirty and nothing had come down that lane.

The page at the back of that lot book was at sixty-five lines and none of those sixty-five was in the lot book.

The sheet at that gatepost carried four hundred and eleven, and nobody in this basin has done anything with it.

---

That page stood at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it did not carry a sixty-sixth line. That lane brought nothing down it and it is at thirty pages behind it. Nobody in this district has ever heard those three read. The fifth of that five is five and unpaid. Seven months is counted in this district and nothing here holds it. That gatepost sheet will be carrying four hundred and eleven in the morning.

The boards came down off that cart face down and that yard was empty, and the frame stood at the near end of that first table with the turner standing at it, and the woman was standing at about four feet off the near end of that second table, and the man of thirty-eight was standing at about four feet off the near end of that first table with the cloth in his hand, and none of the three had gone up that bank.
