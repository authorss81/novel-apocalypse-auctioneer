# Chapter 1057: The Three Hundred And Forty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Far End Of That Table

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it is seven hundred and ninety-six days old, and nothing has ever been done about it in this basin.

Nine hundred and fifty-five marks have been cut off that board. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventy-one days and nothing has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and eighty-five days. Nine hundred and forty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long that bid stood open, and it was not run, so it is not a lot and it is not open this morning. This yard is six hundred and sixty days behind the figure on the second line of that book. Six hundred and sixty-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and thirty-five days since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and seventy-four days past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and thirty-eight days ago. Six hundred and fourteen marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk and there is no list of anything in this district for that count. Four documents are not owned here. Those three lines can be read in four places here and never in a fifth. Four conditions were set and none of them has ever had an end put on it. Fourteen things have been made here. Five were never got, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and there was no page at the back of that lot book for one to go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning of a kind that goes at the back of that book. That page is at sixty-two lines and it took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Three hundred and seventy-one mornings is how long the second table has stood at the foot of that bank. Three hundred and twenty-one mornings is how long the near rail has stood on the near side of it, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and fortieth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. A man has been at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-one mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-sixth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-five of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eighty-second of those mornings and got all four of them. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-sixth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. And that is the one hundred and ninety-seventh morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and thirtieth time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it, and five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever, and nobody has been asked what goes in the second space on that form. That lane carries thirty pages and delivered nothing this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-six days. It has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and about nine people are standing on it.

Nobody here has been given either of those and nobody here has walked them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight was paid, and is spent, and is done with. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have gone through this district and nobody in it heard them read.
---

He was at the end of that second table at half past eight, the end he has worked at for fifteen years, and he did the first hour and a half of that morning at that end.

The wheel was at its speed the whole morning. The turner was at the frame and did not say one word out loud at any point of it, and nobody in that yard put a question to him at any point of it, and nobody in that yard looked up.

At about half past twelve she came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of that second table and put a bar on her own pan and let it want to follow the wind until it came still, and she said nothing out loud that he heard.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and did not come back.

Nobody in that yard looked up for that either, and nobody in that yard has ever looked up.

---

At about two he went round the back of his own table and along it to the far end.

It is about four feet off the near end where he has been sitting every morning for fifteen years, and he went the whole length of that table with the cloth in his hand, and the light was on the boards at about eight feet of it and he went into it.

From that end he could see the whole of that ground at once for the first time in fifteen years.

The frame with the wheel on it was at the near end of the first table. The frame, and the length of that first table, and about four feet of the man of thirty-eight standing at the near end of it with a cloth in his hand and his back turned. The boards with nine spaces in them and the boy standing at the end of them counting. The boards themselves, going up that bank to a top that was about eleven feet above the wall.

And the man of thirty-four at the far end of a table of his own with a foot and a bit of pallet lying on it.

He had known every one of those was there the way a man knows the things in his own kitchen is in the dark. He had never once seen them all at the same time, because you cannot see the whole of a yard from one end of a table, and he had never once thought about that until his hand was on the cloth and he was standing at the far end of his own.

He worked at that end for about an hour.

There is a thing about the far end of a table. Nothing happens there that has not happened at the near end, and you do the same work with the same hands, and the top is in the same place and the boards under your boots are the same boards, and the light is on it, and it is a different hour.

---

The boy came along at about three and stopped about nine feet off him.

"You have never stood at that end."

"I have not," said the man at the end of that second table.

"Two days ago I counted the spaces in those boards and I got nine, and yesterday I got nine, and this morning I got nine, and I have said that out loud in this yard about four times a week since I came here and nobody has ever asked me what I am doing it for, and I have started to think that the counting is the whole of the job and the number is not the point."

The man at the end of that second table looked at him.

"You have been at these boards two years and about three weeks."

"Two years and about three weeks."

"Then you have said that number out loud about four hundred times."

"Somebody said four hundred," said Orren Pike. "I did not say four hundred. I have said thirty at ten to eight and I have said nine when there was nothing behind it, and those are the only two numbers I have ever said out loud in this yard and neither of them has ever been written down."

Nobody answered him.

---

The man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall at about four and took the four off it in the order they come and set his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Two hundred and thirty mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got all four of you off you again, and there is a man in this yard who walked to the far end of his own table this afternoon and came back, and I am not going to say one word about that to anybody, because a man came back."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and thirtieth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

---

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards with the book open against the rail and the pen still in the holder.

"Those boards," said the man at the end of that second table. "Do they go up at the same hour every day, or is that a thing they do."

The clerk gave the whole of that morning out loud with the pen in the holder, before she had her hand near any part of it.

"The three hundred and forty-first morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes at the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my own hand by itself with nothing in it, and that is the whole of the account of it, and it went out loud before the writing of it."

Then she wrote the three hundred and forty-first morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it, and she did not answer him, and nobody answered him.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and the cart stood against them until they came down, face down, and went up the bank. About four people said the goodnight. The turner banked the wheel at the frame and stopped it with his hand, which is what he does at the end of every day of that run.

---

That page stood at sixty-two lines and carried no line this morning. That lane stands at thirty pages and nothing came down it today. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page are still unread in this district, as they always have been. The fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months is counted and it is on nothing whatever. The figure four hundred and eleven is on that gatepost sheet still.
