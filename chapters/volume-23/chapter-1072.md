# Chapter 1072: The Three Hundred And Fifty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Thousand Days At That Rail

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and no hand in this basin has ever been on that post. That figure is eight hundred and eleven days old.

Nine hundred and seventy marks have been cut into that board. That train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and eighty-six days and has not had a wagon off it. At that rail nothing has been entered for one thousand days. nine hundred and sixty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and was not run, and is not a lot and not open this morning. This yard is six hundred and seventy-five days behind the figure on the second line of that book. The rule said out loud in that yard has stood six hundred and eighty days. It has been seven hundred and fifty days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is six hundred and eighty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and fifty-three days. six hundred and twenty-nine marks have been cut off that board. six hundred and fifteen marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months is counted by a clerk here and it stands on nothing. Four documents are wanted in this district and this district holds none of the four. There are four places here where those three lines can be read and there is no fifth of them. Four conditions stand here and not one of them carries an end. This yard has made fourteen things and there is no fifteenth in it. Five of those fourteen were never got in this yard and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that book, and that page is at sixty-three lines and took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and eighty-six mornings and that near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and thirty-six mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifty-six mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and fifty-fifth morning. It is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and eleventh night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and ten of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and ninety-seventh of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and twelfth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and it was the two hundred and forty-fifth time running.

The lot book is open on those boards with nothing entered in it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the page after that has one name in it under a heading which still reads whoever. The second space on that form has never been asked about and it is empty. That lane has thirty pages behind it, every one in its own carrier's hands, and nothing came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and seventy-one days. Public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about nine people on that ground.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and first afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of these and nobody here has ever walked either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll is ninety-eight, paid and spent. Three of the first thirty-third lines on that back page went through this district and nobody in it heard them read.


---



What he wanted off that morning was one number out of that yard that would come out different twice in one day, and he had been at it for four years and about three weeks.

He had four on a wall and a man got all four of them twice every morning and had done for longer than the boy had been alive. He had ten boards in that stack and it had been eleven a fortnight back. He had nine spaces in those boards every morning and one board on that stack that the light comes over first, and one thing through the gap in that bank in a week, and thirty pages behind that lane and not one of them in a year.



---



At ten to eight he said his count out loud at those boards before the clerk's hand had gone near anything.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and I have been thinking about it and I am not going to stop saying nothing behind it, because I have now said it for a week and a half and nobody has come down that lane to tell me there was something."

"Somebody may come down that lane for their own reasons," said the clerk, without looking up from the rail.

"Then they can say what they have come for," said the boy, "and I can go on saying nothing behind it."

He did the spaces at ten to eight anyway, and got nine.



---



He went along the near side of that second table at about a quarter to nine and stopped at the near rail.

He had walked past that rail every morning of every week since he first stood at those boards. He had never once put his hand on the top of it.

The top of it is worn. Not all over — there is a strip about the length of his hand and about the width of two of his fingers, and it is worn down into a shallow hollow with a shine on it, and it is the only part of that rail with a shine on it.

He put his own hand flat in that hollow and it fitted.

He stood there with his hand in it for the length of about nine breaths, and then he took it out and looked at the hollow, and then he put it back.



---



A thing put on that rail three thousand times by the same person in the same place would make that hollow. A thing put on that rail by four different people in four different places would make four dull patches instead of one.

So it was one thing, and it was one person, and it was the same nine inches of that rail every time, and there had been a last time.

He could not find out what the thing was. He had the shape of it and nothing else, and there was nothing in that yard that put a shape to it, and he was not going to go and ask the man who fitted that rail because that man was not to be thanked and asking a man you have to thank is worse than not asking.

So he put his hand back in it and left it there.



---



The man of about thirty-four came past about ten feet off at about ten and looked at what he was doing.

"You have found the shine on that rail."

"There is a shine on that rail."

"There has been a shine on that rail as long as I have been in this yard," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have known about it since I was nineteen and I have never put my hand in it."

"You have not put your hand in it because you have never wanted to know what it is for."

"I have never put my hand in it because I have never wanted to know," said the man of about thirty-four, "and that is a different thing and I would thank you not to put them together in this yard."

He went along his own boards and did not say another word.



---



At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan about forty feet off where he was standing, with her basket on the ground at her feet, and she put a bar on that pan and let it want the wind until it stopped wanting it.

He watched her do that from about four feet off and said nothing at all, and she said nothing at all, and neither of them had any reason to say anything to the other.

The bar went over and came back and went over to the same place it had gone before.

He watched it do that twice and then he stopped watching it and went back to the rail, and he was aware the whole time of not having asked her anything, and he has never known since which of those two things he was proud of.



---



At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off that bank and did not come back to that ground.

The wheel was at its speed the whole of that morning and the turner was at that frame from six and a half, and nobody in that yard said a word to him at any point of it.

There was one thousand days of nobody entering anything at that rail, and nobody in that yard looked up at that figure this morning either.



---



At about four the hand went up on that wall and got all four off it from the top down, and then it laid itself flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed there.

"Seven hundred and ninety-seven mornings, all four of you."

Then the flat answer, which is never twice the same sentence and has never once been tried as one.

"Two hundred and forty-five mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got you twice today and got you both times, and there is a boy in this yard with his hand in a hollow in the near rail and he has been in it since a quarter to nine, and he will not say what he is after and I am not going to ask him."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and forty-fifth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.



---



At ten past four the clerk read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder and before her hand had been near any part of it.

"The three hundred and fifty-sixth morning," she said. "One thousand days at that rail this morning and nothing went out loud in this yard that goes at the back of that book, so the morning goes down in my own hand and nothing beside it, and the saying of it came before the writing of it."

Then she wrote the three hundred and fifty-sixth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

About four of the nineteen said the goodnight at about half past four.



---



The boy put his hand flat in that hollow one last time and took his hand away and did not rub it out.



---



That page was at sixty-three lines when that morning was done and it took no line and it did not carry a sixty-fourth. Nothing came down that lane and it is at thirty pages where it has stood. Three of the first thirty-third lines on that back page are unread in this district and have always been. That fifth of the five is five and it is unpaid. Seven months has been counted and there is nothing here for that count to sit on. That gatepost carries four hundred and eleven and it will carry it in the morning.
