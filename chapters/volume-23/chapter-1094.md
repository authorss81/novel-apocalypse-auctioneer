# Chapter 1094: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And His Heel On That Frame

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it has been on that sheet through everything this basin has done. It is eight hundred and thirty-three days old.

That board carries nine hundred and ninety-two marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and eight days and no wagon has come off it in all of that. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and twenty-two days. Nine hundred and eighty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid was open four hundred and fifty days and was not run and it is not a lot and it is not open. Six hundred and ninety-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and seventy-two days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and eleven days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-five days back. Six hundred and fifty-one marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and thirty-seven marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing here that it sits on. Four of those documents have never come into this district at all. Three lines are readable in four places here and in no fifth. Four conditions have no end upon them in this district. This basin has made fourteen things in the world and no more than those fourteen. Five of them never came here at all and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book was not able to take a line for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning came along, so that nothing written on it earlier than that morning was a line for a reason, and no figure has ever been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it, and it is counted nowhere.

That page is at sixty-five lines and it did not take a line on it this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and eighth mornings and that near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and fifty-eighth mornings, and the man who fitted it is not to be thanked. Three hundred and seventy-eighth mornings is how long a man has stood at that foot of that bank on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventy-seventh morning and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. On the seven hundred and thirty-third night of his run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, having slept on seven hundred and thirty-two of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and nineteenth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and thirty-fourth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. This morning was the two hundred and sixty-seventh time running for the flat answer he gives to that stone.

That lot book lies open along those boards and there is nothing at the back of it. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and the column on the page after it holds one name under a heading which still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody in this district has ever asked anybody about it. Behind that lane there are thirty pages in the hands of those who brought them, and it brought nothing down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-three days. About nine people were stood on that ground at ten to eight, and that ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-third afternoon running and one word was the whole of it. Neither of these has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody in here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and there is nothing of it left. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page came through this district and no voice in this district has ever read one of them out.

---

The stove was in before the light and he had that wheel banked by six and a half and it was at its speed by ten to eight.

What he wanted off that morning was to put the middle back in that belt.

---

The belt had walked about half an inch to the left in the groove of that pulley and it had done it slowly, over a period nobody could date, and a belt that has walked half an inch in a groove is a belt that will walk the other half an inch the next time it is warm.

He has run that wheel at one speed for thirty-one years and that belt has walked about half an inch in all that time and it is the only thing about that frame that has changed in it. He had been putting it back by hand for as long as he had been at that frame and he had never once left a mark on the casting to say where the middle of that belt was supposed to be, because there was nowhere to put a mark on a casting that somebody comes through with a rag every morning.

What he wanted was two marks. That was the whole of what he wanted off that morning.

---

Ten to eight and the boy was stood at those boards with the light still lying along the front of that stack.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "And I have stopped saying the other part."

"You never said an other part."

"You have been here every morning I have said that number," said the boy, "and you have never once asked me what the other part was, and I have noticed that, and I am not asking you about it now."

Then he went away up the slope and put the pencil behind his ear.

---

He got that belt off the pulley by hand and it took about forty minutes because the stiff end of it has to come round through the last quarter of the turn and there is no way to do that with anything but your hands and a bad temper.

He laid it out on the ground beside that frame, well away from the boards, and found the damp end heavier than the rest of it by about as much as a cup of water in a coat pocket, and the other end stiff, and the middle of it the only part of that belt worth anything.

He worked the stiff end round into the groove with his thumb and got it a half inch nearer the middle than it had been and then held it there while he counted, and then let go of it, and it stayed.

---

At about nine he got to the front of that sentence of his.

He has got to the front of it most mornings and he has never once got past the front of it, and he has never told anybody that he gets that far, and he stopped there and went and did the other thing on the list.

---

He got a nail out of his pocket at about a quarter to ten and a short file, and he put the point of the nail on the casting where the middle of that belt had been on the day he came to that frame, and he looked at it a while and then he took the nail away and put it in his pocket again and got the other end of that belt and put it where it belonged and took the nail out and tapped a small mark into the paint.

That is a mark the width of a grain of rice and it is the only mark he has ever put on that casting.

---

"You have got the belt in the middle."

The man of about thirty-four was standing at the near end of that first table with the cloth over his arm and had been standing there some time.

"It was half an inch out."

"It was where it was when you and I were last at it."

"It was where it had got to," said the turner. "It is a different thing. What is where it got to is a thing that happened to the belt. What is where I left it is a thing I did."

He got his rag out of his coat and wiped his hands down the front of his coat instead, because the rag was dirty and his coat was not, and that was the whole of the answer and it was a complete one.

---

"They go up while the light is on them and not one minute after," he said out loud, out on the boards where about four people could hear him, "and the ground under that cart is still soft from what was lying on it yesterday and whoever stands on it will go in to the ankle."

Nobody asked him anything.

He went back down to that frame and put his hands in his pockets and stood at the near end of that first table for a while.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan four feet off the near end of that second table with her basket on the ground at her feet.

About half past one his heel went on to that frame and the wheel came off.

He took his heel off and stood there with his hands at his sides for about as long as it takes to put a heel on and take it off again, and then he set that heel on the ground where he could find it again and went and got the file he does not use and stood it up in the corner of that wheelhouse out of the wet.


---

The man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.

Nothing else went out of that yard that day.

---

He got a file and a cold chisel and went at the grease on that casting, which has been on that casting since before he came and which is not coming off and which he has not once got all the way back to the metal under.

He got about the size of a thumbnail off it and then he stopped, because the casting under the grease is soft and a man who wants that wheel to keep its speed does not go further into a casting with a cold chisel than he has to.

He put the tools down and stood and looked at the two marks on that casting and at the belt sitting in the middle of the groove between them.

---

At about four there was a voice at that low wall and it was not raised and he was at the far end of that ground with his back to it and he did not go over.

Then a second voice at that wall, longer than the first, and after that nothing at that end of the ground for a while.

At about four the clerk's voice came along the row of boards and it came in on him as a shape and not as words, the way it has come in on him for as long as he can remember, and he did not hear one word of it.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of that nineteen said the goodnight and the boards came down off that cart face down and went up that bank.

He stood at that frame and watched the last of them go up the slope, and the two marks are on that casting where he put them at about a quarter to ten, and the length of belting is still lying flat on those boards with its damp end under it, and the straight edge is still lying on the top rail of that frame where he left it, and the nail is still in the wall of that wheelhouse with nothing on it.

---

That page was at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it has not taken a line on it. That lane brought nothing down it this morning and it is at thirty pages behind it. Not one of those three lines has ever been read out by anybody in this district. That fifth of the five stands at five and is unpaid. Seven months is counted here and there is nothing it sits on. The sheet at that gatepost will still be carrying the same four hundred and eleven in the morning.

That wheel came off that frame about half past one and it did not go back up that day.

Nobody in that yard started it and nobody asked him why.
