# Chapter 1053: The Three Hundred And Thirty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And His Heel On The Frame

Nobody has ever come at that sheet with a rule or a string or a wish, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. It has stood on that post for seven hundred and ninety-two days and it has carried the same figure since before anything in this yard was built.

Nine hundred and fifty-one marks have been cut into that board. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixty-seven days and has had nothing taken off it. At that rail nothing has been entered for nine hundred and eighty-one days. Nine hundred and forty-two days lie between the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days. It was never run, so it is not a lot and is not open. This ground is six hundred and fifty-six days behind the figure on the second line of that book. The rule said out loud in this yard has stood six hundred and sixty-one days, and it is seven hundred and thirty-one days since the first day of the eighth month, and six hundred and seventy days is how far past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off has got.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and thirty-four days back. Six hundred and ten marks have been cut off that board, and five hundred and ninety-six marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk in this district and there is no list of anything for that count to sit on. Those three lines can be read in four places here and not in a fifth. Four conditions were set down here once and none of them has an end on it. Four documents are not owned here. Fourteen things have been made here, five were never got, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

Out loud in that yard, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, a reason was said, and the page at the back of that lot book had nowhere to hold one until the two hundred and eighth morning, so that nothing written on it before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning of the kind that goes at the back of that book. That page stood at sixty-two lines and took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixty-seven mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and seventeen mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and thirty-sixth morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirty-seven mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-second night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-one of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and seventy-eighth of those mornings and got all four. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-second afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. And so that is the one hundred and ninety-third morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer went to that stone twice and this morning it was the two hundred and twenty-sixth time running.

That lot book lies open on those boards with nothing entered in it, and five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and a column down the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever. Nobody has been asked what belongs in the second space on that form. That lane carries thirty pages in the carrier's own hand and brought nothing down this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-two days. Public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are standing on it.

Nobody in this district has ever gone over either of those, and nobody has ever been given them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll was ninety-eight and it is paid and spent. Three lines of the first thirty-three on that back page went through this place without anyone here hearing them read.
---

He had banked the wheel by six and a half and it was at its speed by ten to eight, and there was a length of belting in his coat that had been in his coat two mornings now and had not gone near the frame.

The light comes off the top of that bank in a wedge and takes the boards first, and he has watched it do that since before the boy came to those boards, and the boards are what he sets the morning by.

At ten to eight the boy said his count out loud with the light still flat on the bank.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and there is nothing behind it this morning."

The clerk turned a page with her thumb and did not write.

"You said that yesterday morning."

"I said it the morning before that as well," said the boy. "You have not got any of them written down and there is nothing in the book to say there were not any of them either."

The clerk did not answer him, and she is not a woman who is asked what a line is for, and the boy has never once asked her.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down that bank and set up about four feet off the near end of that second table and put a bar on her own pan, and she let it want to follow the wind until it came still, and she said nothing out loud at all that morning.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally stood where he stands and put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it.

About four voices took it up after him, out of step with one another, one of them a half beat behind the other three, and it went off the face of that bank and was gone, and there was nothing in that yard at that hour that went out after it.

About four feet off the near end of the first table the man of thirty-eight had the cloth in his hand and had had it since half past nine and had not said a word out loud all morning, and the cloth was the only thing about him that moved that hour except his arms.

---

The turner was at the frame at half past one and he put his heel on it.

That is the only way that wheel has ever come off that frame. The frame takes the weight of his leg and the wheel comes off it and the sound goes out of that yard and comes back in, and it takes about as long to go and come as it does every time, and he has never once counted it and never will.

The frame is cold under his hand at that hour in that weather and it takes the skin off the palm if you keep it there, and he has kept it there before and knows what it does.

He stood there with his hand on the frame and looked at the boards.

Not one person in that yard said a word about it. Nobody came over to where he was standing. Nobody asked him why and nobody thanked him.

About nine people were in that yard and about four of them were at the top of eleven feet at that hour, and not one of them looked down the bank.

He got as far as the front of his sentence about half past one, the way he does, and he stopped there.

Then he took his heel off the frame and let the wheel take up its speed again, and it took it up at exactly the speed it had been going at, and it was the same sound it had been making since ten to eight, and he did not stop it again that day.

He went on with what he was doing, which was wiping the grease off his hands on a rag and putting the rag over his shoulder and looking at the belt on the frame without touching it.

A length of belting in a coat is not a thing a man carries for years because he cannot carry it. He has carried it into that yard and out of it on every morning of that run and it has been in the coat pocket the whole time, and he has never once worked out what a man is doing when he puts a thing in a pocket and then takes it out every morning and puts it back.

The man of about thirty-two came along his own boards about two with the cloth over his arm and stopped where he stops, four feet short of where he would have liked to be.

"My corner is square this morning."

"I heard you telling the boy about it."

"I told him the fault and not the corner," said the man of about thirty-two. "The fault is a bowed board and I can get at that without help."

"That is the first thing said in this yard today that is worth writing on anything," said the turner, and meant it as a thing about wood, and the man of about thirty-two took it that way and went along his own top with the cloth.

---

The woman of about fifty-two came down that bank at about three with a frame under her arm and a bag in her hand, and stopped at those boards and stood looking along them at nothing in particular.

"They will go up at half past four," said Orren Pike.

"They will," said the woman of about fifty-two.

"You have had that frame up and down this bank every morning of a run of mornings and you have never once put it down on this side of it."

"There is nowhere on this side to put it down," said the woman of about fifty-two, "and that is not a thing I am going to say out loud twice."

He went back to the end of the boards and counted the nine spaces again and got nine, and put the pencil in his pocket.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six had his hand up on that wall and got all four off it and laid his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"Two hundred and twenty-six mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have got all four of you off you and I could take any one of them off you with my hand shut, and that is the whole of what I have ever wanted off a wall, and nobody in this yard has ever wanted anything off it at all."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and twenty-sixth time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any of it.

At ten past four the clerk came along those boards and gave the whole of that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder and before she had her hand near any part of it, and he could not hear one word of it from the frame and could hear that she was giving all of it.

At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that is said at the end of a day in that yard. The boards came off the cart face down and went up the bank.

He banked the wheel and put his hand on the frame to stop it, which is what he does at the end of a day, and had done at the end of every day of that run.

---

The page at the back of that lot book stood at sixty-two lines and took no line on that morning. About half past one on the three hundred and thirty-seventh morning the man of about forty-eight said his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four voices took it up out of step with one another and nothing came back out of that yard at any of them. That lane stands at thirty pages and brought nothing down this morning. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page have never been read by anyone in this district. The fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months is counted by a clerk and it is on nothing at all. Four hundred and eleven is on that sheet at the gatepost and will be on it this afternoon.
