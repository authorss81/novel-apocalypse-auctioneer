# Chapter 1060: The Three Hundred And Forty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Number Not Written Down

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it is seven hundred and ninety-nine days old, and nothing in this basin has ever been done with it.

Nine hundred and fifty-eight marks have been cut off that board. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventy-four days and has not had a wagon come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for nine hundred and eighty-eight days. Nine hundred and forty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

That bid stood open four hundred and fifty days and never once in that time. It was never run at all, so it is not a lot and is not open. Six hundred and sixty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and sixty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and thirty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. Six hundred and seventy-seven days past a printing it did not make that body four hundred miles off.

The ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and forty-one days ago. Six hundred and seventeen marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and three marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted by a clerk and no list of anything in this district holds that count. Four documents are not owned here. Those three lines can be read in four places and not in a fifth one of them. Four conditions stand open in this district and not one of them has an end on it. Fourteen things have been made here. Five were never got and the fifth of that five is still unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning there was a reason said out loud in that yard, and there was no line on the page at the back of that lot book for one to go on until the two hundred and eighth morning, and so nothing written on that page before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing that goes at the back of that book went out loud in that yard this morning, and that page stood at sixty-two lines from the first board up to the last one off and took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-four mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and twenty-four mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and forty-third morning and is neither the second table nor the near rail. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and forty-four mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and ninety-ninth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ninety-eight of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the seven hundred and eighty-fifth of those mornings and got all four. A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the two hundred and eighty-ninth afternoon running and gave that afternoon its one word. Two hundred mornings running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and thirty-third time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it, and five lines are ruled across one of its pages, and the column on the next of them holds one name under a heading that still says whoever, and the second space on that form is empty and nobody has ever been asked to fill it. Thirty pages have gone down that lane in their own carriers' hands and nothing came down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and fifty-nine days. It has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and about nine people are on it.

Nobody here has ever been given either of those and nobody here has ever walked them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent, and three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page went through this district and nobody in it heard them read.
---

She was at those boards before the light with the book open against the rail and the pen in the holder, and she did the thing she does perhaps once in ten mornings, which is to look at the lines on that back page without the pen near it.

She had been looking at them for fifteen years. She never wrote a figure down when she did it and she never told anybody she did it, and there was nobody in that yard she could have told, because the whole of what everybody else knew about that page was what she said out loud about it.

She got sixty-two, and then she got sixty-two again from the bottom up, and the second one took her three goes and she was not certain of the third.

---

At ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards with the light flat on the bank.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike, "and no figure after it."

She shut the book and did not write anything in it.

Then he stood there with the pencil going round in his fingers, and she could see that he was working at something, the way a man works at a thing he is deciding whether to do.

"How many spaces are in those boards," she said.

The boy looked up.

"Nine," he said.

"Nine," said the clerk.

He went off up the slope and she stood at the rail and looked at the back of that page for a while with the pen in her hand, and she did not write nine on anything, and she has never written it on anything.

---

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of that second table and put a bar on her own pan.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number up into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices took it up after him out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank and was gone.

About four feet off the near end of that first table the man of thirty-eight had the cloth in his hand and had had it since half past nine, and that was the two hundredth morning running with nobody having asked him anything, and nobody in that yard knew that there was anything in it to be asked, and she was at the rail about nine feet off him with the pen in her hand.

---

The woman of about fifty-two came down the bank at about two with a frame under her arm and a bag in her hand and stopped at the boards and stood looking at the rail for a while.

"There is a place on my form that has been blank a long time," she said.

"I know the place."

"You do not know the place, you know that I have got a form with a space on it, which is a different thing and is how I get by most mornings."

"That is a fair way round it."

The woman of about fifty-two laughed, and it went about four feet along the boards and stopped, the way it does.

"There is a thing on my form that I could write in that space," she said, "and I have known what it was for a run of mornings, and the reason I do not write it is not that I have not got it."

"Then it is not the writing," said the clerk.

"It is not the writing," said the woman of about fifty-two, and she put the bag on the boards and then picked it up again and did not sit down.

Neither of them asked the other anything, and whatever the four people at the top of that bank have made of the two of them, they have kept to themselves about it, and nobody in that yard has ever put one of them to the other.

She went up the bank with the frame and the bag about ten past four, and the space on that form was still blank when she went up it.

---

The hand went up on that wall at about four and got all four off it and stayed on the stone under the lowest one for a while.

"Two hundred and thirty-three mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have never once got one word off anybody about you, and there is a woman on that bank tonight going up with a form in a bag and there is a boy on these boards who gets nine every time, and every single thing in this yard is in the same place it was in, and I say that to you because you are the only one in here that never asks me a question, so you are the only one I can say it to."

About nineteen people heard that, and it was the two hundred and thirty-third time running, and nobody has ever asked him anything about any part of it.

---

At ten past four she came along those boards and read that morning out loud with the pen still in the holder and before she had her hand near any part of it.

"The three hundred and forty-fourth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in that yard this morning that belongs at the back of that book, so this morning goes down this afternoon in my own hand with nothing in it beside it, and that is the whole of what there is of it, and the saying of it came before the writing of it."

Then she wrote the three hundred and forty-fourth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

That line went over the worn place in that paper and it did not sit level with the rest of them, and she has never once tried to correct for it in the whole of the fifteen years she has been opening that book.

The wheel went up at six and a half that morning as it has gone up at six and a half every morning of thirty-one years.

---

The covers came over that book at about half past four with the boards down off the cart face down and going up the bank ahead of it, and about four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard.

That page stood at sixty-two lines when the covers came over it and it did not move and it did not carry a sixty-third line. That lane stands at thirty pages and nothing came down it this morning or on any morning behind it. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that back page are still unread in this district and always have been. The fifth of that five is unpaid. Seven months has been counted and it is on nothing whatever. Four hundred and eleven is on the sheet at that gatepost and will be there tomorrow.
