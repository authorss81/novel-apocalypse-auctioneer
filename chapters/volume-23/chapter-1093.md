# Chapter 1093: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Two Feet Of Near End

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and there is no use made of it anywhere in this basin. That figure is eight hundred and thirty-two days old on that sheet.

Nine hundred and ninety-one marks are cut into that board. One thousand three hundred and seven days is how long that train has stood on that siding and no wagon has come off it. Nothing has been entered at that rail for one thousand and twenty-one days. Nine hundred and eighty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is all of that bid and it was not run and it is not a lot and not open. Six hundred and ninety-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Seven hundred and one days is how long that rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and seventy-one days since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and ten days past a printing it did not make.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is eight hundred and seventy-four days back. Six hundred and fifty marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and thirty-six marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district and there is nothing whatever in this district for it. Four documents are outside this district and have always been. Three lines can be read in four places here and there is no fifth. Four conditions have been set out in this district with no end upon any one of them. Fourteen is what this basin has made. Five of those fourteen were never got in this basin at all and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and that page at the back of that lot book was not to take a line for a reason until the two hundred and eighth morning came, so nothing on it earlier than that was ever a line for one, it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

That page stands at sixty-five lines and took no line on it this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A figure went out of that yard in her voice this morning and another one went out of it about two minutes afterwards, and the page at the back of that lot book has no line for either.

Four hundred and seventh mornings has that second table stood at the foot of that bank, and three hundred and fifty-seventh mornings has the near rail stood on the near side of it, and the man who fitted it is not to be thanked. Three hundred and seventy-seventh mornings is how long a man has stood at that foot on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two was at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventy-sixth morning, which is not that second table and is not that near rail either. On the seven hundred and thirty-second night of his run the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, and he had slept on seven hundred and thirty-one of the nights of it. Four figures came off that wall for the eight hundred and eighteenth of those mornings this morning and all four of them were got. Nobody having asked him anything has now run for the two hundred and thirty-third morning. What he said to that stone after the four of them was the two hundred and sixty-sixth time running.

That lot book has nothing written at the back of it and it lies open along those boards with the covers off it. One page of it has five lines ruled across it, and the column on the page after that has one name in it under a heading which still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has ever asked about it. Behind that lane are thirty pages in the hands of those who brought them, and it brought nothing down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and ninety-two days. About nine people are stood on that ground this morning, and it has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and twenty-second afternoon running and that afternoon was one word and no more of it. Neither of these has ever been given to anybody in this district and nobody here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have gone through this district without a voice in it ever reading one of them out. That toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and nothing of it is left over.

---

The near end of that top has been down about the thickness of a thumb for as long as he has owned it, and the far end of it has been up about the thickness of two fingers, and both of those have held at the same time on every morning he has worked at it.

What he wanted off that morning was for the near end of that top to stay up.

He had given up on the whole of it being flat about a week ago, because a top that is flat is a top that has been planed and a top that is planed is a top that is not that top any more, and he has enough of that wood as it is. But the near end coming up was a different matter. The near end coming up was not about flatness. The near end coming up was about the four inches of air under the leg nearest his own left knee, and he had been looking at that air for years.

---

He got to his own table at about seven and put the cloth down on it and got his hand under the near corner and lifted.

It came up about a thumb's thickness and then the wood stopped and the rest of that top stayed where it was, because that top is twisted and not dipping, and a twisted top is two boards pretending to be one board and they lie at an angle to one another along their whole length.

He had it square edge on his knee by about half past seven and he was looking down the length of it at the angle in the daylight when the light came over the top of that bank.

---

Ten to eight and the boy was at those boards.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike. "Nothing down it."

That is all the boy has said at that hour this week and he has stopped saying the rest of it.

---

The turner had that wheel at its speed by ten to eight and he was at the near end of that frame and he was talking to a man about the length of a board, and after a while he came along behind the first table and said one thing out loud that was not about anything in particular.

"Anybody that stands on those boards in the wind gets a foot on them and not a hand on them, and anybody that picks up a board in the wind picks up a lever and not a board."

Then he went back down to that frame and nobody put a question to him about any part of it.

---

The near two feet of that top came down on the trestles at about nine and stayed down for the rest of the morning, which was what he wanted, and which cost him about half an inch of air under the leg nearest his own left knee.

He worked from the near edge inward, the way he has worked it since he stopped trying to get it flat, and he took the twist with him the whole length of it and let it be there.

At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale was at her own pan, four feet off the near end of that second table, with her basket on the ground at her feet, and a figure went out of that yard in her voice.

About two minutes after it a second figure went out of that yard in a man's voice from about four feet off the near end of that first table.

That page at the back of that lot book took no line for either of them.

---

He went on with the near edge of that top.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four voices in that yard carried it on out of step with one another, and he went on with the near edge.

The man of thirty-eight was at about four feet off the near end of that first table from about half past nine until about four with his cloth in his hand, and nobody in that yard asked him one thing at any point of the whole of it.

---

At about one he put his hand in his pocket and took out the foot and about four inches of sound pallet with the square cut on the end of it.

He had carried that piece since long before anybody in that yard knew he had it and he had laid it down nowhere and he had told nobody what it was for, and this morning he thought he would find out whether a sound packing piece would hold a low corner up.

He cut it to fit the low side of that corner, drove it in with the heel of his hand, and put the weight of that top back on it.

The top came up about a quarter of an inch and settled again, further over than it had been, because sound wood packs harder than the loose stuff he had been putting in there and hard packing under a twisted corner rolls the twist.

He pulled it out and looked at it for a while and then he went and got the grit off his own cutting from the bin at the back of his table, which is soft and closes up behind the blade of a spade, and he packed that in instead.

That held the corner up about a quarter of an inch and it stayed up.

---

He left the pallet lying on the top of his own table where he had put it, in the middle, with the square cut end pointing at the near edge, and he did not lay it in anywhere.

He has had it a long time and he has never laid it anywhere and he did not lay it anywhere on this morning either, and the only thing he got out of the morning was that it is the wrong wood for what he had thought it was.

"You tried it," said the man of about thirty-two, coming along his own boards later with the cloth over his arm.

"I tried it and it is the wrong wood."

"You are not going to use it?"

"It is sound," said the man of about thirty-four. "You do not put sound in a soft hole and expect the soft to hold it up. I have known that since I was about nine years old and I have gone on carrying it anyway."

---

The hand went up on that low wall at about four and brought four figures off the top of it one after another, and then it lay flat on the stone under the lowest one and stayed where it was.

"You four. Once before and once after."

Then he said the other thing.

"Two hundred and sixty-six mornings," he said out loud to that stone, "and I have had the two of you twice and got them both times, and I have got a corner of a table up a quarter of an inch this morning with soft stuff out of my own bin, which is what it takes, and I am not going to tell anybody else that, and this stone is not going to tell anybody either."

That was the two hundred and sixty-sixth time running, and nobody in this district has ever put one question to him about it.

---

The clerk came down that row of boards at about four with the pen still in the holder and read that morning out loud all the way through before her hand went near any part of it, and then she wrote the three hundred and seventy-seventh morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took no line for it.

It went out loud before the writing of it, and there is no band of thin paper to write it on tonight because a morning number sits above the band on this page.

Nobody in that yard has ever asked her what a line on that page is for, and nobody did this morning.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and four of that nineteen said the goodnight and the boards came down off that cart face down and went up that bank.

He squared his own top along the near edge, wiped the grit out of the low side of that corner with the heel of his hand so it would not cut into the leg, and left the pallet lying in the middle of it with the square end at the near edge.

---

That page was at sixty-five lines when the covers came over it and it took no line on it. That lane brought nothing down it in this morning and it is still at thirty pages behind it. Not one of those three has ever been read out by anybody in this district. Nothing has been done about the fifth of that five, which stands at five. The count of seven months is on nothing at all. That gatepost sheet goes on carrying four hundred and eleven into the morning.

One figure went out loud in that yard this morning and a second figure went out loud about two minutes after it, and that page took no line for either.
