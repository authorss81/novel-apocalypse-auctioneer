# Chapter 1089: The Three Hundred And Seventy-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Four Inches In From The Foot

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and nothing has ever come off that sheet in this basin to shift it. That figure is eight hundred and twenty-eight days old.

That board carries nine hundred and eighty-seven marks. That train on that siding has stood one thousand three hundred and three days and no wagon has come off it. Nobody has entered anything at that rail for one thousand and seventeen days. Nine hundred and seventy-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is all of that bid, and it was not run, and it is not a lot and not open this morning. Six hundred and ninety-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that book this yard stands. Six hundred and ninety-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Seven hundred and sixty-seven days have gone by since the first day of the eighth month. That body four hundred miles off is seven hundred and six days past a printing it did not make.

That ninth of the nine printed nights went back eight hundred and seventy days. Six hundred and forty-six marks have been cut off that board. Six hundred and thirty-two marks in chalk stand along the edge of that second table.

Seven months has been counted in this district by a clerk and there is nothing whatever in this district that it sits on. Those four documents have never come into this district at all, and not one of the four is in it. Those three lines are readable in four places here and there is no fifth place here that has any of them. Four conditions are set out in this district and no end has been put on one of them. Fourteen is what has been made in this basin and there is nothing else. Five of those fourteen things were never got in this district at all, and the fifth of that five is unpaid.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and there was no page at the back of that lot book that would take a line for one before the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing written on it before that morning was a line for a reason, and it is counted nowhere, and no figure has been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

Nothing went out loud in that yard this morning for the back of that book, and that page stands at sixty-four lines and it took no line on it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

That second table has stood at the foot of that bank four hundred and three mornings and that near rail has stood on the near side of it three hundred and fifty-three mornings, and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventy-three mornings on the word of the man who keeps it. The table of the man of about thirty-two stood at that end of that ground for the three hundred and seventy-second morning, and it is not that second table and it is not that near rail. The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the seven hundred and twenty-eighth night of that run, having slept on seven hundred and twenty-seven of the nights of it. A man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall this morning for the eight hundred and fourteenth of those mornings and got all four of them. That is the two hundred and twenty-ninth morning running with nobody having asked him anything. That flat answer was given to that stone twice this morning and this morning it was the two hundred and sixty-second time running.

That lot book lies open along those boards with nothing entered in it in any part. Five lines are ruled across one of its pages and its one name stands in the column on the page after that, under a heading that still says whoever. A second space on that form stands empty and nobody has ever been asked about it. That lane is at thirty pages behind it in their own carriers' hands and it brought nothing down it this morning. That record has stood two hundred and eighty-eight days. That ground has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning and about nine people are standing on it this morning at ten to eight.

A man of sixty-one sat on those stones for the three hundred and eighteenth afternoon running and one word was the whole of that afternoon. Nobody in this district has ever been given either of these two and nobody here has ever been up to see either of them: about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

That toll came to ninety-eight and it has been paid once and there is nothing left of it. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three came through this district and nobody in this district heard one of them read.

---


She came in past the boards at about half past seven, which was not her hour, and she had the book under her arm and the pen still in the holder.

She could not have said why she was in early if anybody had put it to her, and nobody put anything to her, and there is nobody in that yard who would.

What she wanted off that morning was to get to the end of it.

That was all she wanted and it was fifteen years old. She read the morning out loud before she wrote anything on it, every morning she wrote at all, and on every one of those mornings there was a place in the reading she went over rather than through.


---


At ten to eight the boy was at those boards with the light lying along that stack and not yet off it.

"Thirty," said Orren Pike.

That was the whole of what he got. He had said three mornings running that he was going to stop saying the rest of it, and he did not say the rest of it, and he put the pencil behind his ear and went off up the slope.

She wrote nothing at ten to eight and she did not want to. Her hand had not been near that book since she had put it on those boards.


---


At about half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale set up at her own pan about four feet off the near end of the second table with her basket on the ground at her feet, and she let the bar want the wind until it stopped wanting it.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four voices out of that yard took it up after him, out of step with one another, and it went off the face of that bank.

That wheel was at its speed and the turner was at the near end of that frame.

None of it went into that yard as a reason.


---


The wind came up at about one and it came up out of the north-east, and by two it had found the eleven feet.

That is the only thing that happened in that yard that day and about nine people were in it.

A board that has been lying flat on that ground since before the light is heavy in a way that has nothing to do with what it weighs. The moment there is a hand on the near end of it, the far end is trying to lift, and the wind does not have to do much to decide which of those two things happens.

"You get a foot on it," said the man of about thirty-four, "and you do not let that foot go up."

Nine of them stood on those boards for about an hour in the places they had chosen themselves. Nobody organised it. Nobody said the word that would have made it an arrangement, and the man of about thirty-four did not say it out loud at the time, and what he said was about a foot and not about anybody else being in that yard.

About four feet off the near end of the first table the man of thirty-eight had both hands on the second board up from the bottom and his weight over it, and he stayed where he was, and nobody put a question to him at any point of that hour, and nothing has ever been put to him.


---


At about two the turner came along the far end of those boards with the rag in his hand.

"The boards go up while the light is on them and not one minute after," he said, "and the wind takes the last of the light off them early today, and I am telling everybody that at once so that nobody stands at the end of a board at half past four wondering what has happened to his afternoon."

Nobody asked him anything.

He went back down to that frame and put his hands in his pockets and watched that ground, which is not his work and which he has done for years.


---


She had read that morning out loud four hundred mornings and more, and she had two forms of words for it, and she had had them since she was twenty-six.

The first is for a morning when nothing went out loud. It is short and it ends on the word morning and there is no room in it for what was done in that yard.

The second is for a morning when something did. She has used it four times in fifteen years and it is longer and it has a place in the middle of it for the thing itself, and the place is the same size every time, and she has never once had to make it any bigger or any smaller than the thing that had to go in it.

What she has done for fifteen years is come to the end of the form and then stop and start again somewhere further on, so that anybody listening hears a pause where the third sentence would be if there were a third sentence.


---


She came along those boards at about four and the light was still a long way off the ground and she got to the end of it.

That was the whole of what she came early for.

"The three hundred and seventy-third morning," she said. "The wind came up out of the north-east at about one and about nine people stood on those boards for about an hour with a foot on each of them and nobody arranged it, and a man at the far end of them told everybody the boards go up while the light is on them and not one minute after, and one man stood at about four feet off the near end of the first table with both hands on the same board for an hour and nobody said one word to him and he did not say one word either, and about four feet of that second table went under a bar at half past twelve and the wind had at it for the rest of the day. Nothing went out loud in that yard today for the back of this book, and there is nothing at the back of this book for it."

She got to the end of that and there was nothing after it to go over.

She stood there with the pen still in the holder and found that she had said the whole of a morning out loud in one piece for the first time, and she had got about a minute and a half of it out of the yard's own noise, and about nine people had heard every word of it and every one of them had a foot on a board.


---


She sat down on those boards to write it.

That is not a thing she does. She writes standing up, at the end of them, with the book open on the near rail, because that is where the rail is and because fifteen years is long enough to make a place into a reason.

She had time. That was the other half of coming early and it had not occurred to her until she was already sitting down with it.

There is a band in that paper four inches in from the foot of the back page, about the width of two fingers, where the page has gone thin. She has known about it for years and she has never once made any allowance for it and she is not going to start now, and a morning number written across that band comes out with a gap in the middle of it where the nib stops catching and lets go.

She wrote the morning number under the last line.

Three hundred and seventy-third.

The gap came out in the middle of it, where it always comes out, and the ink spread either side of the gap into the thin of the paper and made the two halves of the figure further apart than a figure ought to be.

She looked at it for a while.

Then she put the pen back in the holder and did not do anything about it, and she did not count the lines on that page, and nobody in that yard has ever asked her what a line on it is for and nobody is going to.


---


At about half past four the light came off those boards and about four of the nineteen said the goodnight, and the boards came down off that cart face down and went up the bank, earlier than they do on most mornings, and darker on the underside than they are on most mornings.

The wind had not gone down. It had got up another notch at about four and that ground had been standing at about four people for two hours by then.


---


She went up that slope behind the last board with the book shut under her arm and the pen in her pocket, and she had not taken a line for that morning and she was not going to.

At the top of that bank there were about four people and none of them said anything about any of it.


---


That page stood at sixty-four lines when she shut it and it took no line on it. That lane stood at thirty pages all morning and not one page came down it. Of the first thirty-three lines on that back page, three have never once been read out in this district. The fifth of those five is five and unpaid and it will be unpaid at the end of this morning as well. Seven months has been entered in this district and nothing in here holds it. That gatepost sheet carries four hundred and eleven and it will carry it in the morning.

That wheel ran at its speed from six and a half until the light was off those boards and it did not stop at any point of it, and nobody asked him anything about any of that.
