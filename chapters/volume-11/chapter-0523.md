# Chapter 523: The Yard Asked What It Was For

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing asked, out loud, in the ordinary voice, in front of about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen on the morning of the nineteenth of the second month, at about eleven, what that sheet of paper is for, and nobody answered him for about a minute and a half, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that a yard asked a question on this morning that it has not asked on any morning before it, and entered that the answer was nobody knows, and about nine people have said since that this is the first question anybody has asked in that yard this month that nobody in it had already asked themselves.

It was the nineteenth morning of the second month and the ground under that yard was dry for the first time since about the fourth and the boards of that second table had gone from cold to warm in about half an hour, and by ten there were about nineteen people in that yard.

At about a quarter to eight a clerk of nineteen years entered, at the near end of that first table in the yard of Lot Seventeen, that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered two hundred and sixty-two days as the age of that figure, and entered that the age of a figure is a figure about the figure and that the sheet has no day-count of its own and never has had one.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that he has said those four every morning since before the middle of the sixth month and that this is the two hundred and forty-eighth of them, and that nobody in that yard has asked him a question in about nine days.

"**The board carries four hundred and twenty-one, the train on that siding has stood seven hundred and thirty-seven days, nobody has entered anything for four hundred and fifty-one, and four hundred and twelve days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

---

He asked it at about eleven at the end of that second table with his right hand flat on the boards and his left hand in its cloth, and about nineteen people heard every word of it, and the boy of about nineteen counted what he said and got fifty-four and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Twenty mornings. I have been in this yard on about nineteen of them and I have watched about nineteen people a day look at that sheet and nobody has ever said out loud what it is for. Not what it is, everybody has had a word for what it is. What it is for.**"

Nobody answered him for about a minute and a half. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a question out loud in that yard and entered the question, and entered that nobody answered it, and entered that she is not entering that nobody answered it as a finding and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, who has stood at the east end of that yard with the flat book under his left arm for about thirty years, said one thing out loud, in about four seconds, from about nine feet off, and the boy counted it and got twenty-three and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**That is the first question anybody in this yard has asked in a month that I have not already heard somebody else ask.**"

About nine people at the east end of that yard have said since that a man who keeps a tally in this district for about thirty years has now said three whole things out loud in that yard in about a fortnight, and that all three of them have been about this district not having something, and that about four of them have said that a man who has kept a tally for thirty years is the last person anybody would expect to be the one who notices a question nobody has asked. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he was not asked for it.

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in that ditch until about ten and came up and got the four figures at the near end of that first table and said the word with them four times, and read the three lines on that sheet out loud in the ordinary voice, and said one thing after it in about four seconds, and the boy counted it and got forty and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**You have all got that wrong and I am the only one in this yard who can, and I am not going to say it, and I want it entered that I was asked by nobody and I said no.**"

Nobody answered him. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man has said he is not going to say a thing and entered no reason for it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale was in that yard at about ten and said one thing out loud at about half past twelve, in about nine seconds, to about nine people, and the boy counted it and got fifty-eight and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have got a counter with a page on it and four hundred customers a week and a woman who keeps that page is the same woman who weighs their flour, and if you want to know what it is for then that is what it is for and it took me about nine minutes to write it.**"

Nobody said anything to that, and about four people near her have said since that she answered a different question from the one that was asked, and that about four other people have said that the two questions are the same question and that about four of them have said that a page on a counter is for a person who cannot read the one in a yard, and that nobody in that shop is going to be asked which of those three she meant.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was at the end of that second table and put his own mark in chalk on the edge of that table at about a quarter past eleven in the ordinary way and said nothing at all, and there are sixty-six of them in a row going back to the ninth day of the last volume. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man was four feet off a question this morning and entered that she did not ask him it and entered the reason, and about four people at that first table have said since that a man has been four feet off about nine questions this month and has not been asked one of them.

At about half past one the road keeper came up that lane, read the four figures off that wall as he went by, and was not asked about the eleven miles, and he did not stop, and about four people have said since that a question about what a sheet of paper is for went through that yard this morning and past him, and that the second time that has happened is the first time anybody has noticed it happening twice. The cart came up at about three with the near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and did not stop for anybody in that yard on that day, and about four people at that first table have said since that he has now gone up that lane on about four hundred mornings without stopping and that this month he has begun to look at the low step where a page was written out on. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given, and about four people near that wall have said since that a question asked in that yard this morning came within about nine feet of a man on those stones and went past him. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his hundred and sixty-second night of that run, that he has slept on a hundred and sixty-one of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

---

A hundred and seventy-one days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. A hundred and twenty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man. A hundred and thirty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood since the tenth of the tenth month. Two hundred and one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is three hundred and four days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is a hundred and forty days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched any of it.

Eighty marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are sixty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither of those two figures moved this morning. Two of the four figures on that wall are out and neither of them has been corrected, and there is still no figure on any page in this district for the length of the month that came before this one, and a boy asked in that yard for one of those two figures this morning and got nobody knows, and about four people at that wall have said since that a man has now been told twice about a figure of his own and has said no to a third thing today. The count of things this district has made is twelve, and a question nobody in a yard has asked before is not a thirteenth. The reading of that lot did not advance and stands at the fourth of the five, and the fourth of them is a person, and about four people at that first table have said since that a yard of about nineteen people has spent twenty mornings on a question about who made a thing and has now asked a question about what it is for and got nothing. Nobody read an entry out loud in that yard today, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and a clerk looked at it before she left and put nothing in it, and a man was asked nothing in that yard and a woman was asked nothing about the question she had actually answered. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is not paid, the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, the count is still five, and six things stand in that yard that are not a payment and none of the six is one, and a question nobody has asked before is not one of the six.

The two buckets went down that bank at half past five, empty, and came back up it full, and the woman of about fifty-eight read the three lines standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her, and about four people have said since that she has read that book about four hundred times and has never once said what is in it. After that the man of fifty-six said the four figures off that wall for the last time that day, got all four of them, and about nine people at the near end of that first table have said since that the question asked this morning was the first one anybody has asked in that yard that he could have answered out of his own four figures and did not.

A yard of about nineteen people asked this morning, out loud, what a sheet of paper on the end of a table is for, and nobody answered it for about a minute and a half, and a clerk entered that a question was asked and entered that nobody knows, and a man who has been in that ditch every morning since before the second of the twelfth month said out loud that the yard has got it wrong and that he is not going to say what the right answer is, and the sheet on the end of that second table is on it for the twenty-third morning with a ruled third line and nothing on it.
