# Chapter 506: Read Out Loud, And Not Into The Column

A clerk of nineteen years read an entry out loud in the yard of Lot Seventeen at about eleven on the morning of the second of the second month, standing at the near end of that first table with her page in her hand, in the ordinary voice, in front of about nineteen people, and the entry she read was the one about a person putting a sheet of paper on that second table and about nobody asking which person, and about four people in that yard have said since that a clerk read out the entry about not asking and not one of them has said what she read it for.

It was the second morning of the second month and the light came up hard and clean off the south end of that ground before seven and there was no wind in it at all, and the boards of that second table were warm by about nine, and by ten there were about nineteen people in that yard.

At about a quarter to eight a clerk of nineteen years entered, at the near end of that first table in the yard of Lot Seventeen, that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered two hundred and forty-five days as the age of that figure, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a figure about a sheet and that there is no day-count for the sheet.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that he has said those four every morning since before the middle of the sixth month and that this is the two hundred and thirty-first of them.

"**The board carries four hundred and four, the train on that siding has stood seven hundred and twenty days, nobody has entered anything for four hundred and thirty-four, and three hundred and ninety-five days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

---

She read it out loud at about eleven and it took about nine seconds and about nineteen people heard every word of it and nobody in that yard had ever heard a clerk read anything out loud in that yard, and the boy of about nineteen counted what she said and got fifty and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**A person put a sheet of paper on the end of the second table on the morning of the twenty-eighth of this month at about ten past eight and nobody in that yard asked that person one word about it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.**"

Nobody said anything at all. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a clerk read an entry out loud in that yard and entered the entry, and entered the time and the number of people standing there, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Then she said the rest of it out loud without putting the page down on the boards, and about nineteen people heard it, and the boy counted what she said and got eighty-seven and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I read that out loud and I am not going to put a name in that column. That column has been on that page since before the second of the twelfth month and it has never had a name in it, and if my name goes into it this morning then in a month somebody is going to ask me why I was standing in that yard on the second of the second month, and I am not going to have an answer that is a column.**"

Nobody answered her. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a clerk said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that an entry was read out loud in that yard and no name went into the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and entered that she is not going to rule a fifth column for it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The boy of about nineteen asked her, out loud, in front of about nine people, whether anybody in that yard has ever read the third line of that book out loud, and about nine people at that first table looked at each other and nobody answered him for about a minute and a half, and a clerk entered that a boy asked a question out loud in that yard and entered that there was no answer and entered that she is not going to say that there was no answer either, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing was at the end of that second table from about ten with his left hand in its cloth and heard all of it and said nothing at all, and about four people at that first table have said since that a man who has refused to read a page of that book out loud for two volumes stood about four feet off a clerk who was reading one out loud and did not look at the page in her hand.

Then he said one thing, in about four seconds, to about nine people, and the boy counted it and got fifty and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**That column has been empty since before the second of the twelfth month and about nineteen people have read something out loud in this yard since and it has been empty every morning, and I have watched it and I am not going to be the one who fills it.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man has said he is not going to be the one who fills a column and entered that she is not entering that as an offer and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve with the last of that day's flour on her hip, read those three lines in that lot book standing up, walked over to that second table and read the three lines on that sheet standing up, and asked the boy of about nineteen, out loud, in front of about nine people, which of the two of them he thinks a stranger would take, and he counted what he said and got twenty-one and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Neither. I would take the one with the stone on it and I have never seen anybody else take it either.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a woman said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a boy gave an answer to a question nobody else in that yard has answered and entered that she is not entering it as a finding, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in that ditch until about ten and came up and got the four figures at the near end of that first table and said the word with them four times, and did not stop at that second table. The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was at the end of that second table and put his own mark in chalk on the edge of that table at about a quarter past eleven in the ordinary way and said nothing at all, and there are forty-nine of them in a row going back to the ninth day of the last volume. The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally stood at the east end of that yard with the flat book under his left arm and about four people near him have said since that he moved his head about four inches to the left at the moment she started reading and did not move it again.

At about half past one the road keeper came up that lane, read the four figures off that wall as he went by, and was not asked about the eleven miles, and about nine people have said since that he comes up that lane about twice a day and reads that wall both times. The cart came up at about three with the near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and did not stop for anybody in that yard on that day, and about four people at that first table have said since that a man who puts a sheet of paper down at ten past eight does not stop for anybody at three. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his hundred and forty-fifth night of that run, that he has slept on a hundred and forty-four of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

---

A hundred and fifty-four days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. A hundred and nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man. A hundred and fourteen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood since the tenth of the tenth month. A hundred and eighty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is two hundred and eighty-seven days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is a hundred and twenty-three days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched any of it.

Sixty-three marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are forty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither of those two figures moved this morning. The count of things this district has made is twelve, and an entry read out loud in a yard is not a thirteenth. The reading of that lot did not advance and stands at the fourth of the five, and the fourth of them is a person, and nobody has read the third line of that book out loud in that yard on any day this district has a record of. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and a clerk read something out loud in that yard this morning and looked at it before she left and put nothing in it. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is not paid, the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, the count is still five, and six things stand in that yard that are not a payment and none of the six is one, and about nine people have said since that a reading done by whoever is standing in a yard is not one of the six.

Both buckets went down that bank at about half past five, empty, and came back up it full, and the woman of fifty-eight read the three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her, and the man of about fifty-six was still at that wall when she went past. After that the man of fifty-six said the four figures off that wall for the last time that day, got all four of them, and about four people at that first table have said since that nobody in that yard has asked a clerk a question about the reading and that four of them would have liked to and did not.

A clerk of nineteen years read an entry out loud in a yard in front of about nineteen people on second morning of the second month for the first time in six volumes, and the entry was about somebody not being asked, and she read it in about nine seconds and about nineteen people heard every word of it, and she said out loud that she is not going to put her own name in the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud and gave the reason and a clerk entered that reason and entered no name, and the column was ruled and empty at about six, and the third line of that sheet on the second table is a ruled line with the paper still on it on the sixth morning it has been lying there.
