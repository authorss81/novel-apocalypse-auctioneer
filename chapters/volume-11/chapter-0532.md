# Chapter 532: There Is No Figure For How Long That One Was

The boy of about nineteen said out loud, in the ordinary voice, in front of about nineteen people, at about half past eleven on the morning of the twenty-eighth of the second month, which is the last morning of that month, that nobody in that yard would be able to say at any point tomorrow how long that month had been, and a clerk of nineteen years entered that, and entered that there are now two months this district has not counted, and entered that she is not joining the two of them, and about nine people at that yard have said since that the day before a month ends is the only morning in this district on which everybody already knows the answer and nobody can say it.

It was the twenty-eighth morning of the second month and the light came up off that ground white and hard and did not soften until about two in the afternoon, and the boards of that second table were hot to the hand by about nine, and the stone lying face up on the end of it had a skin of dust on it that had not been there the day before, and by ten there were about nineteen people in that yard.

At about a quarter to eight a clerk of nineteen years entered, at the near end of that first table in the yard of Lot Seventeen, that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered two hundred and seventy-one days as the age of that figure, and entered that the age of a figure is a figure about the figure and that the sheet has no day-count of its own and never has had one.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that he has said those four every morning since before the middle of the sixth month and that this is the two hundred and fifty-seventh of them, and that about four of them have said that a man who says the same four figures in the same order every morning has never once had to be asked whether he is sure of them.

"**The board carries four hundred and thirty, the train on that siding has stood seven hundred and forty-six days, nobody has entered anything for four hundred and sixty, and four hundred and twenty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

---

He said it standing up with the boards of that first table under his own hands and nobody had asked him anything, and about nineteen people heard every word of it and the boy counted what he said and got seventy and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Tomorrow this month is over. That is not a figure, it is a morning. I have been in this yard every morning since before the start of it and I could tell you what the weather did on about nine of the mornings and I could not tell you how many mornings there were, and neither could the man at that wall, and there are about nineteen of us here.**"

Nobody answered him. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a boy said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that the thing is about a month and that a month is the second of two in this district that has not been counted, and entered that the first of those two is not on any page either, and entered that she is not joining them, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud at about a quarter to twelve, in about nine seconds, and the boy counted it and got ninety-six and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**A man in this yard has been asked what day it is about nine times in a month and has told the truth every time and not one of us has ever asked him what month it is. That is not because we are stupid. It is because nothing in this district turns on it, and a yard that has run for six volumes on four figures off a wall does not find out that it is missing a whole instrument until somebody asks for the thing it is missing rather than the thing it has.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a clerk is not entering the four figures off the wall as a finding about anybody, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally stood at the east end of that yard with the flat book under his left arm and said one thing out loud at about ten past twelve, in about nine seconds, to about nine people, and the boy counted it and got ninety-one and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have a figure in this book about a month and I am not going to say it, and I would like it entered that I decided not to, and entered why, which is that if a man says a number in a yard the morning before somebody counts a row of marks, then about nineteen people are going to be listening for a number instead of watching a hand, and I have not done that in thirty years of keeping this and I am not going to start it now.**"

Nobody said anything to that for about four seconds. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man who has kept a tally for about thirty years said a figure out loud this morning and entered that she is not entering the figure, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-one who digs loam was in that ditch until about ten and came up and got the four figures at the near end of that first table and said the word with them four times, and read the three lines on that sheet out loud in the ordinary voice, and said nothing at the end of the third one, and then went back down that bank and was in that ditch again by about half past ten. At about a quarter past two he came up the second time and said one thing out loud to about nine people at the end of that board, in about eleven seconds, and the boy counted it and got one hundred and eleven and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I cut those marks. About nine of us cut them and I cut the ones on the first morning of a month and I have cut one every month for longer than I want to say out loud, and I cut that one deeper because a month is the only thing in this yard you can feel in the wood. Nobody asked me to. Nobody has ever asked me what they are for. Tomorrow morning I will be in that ditch at the hour I am in it every morning and the mark will be there, and about nineteen of you can come and look at it or you can not.**"

About nine people at the near end of that first table have said since that a man who has said nothing at the end of the third of those three lines for a fortnight said one hundred and eighteen words about a cut in a piece of wood this afternoon, and that about four of them have said that a man who has been in a ditch every morning for about a month has just described a practice of his own that nobody in this yard knew about, and that nobody asked him why he has kept it. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that she is not entering what a mark is for, and entered that she is not entering that a clerk heard a man offer a yard a thing, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

At about half past one the road keeper came up that lane, read the four figures off that wall as he went by, and was not asked about the eleven miles, and about four people at that wall have said since that this is the last morning of a month in this district and that a man who comes up a lane twice a day has not once changed what he does on the last morning of a month or on any other. The cart came up at about three with the near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and did not stop for anybody in that yard on that day, and about four people at the end of that second table have said since that the sheet lying on it has been on it for the thirty-second morning and that nothing in this yard has a date on it. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given, and about four people within about nine feet of him have said since that a month ended tomorrow and that nobody in this district will put a figure on it and that the man on those stones is the only person in the yard who is not waiting for a number, because nobody has promised him one. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his hundred and seventy-first night of that run, that he has slept on a hundred and seventy of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

---

A hundred and eighty days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. A hundred and thirty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man. A hundred and forty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood since the tenth of the tenth month. Two hundred and ten days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is three hundred and thirteen days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is a hundred and forty-nine days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched any of it.

Eighty-nine marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are seventy-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither of those two figures moved this morning. Two of the four figures on that wall are out and neither of them has been corrected, and there is still no figure on any page in this district for the length of any month, and a boy said out loud in that yard this morning that tomorrow this month is over and that nobody in it can say how long it was. The count of things this district has made is twelve, and a man describing a practice of his own is not a thirteenth. The reading of that lot did not advance and stands at the fourth of the five, and the fourth of them is a person, and about four people at that first table have said since that the last morning of a month in this district passed exactly like every other morning of it, and that the only difference anybody could point to is that a man in a ditch cuts a deeper mark at about ten tomorrow.

Nobody read an entry out loud in that yard today, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and a clerk has entered that it is empty and that about four people said something out loud in that yard this morning and that none of the four of them has said what, and about four people said something out loud in that yard this morning and nothing went into it. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is not paid, the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, the count is still five, and six things stand in that yard that are not a payment and none of the six is one, and a man deciding out loud not to say a figure is not one of the six.

That afternoon two things were said in that yard that about nineteen people heard, and a clerk entered the shape of the two of them and entered neither of the numbers, and about four people near the end of that board have said since that a man with a book under his arm chose not to speak a figure and a man with a spade chose to speak one hundred and eighteen words, and that between the two of those this yard has everything it needs to count a month and has not yet counted one.

The buckets went down that bank empty and came back up it full, and the woman of about fifty-eight read the third line of that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her, and about four people near the top of that bank have said since that this is the evening before the last morning of a month and that she came up it at the same hour she has come up it for about four hundred working days, and that nothing about a month has ever changed her hour. After that the man of fifty-six said the four figures off that wall for the last time that day, got all four of them, and stood at that wall for about a minute afterwards without moving.

The last morning of the second month passed in the yard of Lot Seventeen the way every other morning of it has passed, and a man of about thirty-one who digs loam said out loud this afternoon that the mark he cuts on the first morning of a month is deeper than the others, and the sheet on the end of that second table is on it for the thirty-second morning with a ruled third line and nothing on it, and nobody in that yard will be able to say tomorrow how long the month that ends tonight was.
