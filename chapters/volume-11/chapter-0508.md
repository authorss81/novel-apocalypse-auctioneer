# Chapter 508: Nobody Will Hold The Column

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing asked the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, out loud, in front of about nineteen people at the east end of that yard, on the morning of the fourth of the second month, whether he would hold the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and the man of about forty-eight said no in about nine seconds and gave a reason, and nobody asked him a second question, and about four people have said since that a man who has kept a tally in this district for about thirty years said a whole sentence out loud in that yard for the first time in six volumes.

It was the fourth morning of the second month and there was a thin frost on the low step at the end of that first table that had gone off the boards before about half past seven, and by ten there were about nineteen people in that yard.

At about a quarter to eight a clerk of nineteen years entered, at the near end of that first table in the yard of Lot Seventeen, that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered two hundred and forty-seven days as the age of that figure, and entered that the age of a figure is a figure about the figure and that there is no day-count for the sheet.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that he has said those four every morning since before the middle of the sixth month and that this is the two hundred and thirty-third of them, and that nobody in that yard has asked him to hold anything.

"**The board carries four hundred and six, the train on that siding has stood seven hundred and twenty-two days, nobody has entered anything for four hundred and thirty-six, and three hundred and ninety-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

---

He asked it at about eleven and the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally did not move the flat book under his left arm and did not open it and about nineteen people heard the whole of it and the boy of about nineteen counted what he said and got eighty-two and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I am not going to hold it. You have had that column on that page since before the second of the twelfth month and about nineteen people have read something out loud in that yard since, and it has never had a name in it, and it has never had a name in it because a reading in that yard is done by whoever is standing there, and standing in a yard is not a post, and a column wants a post.**"

Nobody asked him anything else. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man was asked and refused and gave a reason, and entered that nobody asked him a second question, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Then the boy of about nineteen looked at the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing and did not ask him anything, and about four people near the east end of that yard have said since that a boy who has asked about four people in that yard this month looked at a man and did not ask him, and that the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing saw him not ask and put his right hand flat on the boards of that second table for about four seconds and took it off again.

Nobody has asked the man of fifty-six to hold that column. A clerk of nineteen years entered that nobody asked him and entered that she is not entering that on purpose and entered that she is not entering that at all, and about nine people at that first table have said since that this is the second time in eleven days that a person has been left standing next to a question and that on the first of the two times it was a boy and on this one it was the man who reads those four figures off that wall, and that nobody in that yard has said out loud whether either of them was left there on purpose.

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve with the last of that day's flour on her hip, read those three lines in that lot book standing up, and said one thing out loud to about nine people about what a man at the east end of that yard has just said, and the boy counted it and got thirty-six and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**That is the first true thing anybody has said in that yard about that column and he said it to keep it empty and not to fill it, and both of those are the same thing.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a woman said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that she is not entering it as a finding about the column and entered the reason, which is that a column is either filled or it is not and a reason is neither of those, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-four who digs loam was in that ditch until about ten and came up and got the four figures at the near end of that first table and said the word with them four times, and read the three lines on that sheet out loud in the ordinary voice and about nine people heard all of it and he said nothing at the end of the third one and went and sat down on the low step.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was at the end of that second table from about ten and put his own mark in chalk on the edge of that table at about a quarter past eleven in the ordinary way and said nothing at all, and there are fifty-one of them in a row going back to the ninth day of the last volume. A clerk entered that he was there and entered that nobody asked him whether he would hold that column either and entered the reason.

About nine people at the east end of that yard have said since that the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said those words about standing in a yard not being a post, and that the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing asked the question, and that about four of them have said since that he asked the question on purpose and about four of them have said since that he asked it so that the man would have to say it out loud in front of them, and that both of those sets of people are describing the same morning and neither of them is going to be asked which of them is right.

The woman of about thirty-three who takes in washing at the second channel was not in that yard this morning and a clerk entered that she was not in it and entered that she did not ask and entered the reason, and about four people at that first table have said since that she was in that yard on the fourth morning of this month and has not been in it on one morning since, and that about four of them have said that one morning is not a habit and that nobody in that yard has worked out what it is.

At about half past one the road keeper came up that lane, read the four figures off that wall as he went by, and was not asked about the eleven miles, and he went on up the lane without stopping. The cart came up at about three with the near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and did not stop for anybody in that yard on that day, and he did not slow down for anybody. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given, and nobody in that yard asked him whether a column is a post. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his hundred and forty-seventh night of that run, that he has slept on a hundred and forty-six of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

---

A hundred and fifty-six days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. A hundred and eleven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man. A hundred and sixteen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood since the tenth of the tenth month. A hundred and eighty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is two hundred and eighty-nine days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is a hundred and twenty-five days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched any of it.

Sixty-five marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are fifty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither of those two figures moved this morning. Two of the four figures on that wall are out and neither has been corrected, and there is still no figure on any page in this district for the length of the month it is living in now, and about nine people have said since that a wall a man reads every morning can be wrong in two directions at once. The count of things this district has made is twelve, and a refusal with a reason in it is not a thirteenth. The reading of that lot did not advance and stands at the fourth of the five, and the fourth of them is a person, and about nine people have said since that a column that wants a post has had more use in that yard this month than a book that wants a reader. Nobody read an entry out loud in that yard today, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and it is ruled and empty at about six because a man who has kept a tally in this district for about thirty years was asked to hold it this morning and said that standing in a yard is not a post, and nothing went into it.

The fifth of the five things this district does not have is not paid, the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, the count is still five, and six things stand in that yard that are not a payment and none of the six is one, and a column that wants a post is not one of the six.

Both buckets went down that bank at about half past five, empty, and came back up it full, and the woman of fifty-eight read the three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her, and about nine people have said since that she did not stop at that first table on this evening and that about four of them have noticed. After that the man of fifty-six said the four figures off that wall for the last time that day, got all four of them, and did not move away from that wall for about a minute and a half afterwards.

A column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled in that yard before the second of the twelfth month and has been named on every morning of this district for a month and it was empty this morning at about six, and on fourth morning of the second month a man who has kept a tally here for about thirty years was asked to hold it, in front of about nineteen people, and said that a reading in that yard is done by whoever is standing there and that standing in a yard is not a post and that a column wants a post, and nobody asked him a second question, and the column will be ruled and empty at about six again tonight and nobody in that yard has said a word about filling it and nobody in that yard is going to.
