# Chapter 753: The Thirty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Stranger Came Down That Lane With A Page In His Own Right Hand And Left It Face Down On The Table

The morning had gone in before he came. Four hundred and eleven is the figure on the sheet at that gatepost and it did not move, and four hundred and ninety-two days is the age of that figure. The board carries six hundred and fifty-one, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and sixty-seven days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-one days, and six hundred and forty-two days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is sixty-seven and moved this morning from sixty-six. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for seventeen mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is thirty-eight, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.

Four hundred and one days is how long that bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and fifty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and sixty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-four days back and nothing has been pulled off it. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy days past a printing it did not make, and nobody has worked out a day on which it will arrive.

Three hundred and ten marks have been cut off that board and there are two hundred and ninety-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

The man of fifty-six said the four and got them. That was the four hundred and seventy-eighth of those mornings.


The stranger came down that lane at about a quarter to nine on the thirty-seventh morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, which was earlier than the four of them who had come in fifty mornings, and he came on foot, and he had a page in his right hand and nothing in his left.

At about a quarter to eight the clerk had entered that the number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand since the sixth of the fifth month is eighteen, and that it did not move this morning. That was the rule and she had kept it for fifteen years: the figure goes in at the old value and it stands there until a person has said out loud that it changed, and never before, and no name goes in beside it, ever, and a stranger is not a resident of this district.

She had a copy of that rule in the front of her own book in her own hand. She had written it herself. She had written most of what this district believes about records.

At about ten the boy said it out loud.

"Nineteen," he said. "There's a man's come down the lane with a page in his right hand and he went back up again and nobody stopped him and he's not one of us." He counted what he had said and wrote the number in the margin of his own sheet and did not read it back to himself, which he had never done before and which Adrian noticed and did not understand.

The clerk entered nineteen. She entered that it moved this morning, that the boy said it out loud at about ten before she entered it, and that she entered no name, and that a stranger is not a resident of this district.

---

At about half past three the man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones and nothing in his hands. That is his three hundred and ninety-second night of that run and he has slept on three hundred and ninety-one of them. Nobody offered him anything. Nobody asked him anything. And there were four people at the foot of that bank looking at a stranger's page and not one of them looked at him, which is not a failing, because in nine years nobody in this yard has looked at him at about half past three.


Adrian got to the bottom of the bank at about ten past nine and there was nothing there, and then a man came down and put a page on the second table.

He did not put it on the lot book. He put it down about a foot to the near side of the book, square, with the string still tied round it, and he straightened it with the side of his hand the way you straighten a picture, and then he went back up the bank without a word and did not stop at the top of it.

The page was folded twice and tied with tarred hemp, and there was a lump of wax on the knot the size of a farthing. The fold had gone soft along one edge from being carried in a coat for a long time, and the whole thing smelled faintly of a stove that was burning something other than wood.

The man who puts tables up was at the near end of that table at about half past nine and had his own right hand flat on the near rail and watched the stranger go.

"He's not one of the four," he said.

"No."

"I've seen the other four. They're not from here but they're from *somewhere*, that was on them. This one's hands are different." He turned his hand over and looked at it, which Adrian had never seen him do. "That one hasn't held a pen for a living. That's a working hand. That's a man who does something with his hands that doesn't need a pen in them at all."

The clerk had come down the bank. She stood at the far end of the table and looked at the page without touching it.

"Don't," Adrian said.

"I wasn't going to touch it."

"You were thinking about it."

"I was thinking about whether it was a document," she said. "It isn't yet. It's a piece of paper with a string on it, and the only thing that makes it a document is somebody turning it over in front of other people and being wrong about it out loud."

"Or right about it."

"Yes," she said. "Which is worse, and which is why I'd rather we did it at the top of that bank in front of nineteen people than down here in front of four." She looked at the string. "He tied that properly. Whoever tied it did it at a desk with a lamp on it and thought about the knot."

"So he came from a building."

"He came from a building that has a fire in it," the clerk said, "and he was told to bring it down a hill and put it on a table and go away, and I'd like about nine minutes with it before it becomes a fact."

They gave her the nine minutes and Adrian stood at the far end of that table and did not touch it, and what happened in those nine minutes was that she looked at it, and refused, twice, to touch it, and then asked the man who puts tables up to turn the whole thing over without unfolding the second fold, because she wanted to see the *back* and there was a place on the back where the paper had been rubbed thin.

He turned it. There was a rectangle about the size of a hand where the fibre had gone soft and pale, at the bottom left, and a corner of it had been bent back and forth so often it had gone furry.

"That's been a corner," she said. "That's been a corner of a bundle, in a room, for a long time, and whoever's in that room has been pulling at it and putting it back and reading it, and they don't take the pages out of the bundle, they read them in the bundle. Which means the bundle has a front and a back, and this page is the front of somebody's read."

"For four hundred and eleven," Adrian said.

"For four hundred and eleven," she said. "And I have been trying to find out since I was nineteen whether four hundred and eleven is a count of people, and I put that question in writing three times, and I got back four words that added up to nothing, and it turns out that the answer has been sitting on a corner under a man's thumb for fifteen years and nobody has been the right shape to notice."

She put her hands behind her back.

"That's not an answer," she said. "That's a reason to go on asking. I'm not going to let a page that arrived on an ordinary afternoon make me stop asking, and if anybody in this yard writes four hundred and eleven on a board this morning I'll strike it out myself."

"You'd strike it out?"

"I would strike out anything I could not support," she said. "Nine years of not doing that is what this book is and I'm not proud of a line of it."

---

At about half past ten Orren came down the bank at a speed Adrian had never seen him move at, went past everybody, went to the far end of that table, and stopped.

The knot was already undone and lay in two pieces on the boards and the first fold was open. He was standing there holding the corner in two fingers with the fibrous bit turned up so it could be seen, which was exactly the thing Adrian would have done and had not thought of, and which he had done in under four seconds at seventeen.

"I've seen that mark," Orren said.

"Where."

"Coast. Eleven years ago, in a room in Brass Verdict Hall, at the back, in a corridor, on the back of the last page of a bundle." He did not look round. "There's about four thousand of those rooms in this basin. Every single one of them has a bundle in it and every single one of them has a corner somebody has been rubbing for years. That's what a room *is*, after a while. It's a place with a bundle in it."

"Orren."

"I know. I know, I know." He let go of the corner and stepped back from the table, which for him was the equivalent of a man putting down a tool very carefully because he has just heard himself. "Sorry."

"No, that's — " Adrian stopped. "Go on. It's the right question, go on."

"It's not the right question if I ask it in a yard," the boy said. "It's the right question in a room with two people in it, one of them seventeen."

---

Orren had already read it.

He was at the far end of the second table, on the other side of the open lot book, with the cut string in his hands and the two folds open, and he had been there some time.

"*Now* you turn it over," Adrian said.

"I didn't turn it over. He handed it over. It was already over when I got here." Orren's hands were careful with the wax. "You're welcome."

"I'd like to hear that in front of her."

"He was here nine minutes," the boy said. "You were up the bank counting a gatepost. You don't get to have the good version of this."

"That's fair," Adrian said.

He had started saying that about four times a day and it was getting to be a habit, and he noticed himself doing it and did not stop, because there was nothing else in that yard to do with the feeling.

He looked at the page.

It was not a lot. He had read four hundred lots and he knew one in the first half-second, the way a man who has spent his life in kitchens knows a kitchen, and this was not one. There was no object. There was no use, no toll, no bearer, no term, no release and no remedy, and there was no seventh part, and a lot without a seventh part is a piece of paper with writing on it.

It was headed:

**FIRST HOUSE — NOTICE OF A SITTING**
**CONVENED BY THE CUSTODIAN'S OFFICE UNDER THE EMERGENCY CLAUSE OF THE COMMON MEASURE**

*That clause, being the emergency provision of an unfinished settlement, has stood open for nineteen years without a guarantor of last resort, and is to be brought to a sitting. Any local market, exchange, road house, ferry, or common within the basin that intends to hold a charter of any kind after the date of that sitting is to send forward one person to be named in the public register of this basin as its bearer, together with a figure of pay, and a term.*

*No name but one. No figure but one. No term but one.*

*The sitting will be held at Alder Reach. Travel is at the cost of the sender.*

Below that, in a smaller hand and a colder one:

*It is not proposed to enquire whether the bearer can perform the office. It is proposed to record that the bearer exists and can be paid. A market which cannot pay a person cannot send one.*

The boy at the far end of the table had got to that last line before Adrian had and had stopped there, and was looking at it with his thumb on the corner of it.

"It wants a name," Orren said.

"Yes."

"It wants a name and a number and a bit of time, and it says it isn't going to check." The boy took his thumb off the corner. "My mum's name is on a list like that. Somewhere. In a building with a fire in it. And they didn't check whether she could do anything either."

Adrian sat down on the near rail, which nobody has ever sat on, and which was warm from the morning.

"Say the rest of it," he said.

"There's no rest of it." The boy turned the page round so the whole yard could see it, if there were a yard to see it, and there were four people at the foot of a bank. "That's a page. There's one more page under the string and I've not opened it because I've been waiting for somebody to say a number out loud, and nobody's said one."

---

She said one.

"Four hundred and one days," said the clerk, "is how long that bid has been open, and it was four hundred yesterday, and it is not run, and there is nothing on that page about a bid, and I would like the record to show that I looked."

"Nobody's writing that down," Adrian said.

"I know. That's why I said it out loud." She put her hand flat on the boards. "Right. Somebody say a number."

And Orren said: "Four hundred and eleven."

He said it without being asked and Adrian turned round.

"It's the one on the sheet at the gatepost," the boy said. "It's the same number. It's on the corner of that page in the small hand, bottom left, and it's not a reference, it's a *count*, and it's the same count we've got on a sheet at a gatepost forty miles from here that nobody has read in nine years." He pushed the page nine inches towards Adrian with two fingers, as though it might be hot. "There's a mark at the corner of it. That's not a signature. That's how you count paper. Somebody down there has counted how many pages have come out of that room, and ours is four hundred and eleven, and they have written the count on the corner of it in case we ask."

The clerk said, very quietly, "I have asked."

"What?"

"Three times," she said. "In nine years, on my own paper, in a column I did not show anybody. I asked in writing whether the figure on the sheet at the gatepost was a count of people or a count of sheets, because the two would be the most important sentence anyone has written in this basin in fifty years and I wanted to know." She put her hand down off the boards. "I got back four words. *The count is a count.*"

"And I never showed you that," Adrian said.

"No. You never showed me that. Because you had a list in your pocket with five things on it and one of them was where those pages are printed, and you put the list back in your pocket and came down the hill," the boy said. "I watched you do it."

He held the page out.

"That's the whole of it," he said. "I don't know whether that corner's ours or whether somebody in a room wrote something on the back of it and then wrote a count on the corner and bound it with ours by accident. Do you want it turned over here, or do you want it turned over at the top of that bank in front of nineteen people? Those are two different things and only one of them is yours."
