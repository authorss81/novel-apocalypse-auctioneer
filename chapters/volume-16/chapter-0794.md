# Chapter 794: The Seventy-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman With A Key Asked A Man Of Sixty-One In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People To Stand As The Named Bearer For A Term And He Said No Out Loud And Asked For The No To Be Entered And It Was

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and thirty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and ninety-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and eight days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twenty-two days. Six hundred and eighty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and forty-two days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and seventy-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and seventy-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and eleven days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and it is four because a man who holds a clause with no end on it nine miles up that hill has never once let a clerk enter it and refused again this morning, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and one of the four people in this yard that can be named and cannot be paid said no out loud this morning and did not make it the sixth of anything.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and eight mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and seven. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-eight mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-eight, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and there is no more in it this morning than there was yesterday morning, and there will not be more in it this evening either.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and nineteenth of those mornings, and he was in that yard for both of them and stood about eleven feet off and heard a man of sixty-one refuse something for the first time in his hearing.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and thirty-third night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirty-two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the twenty-third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of sixty-one said it first and the man of about sixty-four said it back, and neither of them referred to what he had said at eleven o'clock for the rest of the afternoon.

---

"I am going to ask him a question in this yard," said Sabra Holt, "and I am going to say the question out loud before I ask it, and I am not going to pretend that I have not rehearsed it, because I have rehearsed it for three mornings and it will not improve."

She was standing at the near end of the boards and the whole yard knew what it was for.

"I am going to ask the man on those stones whether he will stand as the named bearer for the district of Lot Seventeen for a term. That is the whole question. It has one figure in it or it has none and it has a term in it and he will tell me which, and I am going to take the answer up that hill in whatever shape it comes in."

---

"You have been here thirteen mornings," said Iven Tallow from about four feet off, without getting up.

"Thirteen."

"You rehearsed it three of them and left ten for asking, which is the right proportion and better than mine would have been." He took his hand off his knee. "Before you ask it I am going to say one thing and then you are going to ask it and then I am going to say no."

---

"I would rather you said the one thing after."

---

"No, and I would rather you let me have it, because you have been extremely careful with me for thirteen mornings and this is the one morning on which being careful is not what either of us needs."

---

"Go on."

---

"Six mornings ago I said in this yard that there is a condition on the seat and that I was not going to name it, and that if anybody asked me again I would say no out loud and would like that entered as well." He looked up at her. "I am not going to hold myself to the letter of that, because the person asking is not the person I had in mind, and I would like it noticed that I noticed that. I am still going to say no. The condition is not named and it is not going to be named this morning, and if you ask me what it is I am going to say no to that as well and I would like both of those entered, and you are the first person in seventeen years I have said two noes to in the same breath."

---

"Ask it," said Sabra Holt.

---

"Will you stand as the named bearer for the district of Lot Seventeen for a term."

---

"No."

He said it in the ordinary voice and it took less time than the question and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not move at all, which is what a thing that has been true for a long time sounds like.

---

"Enter it," said Iven Tallow.

---

"Enter what," said Mara Quill, from four feet off the end of the second table.

---

"That he was asked, in this yard, in about nineteen people's hearing, at about eleven this morning, by an appointed administrator holding the third of the nine oldest keys, whether he would stand as the named bearer for the district of Lot Seventeen for a term, and that he said no, and that he said no in the ordinary voice and did not soften it and did not give a reason when he was asked for one." He did not get up off the stones. "And enter that he asked for the asking to be entered, and enter that he asked for it before she had written anything, which she can check."

---

She wrote it. She wrote it on the page at the back of the lot book with nothing ruled on it, in her own hand, and she read it out loud before she wrote it, in the ordinary voice, standing at the near end of the boards, and it is the first thing she has entered in fifteen years on the word of a person about himself and at his own asking with a second person in the room to hear it.

"There are five lines on that page now," she said, when she had read it out, "and none of the five is in the lot book, and the number of documents this district does not own is four and has not moved, because I have not written it anywhere anybody can walk up to and read."

---

"You have not put my name in," said Iven Tallow.

---

"I have put a description of a man on stones and it is another description of a person in two books and every one of them is me. I could not have put anything less and I could not have put anything more, and if you want the count of people in this district that a page has never been able to hold, that is you and the man at that wall and a boy of seventeen and a woman with a scale, and I have had thirteen mornings in which to stop doing it and I have not."

---

Sabra Holt shut her own book and did not open it again, which is the second time in thirteen mornings that anybody in that yard had seen her do it.

"I am not going to ask you twice," she said. "I want that said in this yard in the ordinary voice so that if it turns out later that I asked you twice, one of us has been told. It is a promise and I do not make many and I have made it now."

---

"Then thank you," said Iven Tallow.

---

"It is the first time anybody in that yard has said thank you to you in thirteen mornings," said Adrian, from about nine feet off the near end of the boards, and he had not been asked and nobody had asked him to say it.

---

"Why," said the man of fifty-six, from about eleven feet off the wall.

---

"Because there is a difference between a person who will not do a thing and a person who will not do a thing and will not be asked about it twice, and this basin has been living on the first one for about seventeen years and calling it dignity."

---

The man at the end of the second table had not turned round.

"I am going to say something and it is not about him, and I am going to say it slowly and once," he said. "Two men have been refused out of that room inside seven mornings, and neither of the two refusals was made by a person. Mine is a line I put in when I was about twenty-two. His is a settlement neither of us has ever been inside and cannot get to. And in about four years somebody is going to put those two entries side by side in a piece of writing and conclude that the district of Lot Seventeen refuses everything it is asked, and it will be right, and it will be about the wrong two people, and I have got about four years to say something about that and I am starting now and I do not know how."

---

"You are starting with a thing you cannot fix," said Sabra Holt.

---

"I am starting by saying it in a yard with about nineteen people in it," said the man at the end of the second table. "That is the only part of it I have got."

---

"Then say the rest of it to me and not to them, at about half past four, and I will put it in a book and it will be one of about nine sentences that go up that hill," said Iven Tallow, "and I am not putting your name to it and I am not putting mine."

---

At about half past three the two of them said good afternoon at the foot of that wall and neither of them said anything else, and the man of about sixty-four picked up his coat at about a quarter past four and did not put it on.

"I go out at about half past four," he said, to nobody in particular, and about nine people in that yard heard it, as they had heard him say it on every morning since the middle of that winter.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about four people in that yard said *as sent* at the same time and out of step with each other and about nine did not, and the boy of seventeen wrote the morning into the box at the back of his own sheet, and under it the four figures off that wall in the order they are on the wall, because those four had been said out loud twice in a yard in front of about nineteen people and a box is not a place to leave figures nobody wrote down.

He left everything else out of it, and the leaving out was the whole of what he did with that morning.
