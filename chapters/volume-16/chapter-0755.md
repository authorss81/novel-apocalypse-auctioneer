# Chapter 755: The Thirty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Was Asked In A Yard What His Name Was And Said It Out Loud And Then The Man Of Fifty-Six Said It Back

The man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall from about a quarter to eight with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and that is his three hundred and ninety-fourth night of that run and he has slept on three hundred and ninety-three of them, and nobody in this district has asked him in nine years what the run is for or where it goes or who is on the other end of it, and the record about the not asking says not asked, and it has said not asked three hundred and ninety-four times.

The board carries six hundred and fifty-three, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and sixty-nine days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-three days, and six hundred and forty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.


He did not sleep. That was not unusual and he had stopped finding it remarkable years ago, but he noticed on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month that he had a reason, and that the reason was standing four feet off the near end of a table at the bottom of a bank waiting to be asked a question he had been dodging for four mornings.

He went up the hill at about seven and sat on the boards of that first table with his elbows on his knees and looked at the wall, which was the first time he had ever looked at that wall properly, and the four figures were on it in the clerk's hand about a foot and a half up, and underneath them in the same hand, in a line nobody had asked for, there was a date and under the date the words it was entered and nobody's name.

Six hundred and fifty-three. Nine hundred and sixty-nine. Six hundred and eighty-three. Six hundred and forty-four.

And underneath:

> *The days on that board. The days the train on that siding has stood. The days nobody has entered anything. The days from the second of January.*

*Entered at the quarter to eight and at four by M. Quill. Not corrected.*

*No one in this district has been asked what any of the four is for.*

He read that last line about nine times.

It was in her hand. She had put it there, and she had put it there without being asked, in a district where nothing is entered that a person did not have to enter, and he had been standing in that yard for fifty mornings reading the wall at arm's length and had never once read the bottom.

---

The clerk was there at about a quarter to eight and entered the morning. The man of fifty-six said the four off the wall and got them.

Adrian did not go down the bank. He stayed at the top, which was unusual enough that four people noticed and one of them said so, and by about half past nine there were about nineteen people in the yard and about nine of them were standing in a loose half-circle at the near end of that first table with their backs to the boards, facing him, which is the shape a yard makes when it has decided to have an argument.

Seth said it first, because Seth had gone up a hill yesterday for the first time in nine years and had clearly not gone back down.

"Right. I'm asking him once and I'm asking him in front of everybody, and if he says no, that's the end of it and I'll say no more about it, and Orren can put that in his book." He turned round. "Nine years. You're the only one in this yard nobody knows. That's not a complaint, that's a *situation*, and the situation's about to walk up that lane to Alder Reach and stand in a room in front of people who'll want a name, and I've watched you go grey twice this week thinking about it. So."

"Adrian," somebody said.

It was the woman who keeps a scale, from up by the bank, and she said it the way you say a thing that has been in the room for a while.

"I don't know where that came from," Adrian said. "That's not — I've never used it here."

"It's been coming out of you for two days," she said. "You've been *being* it. Go on."

---

"Adrian," he said.

The clerk's pencil did not stop, which he noticed, and which told him more about her than anything she could have said.

"No," he said. "Not yet. Because before I say it out loud in this yard I'm going to say why I haven't said it in sixteen years, and I want that in the same breath, because if I say the name and hold the reason back then this yard will get one half of it and the half they'll get is the version where I'm being brave."

Nobody in that yard moved.

"Sixteen years ago," Adrian said, "there was a charter. Not here. Four hundred miles. A public one, with about two hundred households behind it, and I was twenty-two, and I wrote the terms, and my name went on the head of it as guarantor because I was the only person anybody could find who would put his name on anything. And it worked for about two years, and then the thing it was chartering went wrong in a way nobody had priced, and forty-one people needed something that page could have given them and couldn't, because the page was written by me, and I wrote it in a clean hand, and the hand was very tidy."

He looked at his own hands on his knees.

"A year before that, the north stair went at Alder Reach platform. I was a junior inventory clerk. I was twenty-one. I wrote the evacuation order out in a clean hand because a clean hand was the only thing the crowd would obey. Eleven people came off that platform because of the words I wrote and four did not." He turned round and looked at the clerk. "I've never been able to make that sentence go anywhere nicer. That's the whole of it. There isn't a version where I'm the man who got it wrong by accident."

"So you stopped," Seth said.

"I stopped putting my name on things that had words on them," Adrian said. "Not off pages entirely — I'll sell you a piece of broken machinery and I'll put a name on it, and there'll be a figure and a description and no consequences. But anything with a *person* behind it, anything a stranger could walk up to in nine years and read and follow back to a body, I would not put my name on. Because that's exactly what happened. Somebody found that page in year four, and it had my name at the top of it, and it was correct, and it was the only correct thing about the whole business, and I couldn't take it off and I couldn't argue with it. Every time that page came up, the name came with it, and the name was doing work I hadn't consented to for nine years after I'd stopped."

"And that's why you don't sign," the woman who keeps a scale said, from up by the bank. "It's not humility. I want to be fair. It's that you don't trust a page not to walk somewhere you didn't send it."

"Yes," Adrian said.

---

"That's still a decision you've got a right to make," the man who digs loam said.

He had said one word in that yard yesterday and nothing at all this morning. He was at the far end of the second table with a spade against the leg of it and both hands on the near rail, and he said it looking at the boards of the first table rather than at anybody.

"Nobody's taking it off you," he went on. "You've spent four mornings telling us you won't do it and none of us said you had to. Say your name or don't say your name. It'll go on the page as *the man who mends fencing* if you'd rather, and that's what it'd go on as for the rest of this district's life, and any fool up that hill at Alder Reach would put it on a bit of paper exactly like that one, and it would be correct."

He turned his head.

"But if a stranger writes that down, then there's a man in Alder Reach who's been told there's a person at the bottom of this bank and doesn't know anything else about him. And that's not worse than what's there now. What's there now is a page that goes *and the man* and stops, and lets whoever's holding it fill in the rest." He took his hands off the rail. "I know what the rest gets filled in with. I've had nine years of it."

---

Adrian stood on the boards of the first table at the top of that bank at about eleven on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month with about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen, and he said it.

"Adrian Vale."

That is all. It took about a second and a half and it was not impressive and there was nothing in the yard at that moment that could have told the difference.

The first thing that happened was nothing, for about four seconds.

The second thing that happened was that the man of fifty-six said it.

"Adrian Vale," he said, and he said it plainly, the way he said the four figures off that wall twice a day, getting it right the first time, and he said it to Adrian and not to the yard, and then he said it again, because he was a man who read things out loud twice a day and he had never in his life read a thing once.

"Say them again."

"No," Adrian said.

"It's the four hundred and eightieth of those mornings," the man said, "and I have said four numbers out loud twice a day for four hundred and eighty of them and never once said the same number twice, and I would like to practise, because that is what I do instead of practising anything else."

---

Four hundred and three days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run. Three hundred and fifty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered. Three hundred and sixty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-two days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twelve marks have been cut off that board, and there are two hundred and ninety-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and the count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move, and the count of counted months is six and did not move.

Four hundred and ninety-four days is the age of the figure on the sheet at that gatepost. The figure is four hundred and eleven and did not move.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eightieth of those mornings.


Nobody wrote it down for about two minutes, and then the clerk did.

She came down the bank and went along the boards of the first table and turned the lot book round, and the lot book came up the hill in her own two hands every working morning and went back down at six, and it has five lines on it, and the fourth of them is nine words in a woman's hand, and the fifth of them is a condition with no end on it at the dictation of the man the third line is about.

She went to the far side of the last page, where there is a ruled column, and it has been ruled for fifteen years.

It is the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud.

She filled it in.

> ADRIAN VALE — FOUR FIGURES OFF THAT WALL, THIS MORNING, AND NINE LINES OF A STRANGER'S PAGE, AT ABOUT ELEVEN. AT THE DIRECTION OF THE YARD. NOT APPOINTED TO ANYTHING.

And then she closed the book, and stood with her hand flat on the cover, and said — to Adrian, in front of everybody, in a voice about as level as the wall —

"That is the first name in that column since the book was opened. I have entered fifty mornings of readings and no names. I entered *not asked* fifty times and I have never once entered a reason, and I am now entering a name, and there is no rule in this book that says I may, and I have checked twice."

"Good," Adrian said.

"It's not good. It's *fifteen years late*," she said, "and it is late because the yard let a man stand in it for fifty mornings without once opening his mouth, and I was the clerk of that yard, and I wrote the rule about not asking, and I did it because not asking was the safest thing I could think of and it turned out it was also the cruellest."

She opened the book again.

"Entered," she said, "that the count of names in the column is one, and that it was zero until this morning, and that I did not know that a person could be entered there and that nobody had ever told me, and that if there is a rule against it then the rule is not in this book, and I am not going to go and look for it."

---

Orren came up the bank at about a quarter past twelve and stood at the near end of the boards with his sheet under his arm.

"You've got a name in the book," he said. "Good. That means the book's got a person in it now, and it wasn't only me."

"Was it only you?"

"There's about nineteen people in this yard and I've got a page for every one of them that's been here more than twice," the boy said, "and there's a column in mine for who was standing next to them when they said it, and I've never filled it in for you because I didn't know what you'd want in it."

"And now?"

"Now I've got a name, so I can put it in the column and it'll mean something, and if you ever go wrong the column's the only part of that sheet that can do anything about you." He looked up. "That's the second column. The one with my mother's row in it is the first. You've never asked me about the first one and I'm not going to volunteer it, and if you ask me today I'll tell you, because you said a name out loud in a yard and a name's the thing you don't get back."

He turned and went down the bank.

The number of mornings that second table has stood at the foot of that bank is sixty-nine this morning and moved this morning from sixty-eight. The near rail has been on the near side of that table for nineteen mornings with nobody's thanks on it. The count of things this district has made is thirteen. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.
