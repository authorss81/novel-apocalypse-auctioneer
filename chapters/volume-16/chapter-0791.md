# Chapter 791: The Seventy-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Who Puts Tables Up Took A Stone Off The Second Table Because A Hollow With Nothing In It Is A Sentence And The Man At The End Of That Table Said Out Loud That He Does Not Know Where A Page Went And Does Not Want It Found

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and thirty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and five days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and nineteen days. Six hundred and eighty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-nine days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and seventy-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and eight days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and is not going to move in this weather. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and a page that was lying on those boards seventeen mornings ago is not one of the four and is not on anything, and nobody in this yard has been able to say where it went. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid and was not paid yesterday and is not paid this morning.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and five mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and four, and nobody in that yard has said a word about the count going over a hundred or a hundred and one, and the count does not care. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a man took a stone off a table this morning and did not make the fifteenth of anything. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and there is no more in it this morning than there was yesterday morning.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and sixteenth of those mornings, and at about half past eleven this morning he asked a woman with a key a question he has never asked anybody in that yard in five hundred and sixteen mornings of saying four figures.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and thirtieth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-nine of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the twentieth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and neither of them said anything else, and the whole of what has passed between those two men in twenty afternoons still fits in those two words.

---

The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up came down the eleven feet of that bank at about ten with his hands behind him, the way he comes down when he is going to look at a thing he has put there before and does not want to touch yet.

He put his hand into the hollow in the middle of the stone and left it there.

"That is a thumb," he said. "Somebody has worn a thumb in it. It is not a mark anybody made, it is a mark a hand made, and a hand is not a chisel, and whatever made that stone brought it here before the tables in this yard were the tables they are."

---

"Who put the stone there," said the man who digs loam.

"Not me and I am not going to guess and I have wanted somebody to ask me that for about seventeen mornings." He took his hand out and looked at it. "I have been putting tables up in this basin for nine years and that is the only stone I have ever come at that was already worn. Everything else I have set has got my own chisel in it and you can see whose it was by the way it runs."

---

Nobody had said the word *page* yet.

Adrian had it in his mouth and did not put it there, and after about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, he said it anyway.

"You are all standing round a stone," he said, "and every one of you has been thinking about a page, and none of you has said the word, and I have been in this yard ninety-six mornings and I have noticed that about four of the things that happen in it happen because nobody says a word first."

---

The woman who keeps a scale said the word from her own step, about forty feet off, without coming up the bank.

"Page. It went on that stone at about half past four on the fifty-eighth morning, which is seventeen mornings back, and there were nine names on it that nobody wrote there, and the ninth of them is a woman on working list forty-one two miles from this yard, and a woman with one of the oldest keys in this basin has said out loud in that yard that she is going to find her, and she has not, and I have been standing on this step for seventeen mornings waiting for one of you to say the word out loud so that the other things could start."

---

That put about nine people on the ground at once, in the way people do when a thing they have been carrying separately turns out to have been one parcel.

The man at the end of the second table came about four feet along the boards to the place where the light was, which is a thing he does, and he had not said a whole sentence out loud in that yard since the seventy-second morning, and everybody in that yard knew the count, and about four of them had been keeping it.

"I am going to say four things and I would like them entered, because I said on the seventy-second morning that I had about four years to say them and I have not started and this morning I have been made to."

---

"Go on," said Sabra Holt.

"One. I do not know where that page went. Two. I have not looked for it, and I have had seventeen mornings to look for it, and looking for it would have meant going up that lane and asking, and I did not go. Three." He put one hand flat on the boards, which is not a thing he does. "I did not look for it because I did not want it found, and I want that in a book, and I am not going to be asked why for about nine mornings, and if I am asked why before then I am going to answer."

---

"Answer it now," said the man of fifty-six. "It is a yard. It is the only place you have got."

---

"Not in that voice." He took the hand off the boards. "That is the voice a person uses when a thing has been said in a yard and wants a figure on it, and I have heard that voice four times since the seventieth morning and I have answered it three of those four and I am not going to answer the fourth one that way."

---

"Then say it your way."

---

"The page has nine names on it and the ninth of them is a woman who has never been asked anything by anybody in her life." He looked at Sabra Holt and not at Adrian. "You told this yard on the seventieth morning that you were going to ask her yourself and that you would carry the answer up that hill whether it was yes or no. That is the best thing anybody has said out loud in this yard since the seventy-first morning, and it is the reason I did not look for that page, and here is the rest of it: if I found it and gave it to you, then in about four years a room is going to be holding a document that an appointed administrator produced, and the document will have nine names on it, and the document will have arrived after the yard had already said the ninth name out loud, and every person in that room is going to believe the document was made first. A page that turns up afterwards is the worst kind there is. It is not evidence. It is a thing somebody found."

---

Sabra Holt had her book out of her own coat before he had finished the first half of it, and she did not write, which is a thing about her that about four people in that yard have learned to watch for.

"I am not going to take it," she said. "I have thought about it for the whole of what you said and I have got to the same place twice from opposite ends. If you find it, I will not carry it. If you find it, I will enter that it exists and that I was offered it and refused it, and those two lines will be worth more to that room than nine names on a piece of paper would be, because one of them can be checked and one of them cannot."

---

"You cannot be the one who finds her," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"I did not say that."

"You said you were going to ask her and I am the one who said her name out loud on the fifty-eighth morning, and you told me then and you told me in front of about nineteen people that a person who is named in a yard is owed being asked by somebody who is not the yard." Mavis Dorr had not moved off the boards. "I have had five mornings to think about whether that was a kindness and it was not, it was a way of being able to say it was not my fault, and I have not worked out what it was instead."

---

"Neither have I," said Sabra Holt, "and I am going to say the whole of it because I have about nine mornings of it left and because there is a woman with a key in this yard who has been carrying four things up a hill and refusing a fifth, and I would like the yard to understand what the fifth is."

She shut the book without opening it.

"It is a question, and I am going to ask it, and I am going to ask it her and not this yard, and I am not going to say anything else about it in this yard until I have asked it. That is all. That is the fifth thing and it is not a name and it is not carried and I am telling you the shape of it because I promised in this yard that the two of those would be the same thing when I said them up there."

---

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight, and got them, and had gone on doing it every morning since the middle of that winter without ever once asking anybody what any of it was for, and this morning at about half past eight, with about nineteen people in that yard and a woman with a key standing four feet off the stone, he asked.

"What happens to that wall."

---

Sabra Holt did not answer it straight away, and the thing she was doing while she did not answer it was the thing she had spent ten mornings doing, which was deciding in front of about nineteen people whether she was going to say a thing she could not carry.

"I am going to answer you exactly," she said. "I do not know. There is a clause nine miles up that hill that is the oldest thing in this basin and it is held by a person and not by the building and not by the room, and I have never read it and I have asked to read it and been refused in writing, and what I can tell you about that wall is that it is not on it. Nothing in that room knows those four figures are yours. Nothing in that room knows you are the reason they are legible. If your district is asked to produce something at that sitting, and it cannot, and somebody in that room wants a way of saying *this district produces nothing*, then a wall that four figures come off twice a morning for nothing is going to be the sentence they reach for, and I would not be in the room when they reach for it and I would not be able to stop them."

---

"You could put my name on it," said the man of fifty-six.

---

"I could put your name on anything," said Sabra Holt. "That is what a key is. That is the whole of what a key has ever been and it is why I have not put your name on anything in eleven mornings, and I would like the yard to understand that the two refusals are the same refusal and that the second one costs you more."

---

"I would like it said out loud in this yard that I asked her," he said, "and what I asked, and that she told me the truth and it was not a good answer. I have stood at that wall for five hundred and sixteen mornings and read four numbers to whoever wanted them and not one person has ever asked me what any of it is for, and a woman with one of the oldest keys in this basin just told me the answer and it was *they will use you as the sentence*, and she did not soften it, and I am not able to say a thank you that would mean anything, so I am going to say this instead: whatever happens to that wall, it was mine for every morning of it and nobody took it off me."

---

She wrote it in her own book in the ordinary voice, with the figures in it and the morning in it, and she read it out before she wrote it, and about four people in that yard heard the words *it was mine for every morning of it* go into a second book nine miles from the first one.

---

At about half twelve the man who puts tables up picked the stone up off the second table, and it was heavier than about four people expected, and he put it inside his coat against his ribs the way a man puts something he has decided not to leave.

"I am taking it down the bank and I am not telling anybody where I am putting it," he said, "because if I say where I am putting it then somebody will put a thing on it, and that is how the last one happened, and I am not going to be the reason there is another hollow in it."

---

The man of fifty-six put his hand on the stone the way he does, and there was nothing on the stone, because it had gone down the bank in a coat four minutes before, and his hand came down onto bare board and stayed there.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and he had read them that morning for the five hundred and sixteenth time and had been in that yard for both of them and had asked one question out of the half-dozen he had been given over two mornings.

Orren Pike wrote the morning in the box on the back of his own sheet, and ruled a space under it, and left it empty, and did not write the question, because the box is headed for mornings on which a thing was said out loud in this yard and was not entered anywhere, and that is not what happened to it.

He looked at the blank line for a while and then wrote *seventy-five* at the foot of it in pencil, which was the morning and not the thing the box was for, and which he knew was not the thing the box was for.

---

At about six the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall on his way down the lane to nobody at all, and got all four of them, and the stone that had been on the second table was about four miles off in a coat against a man's ribs and was not on anything.
