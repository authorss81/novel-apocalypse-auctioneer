# Chapter 759: The Forty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Who Digs Loam Was Asked Whether He Would Put His Name In A Book And Said He Would Give It To A Man And Not To A Page

Four hundred and ninety-eight days is the age of the figure on the sheet at that gatepost. The figure is four hundred and eleven and did not move. The board carries six hundred and fifty-seven, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-three days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-seven days, and six hundred and forty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is seventy-three and moved this morning from seventy-two. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for twenty-three mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-three, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.

Four hundred and seven days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing was proposed about closing it. Three hundred and sixty-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and sixty-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and forty days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-six days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and sixteen marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The count of counted months is six and did not move.


Adrian was on the top of that bank before a quarter to seven, which was earlier than he had ever been, and from up there he could see that the man at the far end of the second table was already sitting on the rail with the spade across his knees instead of against the leg of it, and that he did not look up when Adrian came down.

Adrian stood about nine feet off the near end, which is where he stops, and thought about not doing this, and then did it badly, which is at least a different failure.

"Nobody's taken the line off it."

"I know." The man who digs loam turned the spade over. "I've been down here since a quarter to seven waiting for one of you lot to come and tell me you'd taken it off it, so you can get on with saying something else, and nobody's come, and now you've come and said the opposite, and I'd like to understand what you're at."

"I'm at the fifth line."

"You're at the ninth line of a stranger's page," the man said. "Go on then. You've had four mornings on that page. Say the thing you've come down the hill to say."

Adrian said the thing he had come down the hill to say, which was that a standing offer of labour with no end and no term and no pay and no way of stopping it was the single worst instrument he had ever read, and that he had read a great many, and that he had made the eighth one himself once.

"You want to take it off," the man said.

"I want it *ended*. There's a difference and it's the only difference I've got. Not struck — nothing gets struck. Ended. Termed. 'So long as he agrees to it and says so on the day and can be paid something.' Because the moment there's a person attached to that line who can be asked, it's a bargain. At the moment it's a *fetch*, and a fetch doesn't have anybody in it."

"Right," the man said. "And when the line's ended, what's got four men up that bank in fifty mornings?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing," the man agreed. "It'll be nothing and I *know* it's nothing, because I'm the one it fetches. You know how I know? Because I've been standing in this yard every morning for nine years waiting for somebody to need a man with nobody, and it hasn't happened once, and every single time it did happen it was because a stranger read a line off a page."

He put the spade down on the boards and sat on the edge of his own table, which is the first time in fifty mornings he had sat on it.

"Now do the other half of it," he said. "Do the part where you work out what I am without that line in the book. Go on. I'll wait."

Adrian had an answer and it took him about nine seconds to find it and about four seconds to know it was the wrong one.

"You're a man of about thirty-one who digs loam," he said, "and you've no household, and there's no instrument in this district that can put a figure against a person who isn't in one, so as things stand you're not a labourer and you're not a bearer and you're not a witness and you're not a security, and you're not any of the seven parts of anything, because you can't be entered anywhere."

"Aye."

"And if that line goes, you're not even that."

"I'm *nothing*," the man said. "Say it. It's easier when somebody else says it." He turned his hands over and looked at them, the way Seth had on the thirty-seventh morning. "That's what I've been for fifteen years and I want you to understand what it costs to want to keep it. Everyone in this yard thinks I'm standing down here because I'm patient. I'm standing down here because I'm the only thing in this district that anybody can get hold of. Take the line off that book and I'm a man with no household and no name and no trade on a page and nothing that says I'm real, and that's a man who's missing, and I've seen what happens to them — they send for you about six weeks after you stop being anything and then it's out past the gateposts and then it's a figure on a sheet."

"You'd rather be fetched."

"I'd rather be *findable*," the man said, "which is not the same and is better, and it's the only thing I've ever wanted, and I got it by writing down one line in a book fifteen years ago, when I was about twenty-two, and nobody asked me why a man with no household wanted a standing offer of work with no end on it." He looked up. "Which is what I've been waiting nine years for somebody to ask me."

---

The yard filled up at about ten and Adrian had told nobody else and had to tell them now, because nineteen people cannot stand near the bottom of a bank for two hours without it coming out.

He told it badly. He told it in the wrong order, with the fetch in front of the finding, and Orren stopped him in the middle of it.

"You've got it upside down," the boy said.

"I haven't—"

"You've told four hundred people about a bad line before you've told them what it *is* for." Orren was standing on the boards with his sheet under his arm and he had the flat, patient look of a boy who has watched adults get this wrong for four years. "You started with the fetch and everybody's already decided it's a horror, and now nobody's listening, and you're going to get up there in a minute and take the line off that book and you've already lost."

"I'm not going to take it off it."

"You're going to try to end it," the boy said, "and that's the same thing with better manners, and in about six weeks it'll be ended, and he'll be fetched by nothing at all, and you'll have done it out of the best intentions in the basin and it'll be the worst thing anybody's done here in nine years."

"Then what do I do?"

"You ask him," Orren said. "Not me. Not the clerk. Him. You've spent four mornings asking everybody else in this yard questions about themselves and you haven't asked him one, and he's stood at the end of that table for nine years being the answer to everybody's question about records."

---

Adrian went down the bank and asked him.

Not in a yard. There were four people at the bottom of it and about nineteen at the top and Orren had been right about that, and Adrian could feel the shape of what a yard would do to a question like this, which was flatten it into a vote.

He stood at the near end of that table and said, "Can I ask you one thing in here, and you can say no to all of it and I'll go and say in a yard that you said no?"

The man who digs loam looked at him for a while.

"That's the first time anybody's offered me that in nine years."

"It's been a strange week."

"Aye." He shifted his hands on the rail. "Go on then."

"What was it for."

"You know what it was for. She said it out loud yesterday, at about three, in front of four of us, and one of them was me, and I have been sitting at the end of this table thinking about it ever since."

"That's not what I asked."

The man laughed, which Adrian had not expected and did not enjoy, because it was not a good laugh and it was not aimed at him.

"No," the man said. "It isn't." He looked at the boards at the top of the bank and then at the lot book lying face up with five lines on it. "You've never asked me what I said no to."

"No."

"Nine years, and I've said no once, and nobody has asked me what it was to." He put his hand flat on the near rail. "And I'm not going to tell you, because you're a stranger with a name you gave us four mornings ago and you'll be gone in a year. And that's not a hard thing to say out loud to a man who reads pages for a living. It's the only reason I'm able to say it at all."

"Fair."

"It's not *fair*, it's *mine*," the man said. "But aye."

---

"What I want," the man said, after a bit, "is my name."

Adrian did not understand for about two seconds, and then he understood completely, and what he felt about it was not what he had expected, which was a small clean satisfaction.

"No," Adrian said.

"You haven't—"

"You've not heard the rest of it." The man took his hands off the rail. "I've got no household. I've got no name on anything in this district, and I've had a line in a book for fifteen years that says *a man with nobody*, and it's the only sentence in the basin that has ever mentioned me. So I want to say it out loud, once, in a room, to a person — and I want it to be you, and not that table, because that table's the thing that's been sending men up that hill since I was about twenty-two."

"You want me to hear it."

"I want somebody to *have* it," he said. "That's the whole of it. There's a difference and it's the only difference I've got and I've had nine years to work it out. You can have it. Not there."

---

The yard had about nineteen people in it by ten past twelve and Adrian came up the bank with nothing written down, and Orren read his face from about six feet away and gave him a very small nod, which was not approval, it was recognition.

He did not do it at the top of the bank, and he had thought about doing it at the top of the bank for three mornings.

He did it about four feet off the near end of that second table at about half past twelve, with the sun behind him, in front of four people and a dog that belongs to nobody, which is not a yard and was never going to be a yard, and he looked at Adrian for about a second and a half first.

Adrian did not write it down.

He stood there and made himself not write it down, and it was the hardest thing he had done in nine years, harder than the sentence about the clean hand, because the whole of his life is a set of instruments that put things in books so that they could not be lost, and he stood four feet off a set of bare boards with a pencil in his own pocket and let a man's name go out of the world with his mouth open, on the grounds that a name a person handed to a person is a thing a person did, and a name in a book is a thing a district has.

He did not tell the yard either. He went back up the bank and sat down on the boards and did not say one word about it, and about nine people watched him not say it and worked out for themselves what had happened at the bottom, because it is the only thing that has ever happened at the bottom of that bank.

Orren followed him up. He stood at the near end of the boards for about four seconds with his sheet under his arm and then he said:

"You've given it to him."

"Yes."

"Right." The boy wrote something in the margin and then, before he wrote it, he stopped, and Adrian watched him stop, and understood about four seconds later why. "No. I'm not writing that. I've been hunting for about four years for a row with a word where a person's supposed to be, and you've just been handed one, and if I write it down in this next half minute then I am exactly the thing I've been saying I won't be, which is a man with a sheet."

He put the sheet down on the boards, face down.

"I'm going to leave that alone," he said, "and I'd like it noted that I left it alone, and I'd like the noticing to cost you something, and I don't think I'm going to be able to tell you what."

Then he picked the sheet up and went and sat down on the step of the first table about nine feet away, where he could not see Adrian's hands, and stayed there for about an hour.

Neither of them said anything else for that hour, and Adrian sat on the boards with his hands in his lap and found that the hardest part of not writing a thing down is not the not writing, it is the hour afterwards with nothing to do with your hands.

Adrian went down the bank at about one and told the clerk, because somebody had to and because he had worked out by then that she would rather have it late and entered than never and not entered.

"You are telling me that a man said his name to you at the bottom of that bank," she said, "and that you did not write it down."

"Yes."

"Entered," she said, and wrote it, and wrote fast, which she never did. "Entered that a person said a thing out loud at the foot of that bank on the forty-third morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, and that four people heard it, and that no name is entered, and that the clerk was not present and is entering this from the account of a man who says he is able to be held to it. Entered that he asked me not to write it and that I am writing it anyway. Entered that I asked him why and he said because a clerk is the last place a thing goes to be safe."

"That last part's not what he said."

"It is exactly what he said," the clerk said, "and I am the clerk of this district, and if a man does not know that I know what a clerk does then he has not been in this yard as long as I have."

She put her pencil down and looked at the page for a while, and then she said the thing she had come down the bank to say, which she had said out loud in a yard four mornings earlier and had not been asked to repeat.

"That was the fifth of the five things this district does not have. A way to pay a person who is not in a household. It did not pay him. And the count of the five is still five."

And she said, without looking up, and in the ordinary voice she used for figures:

"And there is now a man in this district who has given his name to one person and to no page, and no stranger coming up this lane is going to be able to find him by reading it, and that is the first time anything in this book has ever worked the way it says it works."

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands. That was his three hundred and ninety-eighth night of that run and he had slept on three hundred and ninety-seven of them, and nobody in this district has asked him in nine years what the run is for or where it goes or who is on the other end of it, and he had now gone three hundred and ninety-eight nights of a run without being given one thing and without being asked one thing, and the count of nights is a count and not a figure behind which anything was bought or owed. About four people within twenty feet of that wall have said since that a man in this yard gave his name to a person this morning and would not give it to a page, and that the man at the bottom of the wall has been giving nothing to anybody for three hundred and ninety-eight nights, and that nobody has come down that bank to ask him for it, and that this is now the only arrangement in the district where a man keeps a thing by never being asked for it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-fourth of those mornings, and he was not asked about any of the four.
