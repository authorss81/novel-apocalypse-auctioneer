# Chapter 773: The Fifty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Sixty-One Gave The Clerk Four Things To Enter And She Entered Two Of Them And Said What She Would Need

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it did not move. Five hundred and twelve days is the age of that figure, and it is the age of nothing that can be added to.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-one, and the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and eighty-seven days, and nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and one days, and six hundred and sixty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty-one days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and seventy-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and that figure has been wrong for that length of time and there is nobody in this district who is permitted to write the right one beside it.

Three hundred and eighty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and fifty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and sixteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and did not move, and a page is on its way up that lane. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move, and the number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and it is four and not five, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank eighty-seven mornings and moved this morning from eighty-six. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for thirty-seven mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is fifty-seven, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a man sat four feet off the near end of that table yesterday afternoon and was described out loud by a person who has read every fair copy of that lot in this basin twice a year since before it was opened, and describing a thing is not a thing anybody in this yard made. The lane count is twenty-one, and a man came up that hill yesterday with a leather case and no page in his hands at all, and that is not the thing the count is for and everybody in this yard knew it before he got to the bottom of the lane.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and ninety-eighth of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twelfth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and eleven of them, and a man of sixty-one sat down on the stones about four feet off him at about three and had not said one word in that first half hour, and about nine people in a yard at the top of a bank stood and watched two men not speak to each other and none of them came down.

---

He was at the near end of the second table at about nine with the leather case open on the boards and four things written on a sheet of his own, in a small square hand, and he did not offer the sheet and did not read from it.

"Four things," he said. "I am going to say them out loud and you are going to do whatever you do with four things a person says out loud in a yard, and I am going to stand here while you do it, and I am not going to help you and I am not going to correct you."

Mara Quill came down the bank.

"I do not enter things that people say out loud."

"You enter things that people say out loud," he said. "You have entered a great many of them in eleven mornings, including a figure of forty coppers a man cannot pay, a want you were not allowed to enter, and a no that a man entered against himself. I have read the entries. I did not come up that hill to be handled by a clerk and I am not going to stand here and be handled by a good one and pretend they are different."

---

"The four things," she said. "Say them."

"The first: there is a clause. It is called the founder's clause and it is in a building nine miles up that hill and it is the oldest clause in this basin and it is the reason a market may not own a bus, a lamp, a ferry or a bell."

"That is not a thing in this district."

"That is the first thing and it is not a thing in this district," Iven Tallow agreed. "The second: it is held. By a person, alive, in that building, and not by the building and not by the room and not by the record, and it has never once been held by anything else."

"And the third."

"The third: it has no end on it." He said it the way a man reads a figure off a wall. "There is no term on the founder's clause. There has never been a term on it. It goes on until it is ended, and the only way to end it is for the holder to end it, and the holder has been me since before that lot was opened, and I have not ended it."

The clerk's pencil was in her hand about four inches off the page.

"And the fourth."

"The fourth is that it was written by me," said Iven Tallow. "I wrote it. In about nine months. And I am telling you the fourth one last because it is the one that changes what the other three are, and I wanted to give you a chance to stop me at the first one and I am aware that that was a piece of stagecraft and I am sixty-one and I am not going to pretend it was anything else."

---

She entered one.

"I will enter that a man came up this lane and said out loud in this yard that a founder's clause exists and that it has no end on it and that he wrote it," she said. "I will not enter that it is held. I will not enter by whom, and I will not enter the building, and here is why and I am going to say all of it so that you can put it in your own book."

"Go on."

"A clause that is held is a document, and a document that is held is a thing this district does not own, and I have four of those already and I am not making it five by writing down a man in a yard." She put her pencil down. "And a clause with no end on it is a condition with no end on it, and there are four of those, and if I enter yours it will be five, and I would then be a woman who has entered a fifth condition into a district that has four, and in about four years somebody will use that to say that a district with five conditions is a district that cannot be relied on, and it will be my hand on the page."

---

Iven Tallow looked at her for a while.

"That is the best answer I have had in about seventeen years," he said, "and I am including four hundred of them."

"Do not do that."

"I am not flattering you. I am telling you what I heard." He closed the case. "Now. What would you need."

She looked up from the book.

"What do you mean."

"You have said no three times in about four minutes and you have not said what would make it a yes, and I have spent seventeen years in rooms where the answer to a refusal is the price, and I am asking you for the price because I have the money and you do not."

---

Mara Quill shut the book.

"I would need to own one page," she said. "That is the whole of it. Not to have the clause. To own one page that says it exists. That page would be mine and not a fair copy and not a copy of a copy, and it would come up that lane in a hand I could compare with any other hand, and it would be on a page and not in a room, and I would be able to send it to about four households in this basin and ask four people whether they had seen it, and if a stranger came down that lane with a page in his hand saying a clause exists then I could hold up a piece of paper and say so do I, and that is not much, and it is the entire of what I have wanted since you sat down."

Iven Tallow put both hands flat on the boards.

"I will send it up that lane," he said. "Not this afternoon. I will send it in three or four days, because I have to get it out of a room in a building and I would rather do that while there is a man in this yard who has told about nine people I was in it."

"Then it will be a page in this district and not a fair copy," said the clerk, "and the number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four, and I am not counting yours, and I would like you to know that I decided that without being asked and that I would have decided it anyway."

"I would expect nothing else from you," said Iven Tallow, "and of the four rooms I have watched a frightened clerk get that wrong in, three are still standing."

---

He went down the bank at about half past three and Adrian watched him do it and did not follow, and every other person at the top of that bank decided, separately and without saying so, that they had better not either.

The stones are at the foot of a low wall about nine feet to the near side of the eleven feet of bank, and a man of about sixty-four has been sitting on them at about half past three every afternoon for nine years with his coat folded beside him, and he now had a man of sixty-one sitting down about four feet off him, and they had not said anything at all for the first half hour.

"You will be the one that's been in the room."

"Yes."

"Then I have nothing to say to you and I have said it."

"All right," said Iven Tallow.

The man of about sixty-four did not look at him. He had his hands on his knees and he was looking at about two miles of nothing through a gap in a hedge, and the sun was behind the far side of it and would be another hour before it was worth walking.

"Nine years," he said. "I have been on these stones since I was about fifty-five, and before that I was somewhere else every night for eleven years, and I worked out in the last eleven days that the whole of my life is that other people went to a place and came back and nobody ever came out and asked me what was there."

"You do not have to answer me."

"I am aware of that," said the man of about sixty-four. "I have been aware of it since about the fortieth morning and I have not said one word to anybody, and I have had a clerk's line about me that I did not ask for, and I have found out since that it is in her book and not in that one, and a man in that yard asked me once in front of about nineteen people whether I wanted to be findable, and he wanted something out of me when he did it, and you have not wanted anything yet, and that is the only reason I am going to let you sit here."

They did not say anything else that afternoon. About four people came down the bank in the last hour of the light and stood about nine feet off and did not come closer, and the turner who comes up that lane twice a day and is not one of anybody's came through at about five, read the wall without stopping, looked at two men sitting on some stones at the bottom of a bank, put his head on one side, and went on up the hill.

---

Then he looked up the bank, at Adrian, who had been standing about nine feet off the near end of the boards since about ten with a pencil in his hand that he had not used.

"You have not asked me a single question," he said. "I have been reading about you since before I got to the bottom of this lane and you have not asked me one, and I have spoken to about nineteen people in this yard today and I do not think any of them has asked me one."

"I have four," Adrian said. "I have had them since about ten and I have not asked any of them because I did not want to be the man who asked the first one."

"That is not a virtue. That is a delay with a good coat on." He picked the case up. "Come up the lane with me. Not far. About nine hundred yards. There is nothing at the top of it but I do not want this said in a room and I do not want it said under a wall either, because a wall in this district has four figures on it and I have no intention of being responsible for a fifth thing a person in this yard has to say to a stone."

---

They went up it. There was nothing to see, which was the point, and at about nine hundred yards Adrian stopped and turned round and asked the first of his four.

"What did you write it for."

"To stop anybody owning them," said Iven Tallow.

"Who is them."

"About four thousand rooms," he said, "and the rights they have in a bus and a lamp and a ferry and a bell and a piece of road, and the whole of the design of it was that not one of those could be held by a single person, including me. There is not one of them in this basin now that can be owned by a market, or by anybody else, and there is a man nine miles up that hill who says that is because of what I did, and he is right, and I have never once corrected him."

"And you are going to end it."

"No."

---

"You said you have no end on it and you have held it for seventeen years and you are sixty-one."

"I am sixty-one," Iven Tallow agreed. "And that is not the reason. If it were the reason I would have ended it and gone and done something else, and I have thought about that very hard on about nine mornings this month."

"Then what is the reason."

"That is the second of your four," he said. "You said you had four and you asked the first one out of order, which I notice, because I used to do it myself, and I want you to know that I noticed."

---

They came back down the bank at about ten past four and the man of fifty-six was at the wall saying the four figures out loud in the ordinary voice to about nine people, and Iven Tallow stopped on the boards and listened to the end of it, and did not go down, and Adrian watched a man who had written a rule in about nine months stand in a yard and listen to a man of fifty-six do four numbers twice a day.

"What did he say to you up there," said the man at the end of the second table, when the yard had emptied down to about four people.

"That he wrote a thing called the founder's clause and that it was to stop anybody owning a bus or a lamp or a ferry or a bell."

"Aye," the man said. "Then he wants something."

"He wants to be asked about it in a yard by somebody who is not him."

"That is not a thing," the man said. "That is a hearing. There are about nineteen people in this yard and every one of them is a person he has decided something about, and he is going to stand down there and answer them, and it is going to look exactly like a question from this bank and it is not one, and you are the man standing on this bank who cannot tell the difference at the moment, because you have been doing the same thing all morning with a different coat on."

Adrian did not answer that, and the man watched him not answer it, and neither of them said anything else about it for three mornings.
