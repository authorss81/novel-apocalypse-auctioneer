# Chapter 764: The Forty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Called Kest Came Down That Lane With A Waybill In His Hand And Told A Yard Full Of People That The Failure Was Not In The Room And That The Boy With The Page Had Been Ruled Out Of The One Place He Was Needed

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. Five hundred and three days is the age of that figure. The board carries six hundred and sixty-two, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-eight days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and ninety-two days, and six hundred and fifty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twelve days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run. Three hundred and sixty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and seventy-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and forty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and forty-five days back, and no night has been named, and a body four hundred miles off is three hundred and eighty-one days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twenty-one marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are three hundred and seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move, and the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and the number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move, and the number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and was not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank seventy-eight mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for twenty-eight mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-eight, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and it did not move, and a man made a waybill entry on a road this afternoon and it is not one of the fourteen, and a thing a person did on a road is a thing a person did.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-ninth of those mornings, and he looked to his right at about a quarter past four and there was nobody on the stones, and he stood there longer than he ever had, and then went home, and told nobody that he had.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundred and third night of that run, having slept on four hundred and two of them, and the clerk had a page of her own with one line on it about him and no line in the lot book, and he did not know either of those things and had asked her for the first one not to happen.

---

He came down that lane at about eleven on foot with a board about the length of a door under one arm and a folded paper in his other hand, and he was about forty-three and built like a man who has spent a life getting off and onto things and getting under them, and there was a grease stain on the thumb of his right hand that had been there long enough to be part of him, and he had a voice that crossed a yard without appearing to try, and he came into that yard and looked at the top of the bank the way a man looks at a junction.

Nobody stopped him. It had become a rule in that yard in the space of a week, and Adrian could not tell whether that was a good thing or the most frightening one, and a man of forty-three said, before anybody had said anything at all:

"Right. Which of you lot sent something up a road yesterday afternoon."

---

"Nineteen," said Orren, from the boards.

"Twenty," said the man. "There's a man comes down that lane with a page in his hand and yesterday there was one of those, and the boy in the blue coat has told the room before anybody wrote it, which is the first time in about nine years that has happened on this network and I want him to know that." He turned round. "I keep the ways. Every road, every turning, every house face and every gate, and I keep a waybill for anything that moves on one of them with a hand on it. Lot Seventeen isn't on my sheet. This bank isn't on my sheet. I have never had a piece of paper for either."

"There's a road," Adrian said.

"There's a lane, and it comes off the eleventh mile, and the eleventh mile is four miles from anything and it doesn't go anywhere, and it has got you on the end of it and no one else." He held the paper up. "And yesterday afternoon at about half past four there was one piece going up that lane in the hand of a man with a cart, and I did not have a waybill for it, because there is no such thing, and I found out about it at six this morning when the carrier I sent up after it came back and said there was a woman at the top of a hill who had read it out loud to about nineteen people, and I said a carrier does not stop for that, and he said she read it out loud and then she stood in a yard and did not say one word about it for the rest of the morning."

He put the paper down on the boards.

"There's a turner comes up that lane twice a day and reads your wall and goes straight through," he said. "He is not one of mine. He has never been one of mine. I do not have him, I have never had him, and I have never had either of you, and I would like that put in whatever you lot call a book, because in about nine years somebody with a lamp is going to come down this lane and ask me who is on it and I am going to have to say that I have been looking at this bank from the top of a road since before that second table came down it."

"That's the first thing anybody has said to me about this yard that I have not been able to answer in one sentence," Adrian said, "and the yard says I have been in it two months and the yard is wrong about a great many things but it is not wrong about that one."

"You've not answered it in one sentence because it's not a question about you," said Kest. "Now. Which of you lot sent something up a road yesterday afternoon."

---

The clerk entered the lane count at about a quarter past eleven, on the word of a boy of seventeen, said out loud at about eleven before she entered it, and she entered twenty and entered no name, and entered the reason, and the reason was that a man whose name she had been given by another man and had not been entered by him had come down that lane with a paper in his hand, and that a carrier is not a resident of this district.

"Call him Kest," the man said, when she had finished. "It's on the board if you want it. I'm not being coy about it. I'm the keeper of the ways and I've got about two hundred men who'll have to be able to find me, and if I don't say it out loud they can't use it, and I've never in my life had a paper that said I was not a person because I had not asked to be one."

"Then it goes in," the clerk said, and wrote it, and Adrian watched a rule that had held for fifteen years get argued out of existence before anybody had finished sitting down, by a man who had never heard of it, and understood that the rule had never once been defended and had only ever been obeyed.

---

They had it out on the boards from about half past eleven until about two, and the yard stood about and listened, and the argument was the best one Adrian had been in since he came down that hill, and it was the first one in ten mornings in which two people who both knew what they were talking about refused each other.

"The room," Adrian said. "Room Four. There's a press in it and a woman cutting pages and a man counting corners, and that's where a page is made, and a page that gets made in a room is a thing somebody chose, and the whole of the last ten mornings has been about a district that has no instrument. If there were an instrument here, the room would have to answer us, and it wouldn't have to be got hold of on a road."

"Right," said Tarin Kest. "And what happens to the eleventh mile."

"Nothing. It's a road."

"There's no such thing." He said it without heat. "Everything that moves on a road either arrives somewhere or it doesn't, and the ones that don't are the ones you spend your life on, and I have spent mine, and I will tell you exactly what the eleventh mile is, and then you can tell me it's a road."

He put the board down flat on the boards of the first table and put one finger about two-thirds of the way along it.

"There's a turning at this end. Then a culvert about half a mile down. Then a mile and a half with nothing on either side of it and not a house and not a wall and not a gate and not a counter and not a man. Then a rise, and then eleven years of road, and at the end of it a building with a fire in it." He took his hand off. "Everything else on that run has got somewhere on it you could put a thing that went missing. A gate, a door, a woman at a window, a boy on a cart who counts carts. The eleventh mile has nothing. I have lost eleven bundles on that mile in nine years and not one of them was reported, because there was nobody on it to report to, and a thing that is lost on a piece of road with nothing on it is not lost. It's *finished*. It stops being anybody's while it's still in the world."

"Who lost them."

"The First House," said Tarin Kest, "and the people who pay them, and about forty households who sent a thing up a road to be counted and never heard what it counted to. And I have known that for nine years, and I have never said it out loud in a yard before, and I am saying it because your boy has a page and I have just seen him fill a column in and I have been alone with that for nine years."

---

Orren had come down the bank about four steps and was standing there with the sheet against his chest and his mouth slightly open and had the expression Adrian had seen on him twice in a fortnight, which is a boy watching a thing he believed in be measured against a thing he had not known about.

"You've lost them," the boy said. "Nine years, and you've lost eleven."

"I've lost the count of them."

"That's a count," the boy said. "You said a count."

"So I have."

"And nobody has a page for any of them, and I have never counted one, and I count things that stop," the boy said, and then stopped, and Adrian watched him arrive at it, and watched him not be able to get past it. "The eleventh mile is a place where things stop and there's no one to write down that they stopped, and I have got four columns and a rule that says nobody can enter them, and I have just been told that this is the only place in the basin where any of it would have mattered."

---

Mara Quill did not answer him at once, and Adrian understood that she was not refusing, which was worse.

"The rule is mine," she said. "I wrote it on the forty-fourth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month and a boy argued me into it and I think it is probably right. Any person in that yard may write, on any paper they are carrying, who was standing near them when they said a thing out loud. And no such paper is to be entered in this book by anybody, including me."

"That's correct," Orren said.

"It is correct and it is the reason your page is worth nothing on the eleventh mile," she said, "and I would like it noticed that I have known that since about a quarter past eleven this morning and I have not said a word, and that the reason I have not said a word is that I did not think of it until he said it, and that is the first time in fifteen years that a person has found a hole in something I did and I did not find it first, and I am not going to pretend to have been waiting for it."

She closed the book.

"It was not a rule about the yard," she said. "I wrote a rule about the yard and I have been treating it as a rule about the world since the moment I put it in, and there are about four thousand rooms in this basin and eleven miles of road where nobody is standing, and if the only page in the district that could answer for a thing happening on one of those miles may not be entered anywhere, then a thing that happens on one of those miles is not a thing that happened to anybody."

She said it in the ordinary voice she used for figures, and about nine people in that yard understood at the same time that she had said the true thing, and that she had said it about her own instrument, and that nobody in the district could do anything with it tonight. About four of them looked at Adrian to see whether he was going to try, and he did not, and that was the correct thing and it cost him the whole of the rest of the afternoon.

---

At about half past three Adrian walked up the lane about nine hundred yards with Tarin Kest, who wanted to see the turn, and there was nothing to see, and on the way back Adrian said the thing he had been carrying since the fortieth morning, and he said it well, and he said it in the ordinary voice, and he did not ask.

"There's a man comes up that lane at about three every morning with a cart that pulls to one side," he said. "Nine years. He has a near wheel that drags and he gets it nine inches off the ground with one arm and goes straight up the lane and has never once stopped at the top of it. On the fortieth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, which is nine mornings back and which is the only day of the last ten that this yard has been about a person, I asked him I asked him to stop so that a boy could write down what time it was, and he said no, and he told me why, and it was the best thing anybody has said to me since I came down that hill."

"Did he."

"He said a man standing still is a man somebody chose to put in a place." Adrian heard how it sounded and kept going. "And then he said, when the next one comes down that lane with a page in their hand, tell him I was here and I said nothing."

Kest had stopped walking.

"Tell me his name."

"He hasn't got one in this district and he hasn't asked for one and I am not going to invent one for him," Adrian said, "and I am telling you because he asked me to tell you, and he asked me to tell you in a yard and I am telling you on a road, and I did not think about the difference until we were two hundred yards back down it."

"You didn't ask him."

"No."

"Right," said Tarin Kest, and took the folded paper out of his coat and opened it on the flat of the waybill board, and wrote one line on it in a small square hand, and Adrian watched him do it and understood, far too late and for the second time in two days, exactly what he had done.

"It is a carrier's entry," the man said, folding it up and putting it away. "It says that a man went up this lane yesterday at about half past four with a piece of paper, and that he was not in a house, and that he said nothing. It does not say where he was. It does not say who he is. It does not say he was *there*. I want you to understand the difference, because I have been the only person in this basin who has ever written one and I did not learn it from a book, I learned it from a man at a turning who did it wrong once and got nine families told a thing that was not so."

"You've written him on a page."

"I have written a road, a direction, an hour and a fact about a wheel," said Tarin Kest. "And if anybody ever asks me about that man I will read it out and it will be true and it will not be him, and that is the whole art and I have been doing it alone for about nineteen years and I would like somebody in this district to know that it can be done, and I would like it noticed that it took me nineteen years and a bad mistake and it has taken a boy of seventeen eleven months and a coat."

---

The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a man made a waybill entry on a road and it is not one of the fourteen, and a thing a person did on a road is a thing a person did.

The lane count is twenty and it moved this morning at about a quarter past eleven, entered after a boy of seventeen said it out loud at about eleven and not before, and a name went in beside it for the first time in fifteen years, and the name is Kest, and he asked for it.

The count of the five is still five and is not paid. There is a man of forty-three in a yard at the top of a bank who runs every road in this basin and has just told it that everything it has built in ten mornings is not admissible on the one stretch of road where any of it would have mattered, and the boy who built it is seventeen and has not said a word.
