# Chapter 769: The Fifty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Who Kept A Scale Read Out Loud In A Yard The Only Copy Of A Note A Man Took In A Corridor And The Fourth Of Its Four Lines Was A Name And Nobody Was Allowed To Enter It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. Five hundred and eight days is the age of that figure. The board carries six hundred and sixty-seven, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and eighty-three days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and ninety-seven days, and six hundred and fifty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and seventeen days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and seventy-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and seventy-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and forty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty days back and nothing has been pulled off it. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and eighty-six days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twenty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twelve marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four and it did not move, and a woman with a scale has had three on a counter for two years and one of them came down that lane in a coat with somebody else's name inside it, and that is a fifth and it is not counted, and she is right. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four, and it did not move, and the count of the four did not move either, and the man who put his no in the book about nine mornings ago is watching a woman not be entered and has not said one word about it. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and it did not move, and a man at the end of a table put four lines in a margin yesterday afternoon and a margin is not a fifth condition and is not a fifth anything.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank eighty-three mornings and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for thirty-three mornings and nobody is to thank anybody for either of them. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and it did not move, and it does not move on a thing one person did, and this morning one person read four lines out loud on a board and it is still fourteen. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is fifty-three, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and ninety-fourth of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundred and eighth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seven of them, and a clerk's own book has a line in it about him and it is the only line in that book about a person, and the lot book has nothing, and he has been asked one question in four hundred and eight nights.

---

The note had been in Adrian's coat for eight mornings and he had not unfolded it, and the reason he had not unfolded it was that it was the only copy, and a man of about fifty had stood at the top of that lane and said *it's the only copy* and then had said *I'd like it read out in that yard by somebody with no interest in it, and then I'd like it sent back*, and had gone up the hill without waiting to find out whether anybody would do either.

"I'm not reading it," Adrian said, at about ten.

"You said that on the forty-ninth morning."

"I'm saying it again. I sent a page up that lane and I wrote nine coppers on it and I have been a party to a thing in this yard for twelve mornings running, and a man in a corridor took this out of a room and it is the only copy, and if I stand up there and read it then it is a man's name in my mouth, and I do not know anything about him and I have already got one name in a column and I did not put it there."

"Then who," said Orren.

That is how it happened. A boy of seventeen stood on the boards of the first table at about a quarter to eleven with a folded page in somebody else's hand and asked a yard of about nineteen people who in this district had no interest in a page, and it took about forty seconds and it was the first question in twelve mornings that everybody in that yard answered at once.

---

The woman who keeps a scale was standing on her own step with a weight on the door.

"I'd like to say something first," she said, "and I'd like to say it before I say yes, because after I say yes I'm a person in a yard and I have been in this yard four times in nine years and I am not going to be able to be half in it."

"Go on."

"There's three pages on that counter and one of them came down that lane about two years ago in a coat with somebody else's name inside it, and I've never told a living soul whose it is, and I have never once been asked, and I asked to be asked about those three pages on the forty-first morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month and about nine of you lot were too busy." She came out from behind the counter, which she has not done while a customer is in, and there was no customer. "So I have a page in a coat on my own counter that I can't give anybody and can't say where it came from, and I have had it about two years, and that is the whole reason I know how to read one of these without taking it apart."

"That's not no interest," Adrian said.

"It is *precisely* no interest," the woman said. "I don't want anything out of that note. Everybody in this yard wants something out of every piece of paper that comes up that hill, including the boy, including her, and you most of all. I keep a scale. I weigh things. Somebody hands me a thing and I say what it weighs and I don't ask what's in it, and that is the only trade I have and I have never been asked to use it in a yard before."

---

She read it at about one, standing on the boards of the first table at the top of that bank, with about nineteen people in that yard and the sun behind her, and Adrian stood nine feet off the near end with his hands in his pockets and did not help and did not interrupt, which is the hardest thing he has done since he came down that hill.

It was four lines and there was no heading and no room on it and no name at the bottom, and she read it the way she would have read a weight out, which is to say without putting anything of her own into it.

> *1. A claim sent up from a market on the eighth mile. Eleven times. No answer sent back. Nobody came up.*
> *2. A condition with an end on it that nobody came to end, because the person it was about was not in the room when it was read out.*
> *3. A name in a working list, indexed four times over: by a word, by a household, by a name that is a colour of cloth, and by nothing. Ask any of the four. All four will say they sent nothing.*
> *4. Nell Cray. Working list forty-one. Ask the market that sent her and you will be told she was never there. I have looked. She was not on a bundle. She was on a list that a man made in a corridor while he was waiting for a bundle, and I do not know what that is, and I have not got a way of writing down that I do not know, so I have written it here, on a page a man took out of a corridor, and I am carrying it down a hill because I do not know what else to do with it and I have carried things for a long time.*

She got all of it. There was nothing in it to get wrong.

And the yard was very quiet, and the woman who keeps a scale was the only person in it who did not know what the fourth line was, and for a moment nobody in that yard understood why a woman who had just read four lines out loud without stopping did not know what the fourth one was, and then about nine people understood at once and looked at the woman of fifty-eight, and the woman of fifty-eight did not look at anybody.

---

The woman of fifty-eight was standing at the top of that bank and she had got there just before the fourth line, which nobody in the yard noticed except the boy, and she said, in a voice that carried about nine feet and not much further:

"Read that one again."

"I read it exactly as it's written."

"I know you did. Read it again."

And the woman who keeps a scale read the fourth line again, and the yard had it a second time, and that was the last time a name that is not on a page in this district was ever said out loud twice in the same yard by two people.

---

"Do not write it down," the woman of fifty-eight said.

She said it to Mara Quill, and the clerk had her pencil about four inches off the page, and Adrian understood about a second and a half later that the woman had spoken to the same person about the same kind of thing on the forty-sixth morning, at the foot of a low wall, and had lost.

"You are not going to write it down," the woman of fifty-eight said. "I know that. I worked it out on the fortieth morning and I have known it since and I have not said one word about it for ten mornings, and I am saying it now because I have just heard my own sentence read out in a yard in a hand a man wrote in a corridor, and it says *ask the market that sent her*, and there is no market here that sent her, and I have carried for her about twice a week for nine years and I have never once sent her anywhere."

Somebody at the back said, "Then the note is wrong."

"The note is not wrong," the woman of fifty-eight said. "The note is the first accurate thing anybody in this basin has had about her in nine years, and it is accurate in the way that only a thing nobody can check is accurate, and that is the sort of accuracy I have spent my life on, and I would thank the man for it if I knew what he looks like."

---

"Entered," said the clerk, and her voice was not steady and she let it not be steady, which Adrian had not heard her do. "Entered that a note was read out in this yard at about one on the fifty-third morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month by a person in this district, and that the note had four lines, and that no name goes in beside it, and that I am entering no name of the reader either, and here is why, and I am going to say it in a yard and not on a page, because a page would be me deciding it and a yard is the district."

She put the pencil down.

"There is a column in the lot book for the name of whoever read a thing out loud," she said. "It had nothing in it for fifteen years. It has one name in it now and the name is Adrian Vale and it went in on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month because a boy of seventeen stood on those boards and asked me to look for a place to put a name, and I looked, and there was one place, and I have not been able to think of a reason that is not that one since."

She put her hand flat on the boards, which she never does.

"And I am not putting a second one in beside it," she said. "Not because it would be two. Because whoever is in that column now is in it for having been asked to fill it, and the man who reads the four figures off that wall twice a morning has been in this district for nine years and has not been in that column once, and I have never once offered, and he is the only person in this yard I have ever been frightened of not offering it to. That is the real reason and it took me four days to get down to it, and I would like the yard to notice that I got down to it."

---

"The heading says *whoever read a thing out loud*," Orren said.

He had come down off the boards and was standing about four feet off the step and he had the sheet under his arm and he was seventeen and he was not being clever, he was being exact, and Adrian understood in about two seconds that the boy was about to cost the district the cleanest thing it had done in twelve mornings and that he would do it because he was right.

"The heading does not say whoever was asked," the boy said. "It says whoever read a thing out loud, and the one name in that column is in it because somebody stood on those boards and asked for a place to put a name, and I was that somebody, and I would like to own that in a yard. And a woman stood on this board at about one and read four lines out loud in front of about nineteen people, and she has read more things out loud in this yard this month than Adrian Vale has read in two years, and she is not on a page, and that is not a difference of kind. That is a difference of who asked."

He shifted the sheet.

"And it is not the woman's fault and it is not yours," he said. "It is mine. I put him in. I asked you to look for a column and you found one that had been ruled and empty for fifteen years, and neither of us looked at the heading, and I have read that heading every morning for four years."

Nobody said anything, and a dog that belongs to nobody went across the top of the bank and did not stop for anybody.

"I know," said the clerk. "I have known it since before you stood up. I have been the clerk of that yard for fifteen years and I ruled that column when I was nineteen and I wrote the heading on it in the same afternoon, and I had not read that heading properly since the day I ruled it until this morning at about a quarter to eight, and I have not been able to put it down since, and I would like it understood that a boy of seventeen has now found it in a yard before I found it in a book."

"Then you'll not enter her," the woman who keeps a scale said.

"No."

"Right," the woman said. "Then I want to say what I want said about that, and I want it said in a yard and not on a page, and then I'll come off the boards."

---

She said it standing where she was, and it was about thirty words, and it is a sentence Adrian Vale is going to be asked about one morning, and did not have to write down anywhere.

"A thing weighed is a thing known," she said. "That is the only thing a scale does and it is worth more than everything else in this yard put together, and it is not worth it because the weight is right. It is worth it because afterwards two people know it, and they know the same number, and neither of them has to trust the other one. And this district has spent nine years with a wall and a boy and a clerk and a man who digs loam, and all four of you have spent nine years being careful about who was standing nearby. I have been behind a counter for nine years weighing about nineteen people's fruit, and I can tell you that the reason a weight works is that it does not care who is standing near it. It works the same in an empty room. And that is the whole of it and you can all go and be careful at each other for another nine years and I will go back and shut my shop."

She got off the boards.

"And I am not going to be a name in a column," she said, "so somebody had better find out what the man who wrote that wants, because he wrote four lines and the fourth one is a name and he carried it down a hill, and he did not do that for a woman with a scale, and I would like to know what it was for before he comes back up that lane and asks."

---

The page went up the lane at about half past four in the hands of the man with the cart, who had not been asked to and who said yes before anybody had finished asking, and who then stood at the top of that bank afterwards, looking up a lane with nothing coming down it, for long enough that the woman who keeps a scale asked him whether he was expecting somebody.

The woman of fifty-eight went down that bank at about five with her bag on her arm, and the woman who keeps a scale went with her as far as the foot of it and then turned back, and what the woman of fifty-eight said at the top of the lane, with her back to about four people who had followed her out, was this:

"I have not gone to a person in nine years. People come to me. I am going to go to one tonight and I am going to tell her that a man she has never met wrote her name in a corridor and that I have said it out loud in a yard, and I am not going to be able to make that sound better than it is, and I am going to do it anyway, and if any of you lot come up that lane after me I will know and I will not be grateful."

Then she went up the lane, and nobody followed her, and the count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and there is a note in a corridor in a building in Alder Reach with a name on it that has now been said out loud twice in a yard in this district and is on no page, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and it is not paid, and a woman with a scale read a page out loud at about one and is on no page, and a boy of seventeen noticed it and was right and the yard did nothing about it.
