# Chapter 782: The Sixty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Thirty-Eight And A Woman Of Thirty-Four Went Up That Lane To Argue About A Valve And A Woman With A Page Was Standing At The Top Of The Bank When They Came Down It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and a woman with a page stood about four feet off it yesterday morning and did not look at it. Nobody in that yard has ever been able to put that figure beside another one. Five hundred and twenty-one days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-six days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and ten days. Six hundred and seventy-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and eighty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-nine days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four, and a page went up that lane in a cart at about half past four two mornings ago and it did not make a fifth. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid and has not been paid on any morning this book has run.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-six mornings and moved this morning from ninety-five. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-six mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-six, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered, and the man who keeps a tally said it at a quarter to eight this morning, which is the time he says it, and he entered it at a quarter past nine, which is the time he enters it, and the two have differed by an hour and a half on every other morning of this book, and he entered no reason for either, as he enters no reason for any of them. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-two and did not move this morning and moves only when one comes down and a boy says it out loud first, and nobody has come down. The lot book is on the boards at the top of that bank with five lines on it, and four of those five have no end on them, and the fifth is a man.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and seventh of those mornings, and this morning he said them to a woman with a page as well as to the yard, because she was standing about four feet off the wall at a quarter to eight and had not asked and he did not notice.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-first night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eleventh afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and she said it first this time, and neither of them has mentioned it since.

---

They went up the lane at about half past eight because there was nowhere else in that yard a man and a clerk could stand for half an hour without the whole of it being worked out inside a quarter of an hour, and because the woman with a page was in the yard and the yard had four things in it now that could be asked about, and Adrian Vale did not want any of the four to be him.

"You have not said no," said Mara Quill.

"I have not said yes either."

"I have been keeping both of those in order since the sixty-third morning and I have run out of mornings to keep them in," she said. "Three mornings. A man of sixty-one offered you a seat with an end on it in a yard in front of about nineteen people, in the ordinary voice, and he asked for the withholding to be entered because he has spent forty years giving the whole of the terms away, and you said you had not said no, and then you said why, and the why was that a closing needs two and a closing has to be entered and we were standing in a yard and nothing had closed. And a man of forty-three came down that bank and told you what you were doing, and he was right, and you have not done it since."

---

"The seat is a valve," said Adrian.

He had been walking about nine hundred yards of it before he started and the words came out at the place where the lane bends and where a person can be heard from the bottom of the bank if the wind is in the wrong quarter, which on that morning it was not.

"Say that again in a different voice," said Mara Quill, "and tell me what a valve is for, because you have used that word once in this yard and you have used it as though it were a thing on a machine and not a thing on a person."

---

"A valve is a way of stopping one bad thing happening to about four thousand rooms at once," he said. "You do not get that in an ordinary argument. You get it in an ordinary week in a ward with a bad week in it, and a bad week in the next ward, and the only thing that has ever kept this basin from emptying out is that there has always been one person who could put a hand on it for a day without asking anybody. That is not me. That is not a man's character. That is a shape in a settlement that anybody can put their hand in, and the day there is no shape in it, the day it is four thousand people each deciding on their own whether this is a bad week, is the day this basin empties out and nobody writes it down."

"Say that slower," said Mara Quill, "and notice what you have just built."

"I have built a case."

"You have built a case with a person in it and the person is you, and you have built it in the third person, and you have done that in front of me on a lane where the wind is in the wrong quarter." She had stopped walking. "Adrian. A valve that anybody can put their hand in is a hand. You have been in that yard eighty-seven mornings and every findable person in it became findable because of a thing you said out loud, in front of people, and about fourteen of them were not findable before you started and one of them is a man of sixty-one who is now the fifteenth and says he does not know whether that is a rescue or a different room. And you have just described a shape that one hand can be put into, and you have described it as though the hand were not a man."

---

"Then give it a term," he said.

It came out of him in the voice he uses for things that are a good idea, and he heard the shape of it about a second afterwards, and he did not take it back, and he had four days to decide whether to take it back and he did not take it back on any of them.

"Give the seat a term," he said. "Give it a year. Give it two. Then the hand is on it for a year and comes off, and the coming off is the part everybody in this basin is frightened of and I am not frightened of it, because I have watched a man in that yard keep a thing going past its end for four years and I would rather it stopped while somebody was still counting. And if the man on the stones says no at the end of the year then it comes off and nobody has been governed and the four thousand rooms have had a year of somebody's judgement, which is a great deal better than no hand at all for a fortnight in a bad month."

---

Mara Quill did not answer for about as long as it takes a man to put a board down and pick it up again.

"You have just offered to be the hand," she said.

"I have offered a shape with a year on it."

"You have offered a shape with a year on it and you are the only man in this basin who could put his hand in it, and you have offered it to a yard, at about nine in the morning, in the third person, on a lane." She started walking again and then stopped again. "And I am not going to give you the comfort of the argument about consent, because the consent is the part you are right about and I have been agreeing with you about it since the sixtieth morning and it has not helped you once. A term that is not renewed by fresh consent is not a term, it is a habit, and I have watched this district make a habit out of one man reading four numbers off a wall and I am not going to watch it do it again with a seat."

---

"What would you have," said Adrian. "Say the whole of it, because we have been doing this for three mornings and I have never once got the end of it."

"I would have no hand at all, and I would have the yard, and I know exactly how that sounds at nine in the morning and I have said it out loud in front of about nineteen people before and I will say it again." She looked at the ground on the lane for a while. "And I will tell you the part I have not said to anybody, and you may do what you like with it, and the reason I am saying it now is that you have just been honest with me and there is a thing I have not said to you in a run of mornings and I am going to say it now. If there is no hand then when the bad week comes there is no hand, and the thing I was read about at the bottom of a bank seven mornings ago is about four hundred people going without bread in a winter. I am not going to pretend that is a small thing. I have never seen it happen. I have only had it in a man's own mouth for seven mornings. I know what I am asking for. I am asking for a worse decade on purpose, because a worse decade is visible, and a hand is not."

---

That was the end of the argument and it went on for the last nine hundred yards of the lane without either of them raising her voice or his, and when they got to the top they stood about a minute in the flat nothing above the top of that bank and did not go down it.

"I am going to say the thing," said Adrian.

"Say it."

"I am going to offer to go up that lane on my own. To the room. Alone, on whatever morning they name, and read the terms myself, and come back down, and say what it costs." He was looking at the hill and not at her. "That is the valve. That is the shape with a year on it, and it does not need a Council and it does not need a market and it does not need a man of sixty-one to die first, and I have thought about it on about nine walks since the fifty-ninth morning and it is the only version of it I cannot argue with."

---

"You are not going to do that," said Mara Quill.

"I have not said—"

"You are going to not do it, and I want to be careful with you about why, because I have got this wrong twice in a fortnight." She turned round and faced him. "It is not that I don't trust you. You are the only person in that yard who has never once made a page go anywhere that he could not follow it to, and if anybody in this district walked up that lane alone and read the terms, it should be you, and I have known that since about the fortieth morning. The reason is that you have not offered it to the yard. You have offered it to me, at nine in the morning, on a lane, which is a room with one person in it and a hedge."

---

"That is what a room is," said Adrian.

"I know what a room is. I ruled a column at nineteen in one." She did not soften it and she did not stop either. "Seven mornings ago a man told you at the bottom of a wall that in about four years you will be in a position to be the only one, and that you will not be able to ask the yard, and that there will be nobody in the room to ask. You have carried that sentence around for seven mornings and you have not once said it in that yard, and this morning you have described a shape with a year on it to me on a lane, and I do not think those are the same man, and I would like to be wrong about that."

---

They came down the bank at about ten and Sabra Holt was standing at the top of it with her page under her arm, and she had been there long enough that the ground on the near side of her boots was dry where the dew had been lifted off it.

"How much of that did I get," she said.

"Most of it."

"All of the parts where you said the word *market* and one of you meant the other one." She did not unroll the page. "I have been an administrator for nineteen years and the two things I have learned are that a district always knows who its market is and never says so out loud, and that the moment a district has to say it out loud, the district discovers it has two of them and no rule for which one is which. I am not here to make anybody choose. I would like that understood before anybody starts choosing."

---

"Nobody in this yard has ever been asked which of you two is the market," said Orren Pike, from the boards.

"I know. I have read the whole of what is in the front of that book and there is a clerk and a lot book and a column with one name in it, and there is a man of thirty-eight whose name is in that column and who is not the clerk." She looked up at the boards. "So I am asking it, and I am asking it in a yard, out loud, in front of about nineteen people, and I am not asking it because I think the answer is a secret. I am asking it because I have to be able to write one line about this district that is true, and I cannot write a line about a district that does not know what it is."

---

"Nobody," said Adrian.

He said it in the ordinary voice and about four people in that yard heard it as a piece of modesty and about nine heard it as a piece of something else, and the boy of seventeen heard both and wrote nothing down, which was the first time in four years he had heard a word offered in that yard in place of a figure and not written it down.

---

"Then write that," said Sabra Holt, "and I will carry it up the hill and it will be true, and I want you to understand what it will be worth there. A district with no market cannot pay a bearer, because there is nothing to pay out of. A district with no market cannot give fresh consent, because consent is a thing people give to a body and there is no body. And a district with no market is not a district that a sitting can put a clause in front of, and that sitting does not care which of those three of yours is true."

She got the page out from under her arm and looked at it and did not unroll it.

"That is the ninth line of your notice arriving in a yard as a sentence," she said, "and I have watched it happen four times and twice it was the other half of the sentence that did the damage and twice it was that nobody would say the first half either. You have just said the first half. I am not going to tell you which half of that is the worse one, because I have been in nineteen years of this and I have watched about four hundred people find out and I have never once been able to predict it."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them, and about nine people in that yard, who had spent the morning being one half of a market, found they had nothing in their mouths to say and said *as sent* instead, because they had been saying it for a fortnight. Nobody asked him anything. He went home. Halfway down the lane he said the four of them again to nobody at all, which is a thing he has done most mornings for five hundred and seven mornings, and it is the only arithmetic in this district that has never been wrong and has never once been asked what it is for.
