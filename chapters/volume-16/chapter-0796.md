# Chapter 796: The Eightieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Who Is On Working List Forty-One In A Corridor Nine Miles Up That Hill Walked Two Miles Down A Lane And Up A Bank To Say No Out Loud In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People And Nobody Had Asked Her To Come And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and thirty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and ninety-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and ten days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twenty-four days. Six hundred and eighty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and forty-four days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and seventy-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and seventy-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and thirteen days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and there is one more piece of paper in this district this morning than there were yesterday, and it is not one of the four and it is not the district's and it has not left the district. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a woman walked two miles this morning to say no out loud to being paid, which is not the same as saying no out loud to being asked, and the difference between those two is the whole of what she came to say.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and ten mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and nine. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for sixty mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is eighty, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-two and did not move, and it does not move for anything that comes up that lane, and it has not moved since the sixty-fifth morning, and it does not move because a person is not a page and a page is not a person and the only direction that lane has ever counted is down. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and there is no more in it this morning than there was yesterday morning.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and twenty-first of those mornings, and at about half past two this morning a woman he has never seen before came up that bank and stood on the boards and he had to stop in the middle of the second one, and then he said the rest, and he got the rest.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and thirty-fifth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirty-four of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the twenty-fifth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and nine feet of bank and a whole yard above the two of them, nobody in that yard was thinking about either of them, which is a thing that had not happened before and both of them noticed.

---

Sabra Holt had a folded paper in her own hand on the boards at about ten and about four people in that yard had come up the bank to look at it from a distance.

"I asked her," she said. "In writing, as a Council and not as a kindness, as I said in that yard ten mornings ago I would. Two mornings ago. She has not written back and I did not expect her to write back and I would have been frightened of a reply in her own hand, because a reply in her own hand is a page and a page a Council puts in a room is a document with a person at the bottom of it."

---

"Has she been told what she would be asked," said the man at the end of the second table.

---

"Exactly. I have written her four sentences and the fourth one is that she may say no and that a no will be carried up that hill in her own words and that nothing will be carried up about her if she says nothing. I have not written her what I think her answer will be and I have not asked anybody in this yard what they think her answer will be, and if anybody in this yard has an opinion I would like it to be that I did not ask them for it."

---

"It was not going to be yes," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"I know it was not."

---

"She is on working list forty-one." Mavis Dorr had not moved off the boards. "She has been on it since before I could read it. It is a corridor with a door and about nine names a week go in and out of it and there is a woman at that end who has never once been told anything about any of them. I said her name out loud in that yard twenty-two mornings ago and I told you the price before I said it, and the price was that anybody who heard a name said in a yard can be asked about it afterwards, and I have had twenty-two mornings to decide whether that was a thing I did or a thing I gave away, and it was a thing I gave away, and I gave it away for nothing, because nobody used it for twenty-two mornings."

---

"Until about ten mornings ago," said Sabra Holt.

---

"Until about ten mornings ago," said Mavis Dorr, and stopped.

---

She came up the lane at about half past two in the middle of the afternoon, on foot, and she was a woman of about fifty with her sleeves turned back and mud on both boots and she had walked two miles because there is no cart that will go up that lane for a person who is not carrying anything to sell.

Orren Pike said the lane figure out loud from the boards before the clerk's hand had moved, the way he has said it every time a stranger has come down that hill with a page, and this morning there was no stranger and no page and nothing came down that lane.

He said twenty-two, and then he said *twenty-two does not move*, in the yard, out loud, so that everybody in it would know he had noticed.

---

"I am Nell Cray," she said, at the top of the eleven feet, to about nineteen people. "I hold the sixth of the nine coppers. I have been on working list forty-one for nine years and I have never once been asked anything by anybody in this district, and I have walked two miles down a lane and up a bank because a woman with a key wrote me four sentences and put them in an envelope and asked me to be here at about two, and I am here."

---

"You were not obliged," said Sabra Holt.

---

"I was not, and I want that said first, in that yard, before anybody else gets a word in." Nell Cray put her hands flat on the boards of the first table, which is a thing people do in that yard and which she had clearly not seen anybody do. "I have had nine years of people handing me things. I have never once been asked what I thought about any of it, and when somebody finally asked me, in writing, what I thought, in four sentences, I said no, and then I sat with it for two mornings, and then I walked here."

---

"You said no in the envelope."

---

"I said no in a sentence in a letter. That is not a no. A sentence in a letter is a thing you can hand round a room and nobody in it has to look at your face while they read it." She turned round. "So here it is to about nineteen people, in the open, with the sun on it: no. I will not be the person your district puts forward. I will not stand in a room for a term. I will not be paid for it, and I am not saying that as a virtue, I am saying it because I have been on a list for nine years and a list has paid for everything I have ever done and I am not going to have my name be a second thing at the bottom of me."

---

"Who says you cannot be paid," said Adrian.

---

"Every morning since the ninth year I have been on that list." She said it without any weight on it at all, which is a thing she has learned off the same people Sabra Holt learned it off. "That list is not generous and it is not cruel and it is nine names a week and it does not pay anybody enough and it pays them the same, and it has never once asked any of us what we are worth, and that is why I can say no so easily. Nobody has ever put a figure on me. There is nothing here to pay out of."

---

"Then I have got to say the hard half," said Nell Cray, before anybody could, "and I have never said it out loud and I have practised it on the road and I am going to say it now and then I am going to sit down if there is anywhere to sit."

---

"Go on," said Sabra Holt.

---

"Your woman told your yard that this district has nine holders of nine coppers. That is not true and I am one of the nine and I can see the other eight are not here." She looked at the boards and not at anybody. "The nine of us are on a list in a corridor. That list holds us. Nothing in this district holds us. There are nine people in this yard whose names were spoken out loud in it and there are nine people who are not in a household and are on a list, and every one of the nine is already being paid by something, which is the list, which is not a market and does not know what a lot is and has never once asked whether we would rather be paid by something we could see."

---

The yard was extremely quiet and the man of fifty-six, about eleven feet off the wall, said nothing at all.

"So the fifth of the five is not a shape," said Nell Cray. "It is nine people, and the reason none of them can be paid is not that there is no money. It is that all nine of us already are, by something that has no figures and no end on it and no clerk, and none of the nine of us has ever been asked whether that counts. And I am not going to stand in a room and be told I represent this district when I do not know whether I represent a copper or a corridor."

---

"That is the fifth of the five said out loud by one of the five," said Sabra Holt, "and it is the first time in seven mornings that anybody has said it as a fact about a person and not as a sentence about a place, and I am not going to write it down, and I want the yard to hear me say that I am not going to write it down."

---

"You are going to write it down," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"I am going to write down that a woman said no and gave the reason in her own words," said Sabra Holt. "Her reason is hers. I am not going to take it and put it in my own words and hand it to a room, because the second it is in my words it is a document with a Council's hand on it and about four people in this basin have watched that happen and it cost one of them a district."

---

The clerk came down the bank and stood at the near end of the boards and put the lot book open on its own cover and wrote a line, and read it out before she wrote it, and there were six lines on the page at the back with nothing ruled on it now and none of the six was in the lot book.

Then the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about four and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet who had been standing in the same place since about half past two heard them and none of them could afterwards have said whether they had heard a woman say no or four figures, in that order, which is the order it happened in.

---

At about five Nell Cray went back down the eleven feet and up the lane at the pace she had come at, and the lane count is twenty-two, and did not move, and does not move for anybody going up that hill, and there is nobody in that district who has ever proposed that it should.

Orren Pike did not use the box on the back of his own sheet at all, because a thing had been said out loud in that yard and entered, and the box is headed for the mornings when it is not, and this was not one of those.

Then he wrote at the bottom of the page, in pencil, without ruling a line for it: *she came up. nobody sent her up. twenty-two does not move.*
