# Chapter 758: The Forty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Who Kept A Scale Asked To Be Asked About Three Pages And A Clerk Of This District Found Out She Had Written The Fifth Line

The morning had been unremarkable in a way Adrian was learning to value. Four hundred and ninety-seven days is the age of the figure on the sheet at that gatepost. The figure is four hundred and eleven. The board carries six hundred and fifty-six, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-two days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-six days, and six hundred and forty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is seventy-two and moved this morning from seventy-one. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for twenty-two mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-two, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.

Four hundred and six days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and sixty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and nothing correct has been written beside it. Three hundred and sixty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-nine days back. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-five days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifteen marks have been cut off that board and there are three hundred and one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move.

The man of about sixty-four sat at the foot of that low wall at about half past three with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on his three hundred and ninety-seventh night of that run, having slept on three hundred and ninety-six of them.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-third of those mornings, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him why the ordinary voice.


There were three pages on that counter and they had been there every morning for as long as anybody had been looking, which was not very long, because nobody had ever looked.

They were folded in three, which is a carrier's fold and not a clerk's fold, and the paper was the cheap grey kind that comes off a roll and not the cream kind that comes out of a book.

"There's one on the counter," the woman who keeps a scale said, "because it comes down that lane once a week. One in the back, because I keep one and a half in the back. And one under the till, and I will tell you which one that is before you ask, because it's the one you'll ask about: it's a page that came down that lane about two years ago in a coat with somebody else's name on the inside of it, and it is not mine and it is not this district's and it is the reason that woman still comes down that lane."

"Whose is it."

"That's the whole of the answer and you haven't got to the other two yet." She put a weight on it so the shop door wouldn't blow. "Nine years. Not one of you lot has ever once asked me a single question about my trade, and I have sat here at quarter to eight every morning for nine years watching a district conduct its entire public life in a yard forty feet from my door, and every single bit of it has come up that lane and gone down again, and not one piece of it has ever come through here. Not the book. Not the notice. Not a stranger's page. They all go up past my window."

"And you'd take them."

"I'd *weigh* them," she said. "That's not nothing. It's the only trade I've got and I'd not be stood in this shop without it. But there's four places in this district where those three lines can be read, and it's on a page, and I've read that page about nine hundred times in fifteen years standing where you're standing, and it's four."

---

"Then it's five," Adrian said.

"It's four."

"It's five if the answer's yes and it's four if the answer's no, and you've just told me a woman comes down that lane once a week and reads on that counter, which makes it five, and I'm sorry that it took fifteen years to notice."

"You don't get to say that to me." She came out from behind the counter, which she has never done while a customer is in. "You'll go down that bank in about four minutes and you'll say it out loud in front of nineteen people, because that's what you do now, and about nine of them will agree with you and about nine won't, and in about nine mornings a man will come down that lane who has never been down it before, and he'll ask her for the fifth line, because he read it in a book that says there are four places, and there will be five, and he will have read a figure that does not agree with the world, and *that* is what happens when you move a count. Every time. Every single time in the history of this basin. Not because anybody lied. Because the count was true on the morning it was written."

Adrian stood in a shop and had absolutely no answer to that, because she had just described the entire argument he had been having with Seth for four mornings, and she had made it in under a minute and about sixty words.

"You're right," he said.

"I've been right since the sixth of the fifth month fifteen years ago and nobody's been in here to be told."

---

He said it in the yard at about eleven anyway, because not saying it was worse and because he had learned that in four mornings.

"It's five," he said. "Not four. A woman keeps three pages on a shop counter at the top of this bank and a person reads on it once a week, and a shop counter is a place, and it is not on the list, and the list is fifteen years old."

There were about nineteen people there and the clerk was at the near end of the boards with her page flat and she did not put her pencil down.

"No," she said.

"It's five."

"It's four and it stays four and here's the reason and it's the reason I've written at the top of that column since I was nineteen and it's the only line in that book I'm proud of." She put her hand on the page. "*A count in this district moves only when a person says out loud that it moved, and never before, and no name goes in beside it.* If I move that count this morning because a man with a list came up a hill and looked at a shop window, then the next time somebody moves it, it'll be because somebody's *window*, and then it will be because somebody heard about the window, and in about four years this district will have moved that count eleven times in a week and I will have written down every one of them and not one of them will have been a fact."

"Then say the fact," Adrian said. "You. Now. You are the one who is allowed to move it. That's your rule, that's your column, and you've just watched a woman show you the fifth place to your face. Say it."

"It isn't mine," the clerk said. "It's the district's. That's the entire point of it."

---

The man at the far end of the second table put his spade against the leg of it and said, "It's five."

He said it quietly, and it carried anyway, because it was a bank with two sides.

"You're the man it sends people to."

"I'm the man it *fetches*," he said. "That's not the same and you all keep getting it wrong, and I'm not going to spend the morning on it. It's five. There's five places. It's had five places for about two years because I have seen the counter, and I said nothing, and I said nothing because the counter's the only safe thing this district has got and I wasn't going to be the man who took it out of the record in a week."

He came round the end of the table.

"But you're not listening to what I said. You're listening to *five*, so let me say the next part out loud, and then you can go and move your count." He put both hands flat on the boards. "If that counter's a fifth place, then any fool who's ever read that fifth line can walk up that hill and read it and say, *that's the man the fifth line's about*, and there's a shop four feet off that counter with the woman who keeps it in it, and she opens at seven. So go on. Move your count. And then you come and stand here tomorrow morning and tell me what the district's done, because there'll be a man up that lane with a page in his hand asking after me, and not one of you will know what his name is because *I have never told anybody*, and you've been standing here for a week deciding whether to write the word *man* on a piece of paper instead."

He sat back down on the rail.

"No," he said. "It's four. Keep it at four. Put my no in the book and let them come down that lane and find an empty shop, because that's the only thing I've got and I'm using it."

---

The clerk entered that the number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four, and that it did not move this morning, and she entered the reason, which was a thing no page in that book had ever carried: that a person asked, in that yard, at about half past eleven, for the count to be moved, and that another person said no.

Orren looked at that for a long time.

"You've never entered a reason," he said.

"I've never entered a reason because in fifteen years nothing has ever happened that needed one," she said, "and I have been extremely pleased about that, and it turns out it's the same as having no reasons at all."

---

Seth came up the bank at about three with his right hand wrapped in a handkerchief and something in his coat.

"I've been up to Alder Reach," he said. "Nine miles. The man that sent for me has got the name of whoever prints the pages and I asked him for it, and he gave it, and it wasn't a person's name, it was a *room* — Room Four, under something else — and the room is in the same building as the page I read out to you three mornings ago." He put the handkerchief down on the boards. "And I stood in the corridor outside it and there was a boy of about my age coming out with a bundle of pages under his arm, and I asked him what the bundle was, and he said next week's."

"So they're printed," Adrian said.

"They're *bound*," said Seth. "There's a press, and a woman of about forty cutting them, and a man counting the corners with a little stamp, and they stack them. And I asked the boy, because I was stood there with nothing in my hands and nothing to do and I'm good at standing about, whether anybody ever came up there and asked who was named on them." He looked round the yard. "And he said, *they don't come up. Why would they come up?*"

Nobody said anything.

"So I asked him what's on them."

"And he told you," Adrian said.

"He didn't have to tell me anything. He said *conditions*." Seth took his hand off the table. "He said, they're all conditions, sir, we bind about four hundred a week and they're mostly conditions, and the ones with names on are in a different pile and the ones with names on are the ones that take longest because a condition has to be *agreed* and a name only has to be *written down*."

The yard was very quiet.

"And I stood in a corridor for about two hours," Seth said, "and I did the thing I do, which is that I looked at a piece of paper for a long time and worked out what it was for. And I want to say the next part out loud in a yard, because I've been standing down there for nine years not saying anything, and I've been a coward about it for nine years, and there's a boy's name and a clerk's hand in it and I'd like it in the air before I put it anywhere."

He looked at the clerk.

"You wrote the fifth line," he said.

---

The clerk of this district did not move for about four seconds, which is four more than she would ordinarily allow herself.

"No," she said.

"You wrote the fair copy and you have recopied it every year since because a fair copy has to be recopied, and it's in your hand, and it's the only line on that book that anybody dictated and I have read five lines off that page four hundred and eighty-three mornings' worth of times by now and four of them are conditions and one of them is a man." He put both hands on the boards. "You were nineteen. He came and found you. He said write this down."

"He did not *dictate*—"

"He did," said Seth. "Because you said so. Nine days ago, in that yard, when he said he'd never told anybody, and you wrote it in your page as *at the dictation of the man the third line is about*, and that's not a phrase anybody uses for a condition."

The clerk looked at the boards for a while.

"I was nineteen," she said. "And a man I'd never seen came to a bank in the middle of the afternoon and said he had a use and could not pay and wanted a line. And I asked him what it was for, and he said, in words I've never repeated to anybody, *for when you need a man with nobody*." She said it flatly and did not look up. "That is what he said. And I wrote it down because it was a condition and conditions are not people and I was proud of knowing the difference."

"Yes," said the man who digs loam.

"And I have been copying that sentence into a fair copy once a year for fifteen years," the clerk said, "and putting the fair copy face up on a table at the bottom of a bank, and it has sent four men up that hill in fifty mornings, and not one of them came for a number, and I did not know that, and I want the record to show that as of this morning I know it."

She picked up her pencil.

"Entered," she said, "that the fifth line on that book was written by me at the dictation of the man it is about, on a day in the fifteenth year back, at about two in the afternoon, and that I did not know it would be read by anybody, and that I have known for four mornings that it was read, and that I have entered the reasons for both of those facts and I have never entered a reason before."
