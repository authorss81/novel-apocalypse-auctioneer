# Chapter 779: The Sixty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Sixty-One Asked A Man Of Thirty-Eight To Take A Seat With An End On It And Would Not Say What The Reading Would Take

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and eighteen days is the age of that figure, and there is a second number on that board that has not moved in about nine years, and about four people in this district know there is one, and nobody has ever put the two of them in the same sentence.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-seven. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-three days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and seven days. Six hundred and sixty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty-seven days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and eighty-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and fifty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-six days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move and it has not moved since a clerk of nineteen counted one off a wall in about ten minutes in front of about nineteen people, which was the last time anybody in this basin counted a month and it was not counted on anybody's behalf. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and a man offered this morning to put a seat in one of them, and a clerk said at nine o'clock that she would enter his name. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man of sixty-one sat at the near end of the first table this morning and said the word *seat* out loud for the first time in seventeen years.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-three mornings and moved this morning from ninety-two. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-three mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it and the man of fifty-six's rule about that is in about nine mouths and has never once been in a book. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-three, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a man said a price this morning in a yard and a seat in the same breath and neither of them is the fifteenth. The lane count is twenty-one, unchanged, and there is nothing on that hill this morning with a figure on the back of it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fourth of those mornings, and he has now said them five hundred and four times to about nine people at a time and has been asked by about four of them what the oldest of them means and has told all four of them that he does not know.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and eighteenth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and seventeen of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the third afternoon running and had said good afternoon out loud twice and nothing else, and the man of about sixty-four had said good afternoon back twice, and between those two words is the whole of what has happened between them.

---

He came and stood at the near end of the first table at about nine with the case shut under his arm and he did not open it.

"If a district in this basin became the thing that holds it," he said, "what would you enter."

Mara Quill had her book open and she shut it.

"Ask the whole question."

"Suppose it happened. Suppose the founder's clause were held by a market and that market were this one, and suppose it were tomorrow, and suppose there were a sitting." He put the case down. "What goes in the book."

"I would enter the number of the five things this district does not have," she said, "and it would be five, and that is the whole of what I would enter."

---

He did not say anything until the cart wheel had finished turning, which for a man who had been in a great many rooms in this basin was a long time.

"That was very quick."

"It took me nine years," said Mara Quill. "You asked me a question nobody has ever asked me and I answered it out of the thing I have been doing since the fortieth morning and there was nothing to think about. It is the only answer this district has and it is the right one and it is not going to change."

"And if a district holds an obligation," Iven Tallow said, "does it not have to say whose hand is on it."

"It does."

---

"You would enter a name."

"I would enter your name," she said. "I would have to. A district that holds something is not a district that holds something, it is a person holding something with a district's coat on, and the whole of what you have been for seventeen years is that nobody can be found at the end of it, and I would not be able to do that, and you would not want me to."

"I would not," said Iven Tallow.

"Then you have asked me the question to hear the answer and not to hear a reason, and every single person in this yard has now asked me things for that reason, and I am going to be in a better mood about it this afternoon than I was two weeks ago."

---

She went back to the boards and Iven Tallow picked his case up and crossed to where Adrian was standing at the far end of the first table with his hands in his pockets.

"You have a blank lot in your coat," he said.

"Yes."

"You have had it since before the Fracture. You had it in a handcart on the eighth mile and then in a coat, and you have never once told me about it, and I have never once asked you about it, and we are both behaving very well."

---

"I have not told anybody in a yard what it is."

"No," said Iven Tallow. "That is the single most careful thing anybody has done in this basin since I left it, and I want you to understand that I have watched you not do it for about nine mornings and it is why I am talking to you at nine in the morning and not at four in the afternoon."

He turned and put the case down on the boards and straightened it with the side of his hand, the way he had done on the fifty-sixth morning.

"A room is not what I want," he said. "I want a seat."

---

"Say the difference."

"A room is where somebody sleeps and I have not slept in one since a morning I could tell you about. A seat is a hand on a thing. The clause says the Custodian is one person. It does not say the Custodian is one *kind* of person, and it has not said anything about a market for seventeen years, and I have been the one person in it so long that I have stopped being able to read the sentence and I read it this morning on the boards up there and it says *one person* and it does not say *one man* and it does not say *named in advance*."

"You have read that clause this morning."

"I have read it four times in about nine days, in a room, on my own, which is the first time anybody has read it without a second person in the room since I wrote it, and I would like that noticed."

---

"And what happens to it if a market sits in it."

"It would have to be rewritten," said Iven Tallow, "and only the holder can rewrite it, and that is the entire of my leverage and I am giving it to you in a yard before eleven o'clock because I am sixty-one and I have been sitting on it since the thirty-ninth morning and it has been getting heavier and I would rather be robbed than hold it. And there is a condition and I am not going to name it this morning."

"You have a condition."

"I have a condition and I have just told a man of thirty-eight that I have one and refused to give it to him, in a yard, at about a quarter to eleven, which is the first time in about forty years I have done that in the open, and I would like it entered by somebody, because I have spent forty years being a man who gave the whole of the terms away and I would like it on the record that I have started withholding one."

Adrian looked at the boards.

"You want me to take it."

"I want somebody in this basin to be able to end it and there is no procedure anywhere for who that somebody is," said Iven Tallow. "That is not a reason. That is a gap. And I have been sitting in a gap for seventeen years and I am not going to do it again with a market, because I know what a market does with a gap. It fills it in the morning."

---

"What do you want for it."

"One reading."

---

"Not in a yard."

"No," said Iven Tallow. "In a room. Two of us. Not entered before and entered afterwards whatever it says. And I am telling you now that I will accept the entry whatever it says, and I am saying that first so that you know it is not a trick and I am aware that it looks like one."

"You have the founder's mark. You have had it for seventeen years. Why have you not read anything."

"Four things in seventeen years," he said. "Two of them were pages I wrote myself, which is the same as reading nothing and I have counted them anyway because I have always believed a man who counts his own failures is a man who has not yet started lying about them properly. One was a lot I did not understand and could not make myself ask about because I did not want to know what asking would cost. And one was a reading I gave in the sixth year to a page a woman in a ward brought me herself, because she could not carry it and did not know she was carrying something."

---

Adrian did not say anything.

"She was going somewhere for a reason," said Iven Tallow. "The reading took the reason. Not the place, not the name, not who she was going to. The reason. And she did not find out for about a year, and when she did she came and found me, and she was reasonable about it, and I could do absolutely nothing, and she was not angry and I have thought about her about four times a year ever since." He shut the case. "That is what a reading costs, and I am telling you because I am not going to lie about it and I am not going to use it on you either, and those two things are both true and the second one is rarer."

---

"You will not tell me what it would take."

"No," said Iven Tallow. "A man who knows what a reading costs cannot refuse it, and a man who cannot refuse it is not a witness, and I have spent seventeen years finding that out and I am not going to be the reason you find it out at about forty-two with nobody in the room but you."

"You said that at the bottom of a wall two mornings ago."

"I said it because it is true and because I wanted it said in a yard before I said it in a room. That is not hypocrisy. That is the only order I have ever been able to work in."

---

Adrian took his hands out of his pockets.

"Everything you have said to me since the fifty-sixth morning is the argument for doing it your way," he said. "One man. One seat. One reading, in a room, with nobody else in it, entered afterwards so that nobody can look at it before. That is a complete and careful design, and I have sat in a great many rooms built exactly like that, and every one of them was built by somebody who was frightened."

"Yes," said Iven Tallow.

"And you have given me the argument against it as well."

"I have given you the argument against it in the wrong order, deliberately, and I would like you to know that I did that on purpose and that it is not modesty, it is the actual defect. I have never in my life been able to put them in the right order. I did not manage it in a hall with about nine hundred people in it and I have not managed it in about forty years since and I have stopped believing I will."

---

"Then why are you telling me at all."

"Because of what you have been doing in that yard since the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month," said Iven Tallow, "out loud, at a price, in a yard, with a clerk writing it down and reading it back. And because I am sixty-one and I have run out of anybody else. And because there is one more thing and I would like it said in front of about nineteen people rather than afterwards, which is the only part of this that is new."

He turned round and faced the yard, which was about a third full and about four of whom had stopped what they were doing.

"I have just offered a seat with an end on it to a man of thirty-eight," said Iven Tallow, "in a yard, at about ten in the morning, in front of people who can be asked about it, for one reading that will be entered afterwards whatever it comes to saying. I have not done the like in seventeen years and I did not intend to do it this morning, and I would like the yard to know that I did it anyway, because a man who is doing something like this and says afterwards that he was manipulated is a man who has arranged to be forgiven and not to be believed."

---

That was the end of his part, and the part of it that was going to cost the most had not started yet and started, as it happened, about four feet away on the boards.

"No," said Mara Quill.

---

Neither man had noticed that she had come back up the bank.

"Mara—"

"You are two people standing about four feet apart on the near end of my table talking about a reading," she said, "and there is a rule in this yard, made on the forty-fourth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, that any person may write on any paper they are carrying who was standing near them when a thing was said out loud, and that no such paper may be entered in this book by anybody, including me."

"We know your rule."

"You know about four lines of it." She was not looking at either of them. "That rule was made to protect people in this yard and it is a good rule and it is the reason the boy's page is worth nothing on the eleventh mile, and I have spent twelve mornings watching it cost somebody something every single time it is used, and it does not reach up that hill. Not one inch. And I am telling both of you that I am telling you, because one of you is going to say something in the next ten minutes that ought to be entered, and I am the only person in this yard who can enter it, and I have just told you that I cannot."

---

Iven Tallow turned round and looked at her properly, which took about as long as it takes to read a page.

"That," he said, "is the first time in forty years that an instrument has refused me in the right direction."

"And there is the second half of it and you should have it as well," said Mara Quill. "If the two of you go up that lane in an hour, then about nineteen people in this yard will know you went up it, and about nine of them will assume what was said in it, and there is no column any of them can enter it in, and no page, and no margin. That is the trap and it was built on the forty-fourth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month and you have both walked into it about nine times, and I have watched it happen every morning and I have not said a word, and I am saying a word now because I am the one who can be asked and there is nobody else in this yard who can be."

---

Adrian looked at the eleven feet of bank going down to the second table, and at the stones, and at the wall.

"She is right," he said.

"I know she is right," said Iven Tallow. "I have been in four rooms that were built exactly the way this yard has been built, and in all four of them the finding was the point, and after about a year the finding was all that anybody did, and after about two years somebody came along who could do it faster with less trouble, and they were not a worse person, and that is the part I have never been able to explain to anybody."

"You are going to use this yard."

"I am going to use it if you will let me, and I am telling you that this morning rather than after, and I would like you to hear the difference between that and what I have just described, and I would not expect you to hear it, and that is a fair thing for me to say."

---

Adrian stood on the boards of the first table in front of about nineteen people with his hands at his sides and worked out what he was going to say, and then found that he did not have it, and then found that he had it.

"I did not say no," he said.

---

The yard heard that and made a noise, and Mara Quill made no noise at all.

"I want it said exactly," Adrian said. "I have not said no and I am not going to pretend to you this morning that I did not hear what you have offered, and I am not saying yes either, and if anybody in this yard wants to know why, the reason is that you taught me that a conversation is a closing term, and a closing needs two, and a closing has to be entered. We are standing in a yard and nothing has closed."

"Then we are standing in a yard and nothing has closed," said Iven Tallow, "and that is the difference between your basin and mine, and it is the only difference I have got, and I have had it in my hands since the thirty-ninth morning and I have not been able to put it down."

---

"You will ask me again."

"I will not ask you again for a year at least," said Iven Tallow. "Not because I have gone away, and I am going to be on those stones at about half past three this afternoon for the fourth afternoon running, and a man who was there for nine years will say good afternoon to me, and that is the entire of what I have in this district."

He picked the case up.

"There is one thing left and it is yours and not mine," he said, "and you may do what you like with it and I would like you to know that I have thought about not saying it about four times since the fifty-ninth morning, and I am saying it because a man of sixty-one who has run out of anybody else does not get to be careful."

"Say it."

"You have made this yard into a place where a person can be found," said Iven Tallow, "and I am going to stand at the bottom of it at about half past three every afternoon and be found, and I am not doing that to help you and I would think less of you for thinking it. About nine people in this district are findable this month who were not findable on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, and I am the tenth, and I have been on the end of a thing for seventeen years that nobody could find, and you have made me findable in four days by being careful in a yard, and I do not know yet whether that is a rescue or a different room."
