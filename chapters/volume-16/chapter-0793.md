# Chapter 793: The Seventy-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Turner Who Comes Up That Lane Twice A Day Asked A Man Of Fifty-Six To Say The Four Figures To Him And He Said Them And A Woman With A Key Ruled That It Was Not To Happen Twice

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and thirty-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and ninety-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and seven days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twenty-one days. Six hundred and eighty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and forty-one days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and seventy-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and seventy-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and ten days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifty marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move, and a man read four figures off that wall this morning to a person who had come to the wall and asked, which is the second time in five hundred and eighteen mornings that any of the four has gone into a person, and it is still six. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man who comes up that lane twice a day asked this morning for the thing that would pay the first of them and was given a quarter of it and was told it could not happen again.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and seven mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and six. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-seven mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-seven, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-two, and the turner comes up that lane twice a day and has done for nine years and he has never brought anything up it that anybody has counted. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and nothing has gone into it since the clerk came up that bank yesterday afternoon.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and eighteenth of those mornings, and for five hundred and eighteen of those mornings they had gone into the air and not into anybody.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and thirty-second night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirty-one of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the twenty-second afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and about nine feet above them a man of fifty-six stood with his hand on a stone and waited until the two of them had finished, which he has never once done.

---

The turner came up the lane at about one and stopped at the bottom of that bank, the way he does, and put his bag down in the grass and came up the eleven feet at the pace of a man with nowhere in particular to be, which is the pace of about nine years.

He has a grey coat and both hands are good and four of the fingers on them are better than the rest, and he has been at that bench somewhere along the lane for nine years, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him about the bench, and he has never offered.

---

"Say those four to me," he said. The man of fifty-six took his hand off the wall.

---

"I have been reading them off that wall for nine years," said the turner, "coming up this lane twice a day, and I have never once heard anybody say them, and I got to the fourth one about three years in and I have had about six years of being right by myself and about six years of having no way of knowing."

---

"You have been saying them."

---

"To myself. In the lane. Going down." He said it without embarrassment and he said it the way a man says a thing he has decided is not a confession. "And there is nobody in that lane. That is the whole of what the lane is for."

---

"Then say why," said the man at the end of the second table, from the boards, without turning round, because he knew the voice.

---

"Because I want to know whether I have been wrong for six years," said the turner, "and I am not going to find out, and I would like it said in this yard that I asked and was answered and did not find out. I am forty-one. I have been coming up this lane twice a day for nine years. I have a bag and a bench and I turn things that nobody in this basin can tell apart from each other after they are done, and I have never once been asked anything by anybody in nine years, and I want the yard to have that in front of it before I say the rest."

---

"Say the rest."

---

"The rest is that not being asked is not the same as having nothing to say." He looked up the eleven feet. "I have said nothing to that yard for nine years because it was cheaper, and I have got about nine coppers' worth of nothing out of it, and yesterday morning a woman carried a beam scale up that bank and told a man of thirty-eight what he is paid in, and I have not been able to think about the bench since. That is not a reason anybody would want. It is just that I would rather say it than not say it."

---

The man of fifty-six looked at the wall, and then at the turner, and then at the wall again, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet had gone very quiet in the way that happens when everybody understands at once what is about to happen and nobody is going to stop it.

"Say them," he said.

---

"I am not going to hurry."

---

"I did not say hurry. I have got about as long as it takes and you have got nine years of your own reading and I have got five hundred and eighteen mornings of mine, and I have never once had anybody to say them to, and I am not going to do it as a favour to a person I am being kind to."

---

"Say them then, and do not do it kindly. And I will tell you what you are about to be given, because there was one of these on the seventy-third morning and I was the woman it was given to, and I entered it, and I have had four mornings to find out what entering one of those does to a person."

---

The man of fifty-six said them.

He said them the way he has said them twice a morning for five hundred and eighteen mornings, in the order they are on the wall, at about the same speed, and he got all four of them, and they went across about nine feet of yard into a man of forty-one standing at the foot of a bank with a bag in the grass.

Nobody in that yard made a sound. About four people at the top of eleven feet found out at that moment that they had never once watched him do it.

---

The turner stood there and did not say thank you and did not move for long enough that the sun came off the boards and came back on them.

"Six years," he said. "Six years off that wall in a lane with nobody in it and I would have been right or I would have been three out of four and I have not got one single morning of knowing which, and you have just given me that, and I am not going to be able to do anything with it for the rest of my life."

---

"You will go on reading them off the wall," said Sabra Holt.

---

"I am going to go on reading them off the wall."

---

"Good. I am not going to tell you that was a mistake and I am not going to tell you it was the right thing to ask for. I am going to tell you what I am going to do, and I want the yard to hear it, and I want you to hear it more than I want them to." She had the book out of her own coat and did not open it. "I am not going to let that happen a second time. Once is not a record. Once is a man reading four figures to a person who asked, and about nineteen people heard it, and there is no page anywhere in this basin that says any of it happened. Twice would be a practice. A practice is what a district has instead of a person, and I have watched four of them build one in about nine years and every one of the four was started by somebody who meant well."

---

"Then I would like it said out loud in this yard," said the man of fifty-six, "that it happened once and that I would do it again." Nobody said anything.

---

"Not to him," he said. "To anybody. Tomorrow or the next morning or in about four years when somebody comes up this hill and asks why a man of fifty-six was able to say four figures to a person he had never spoken to. It happened once. I would do it again. That is three things and you may enter all three."

---

"I will enter all three," said Sabra Holt. "And I would like it entered on the same line that I refused the second time, because in about four years one of those two facts is going to be in a room on its own and it is not going to be the one that is true."

---

"You have been in this district twelve mornings," said the turner. "You have spent all twelve of them finding out what this yard does not have."

"I have," said Sabra Holt.

---

"Then you are the first person who has come up that lane in nine years who has asked anybody anything about what they are," said the turner, "and the fourth one who has asked me nothing at all, and I would like it noticed that I have answered every question you asked and asked for none of them back, and that is not modesty, it is that I have got nine coppers and I have seen what a question costs in this basin this morning."

---

He said it to her and not to the yard, and about nine people heard it, and she did not answer it, and he picked his bag up out of the grass and went down the eleven feet and went up the lane at the same pace.

---

Adrian got to the foot of the bank at about half past two, which he does, and stood at the end of the second table where the boards are narrow enough for two people and not three.

"You passed her on the lane this morning," he said. "On your way up. You did not stop."

---

"I did not stop."

---

"Why not," said Adrian.


"Because she has been in that yard twelve mornings and has not asked anybody anything she did not come here to ask, and I have been in that yard for nine years of mornings without saying anything at all, and if I had stopped on that lane at about one o'clock with a bag in my hand and started asking her what she was doing here then I would have been doing it to be seen doing it, and about four people in that yard would have watched me do it and about nine would have known why."

He shifted the bag.

"You are the fourth person who has not asked me a question in nine years," he said. "The other three are not in this basin."

---

"I am going to ask you one," said Adrian.

"Then ask it and I will answer it in the lane on the way down and not in that yard, because I have been in this yard once this morning and I have taken about nine people's whole morning with me already and I am not doing it twice."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard the third reading of that morning and two of them could not have told you afterwards whether the first one had happened.

Orren Pike turned the back of his own sheet to the boards and wrote the morning in the box headed for mornings on which a thing was said out loud in that yard and was not entered anywhere, and ruled a line under it and left it blank. Then he wrote under the rule, in pencil, and it came out uneven because he was tired: *one person. twice a day. nine years. and he asked.*

He looked at it for a while and did not rule it off.
