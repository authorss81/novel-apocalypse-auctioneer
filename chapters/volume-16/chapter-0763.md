# Chapter 763: The Forty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Boy Found A Word In The First Column Of One Of His Own Rows And Said So Out Loud In A Yard Without Saying Whose Row It Was

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and two days is the age of that figure, and the two of them have not been in the same breath together in nine years, and Adrian had worked out a few mornings ago that the reason he was certain of it was that she was, and that neither of them could produce the reason. The board carries six hundred and sixty-one, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-seven days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and ninety-one days, and six hundred and fifty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and eleven days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run, and the figure is the same figure as the one on the sheet at that gatepost, and Adrian noticed that on the sixth morning and had not been able to put it down since, and had not told anybody, and was not going to.

Three hundred and sixty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and seventy-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and forty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and forty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and eighty days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twenty marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six, and it did not move, and nobody in this district has counted one since a clerk of nineteen stood at a board and counted a month off a wall in about ten minutes in front of about nineteen people. The number of documents this district does not own is four and it did not move. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and it did not move, and a boy of seventeen has said out loud that a row on his own page has a word in it where a name goes, and that is not a place either. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them and it was on that book this morning working exactly as it was written.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank seventy-seven mornings and moved this morning from seventy-six. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for twenty-seven mornings with nobody's thanks on it. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and it did not move, and a boy read out a yard full of people the fact that his own page has a word in it where a name goes, and that is not a fifteenth, and a defect is not a thing anybody made. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-seven, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-eighth of those mornings. He looked to his right once, at about eleven, and then looked back at the wall, and nobody saw him do it and the boy did.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundred and second night of that run, having slept on four hundred and one of them, and about nine people in that yard knew about him now and had known for under a day.

---

The boy was on the boards at about nine with the sheet open on his knee, which he did not do, and Adrian sat down on the step of the first table about nine feet away because the alternative was standing over him and he had learned that week what standing over a boy with a sheet does.

"Go on," Adrian said.

"I'm not asking you anything."

"You've opened it on your knee in daylight, which means you've got something and you're deciding whether to give it away, and I know what that looks like because I did it on the fortieth morning and the clerk took me apart in twenty minutes."

"She didn't take you apart," Orren said. "She agreed with you and let you keep going, and that is worse and you know it is."

He turned the sheet round without being asked, which he had never done, and put one finger on the first column.

"Four columns," he said. "What was said. The figure. The day it was said. Who was standing next to them when they said it. Nine years of building the thing and eleven months of using it, and I've got a page for every person in this yard who's been here more than twice, and I have never once had trouble with it, and there is one row in the first column that has a word in it where a name goes."

"What word."

"*The man*," the boy said. "Same two words as the third line of your lot book. I have written it in the first column where I have written *Sela Pike* and *the man who puts tables up* and *the clerk* and *the boy*, and I wrote it about two years ago, on a morning I can tell you the day of, because I count days, and I have looked at it maybe four hundred times since, and I have never told anybody, and I want you to understand that I did not know I had done it."

"You did know."

"I want you to understand the other thing," the boy said. "There's a difference and it's the only difference I've got. I knew I had written two words. I did not know I had written a *word*."

---

Adrian looked at the sheet for a while.

"Tell me who it's for."

"No."

"You've told me your mother's row. You've told me the shape of the whole thing four times in two days, on the grounds that I was the only adult who'd asked. Don't do that now and pretend it's a different kind of privacy, because it isn't, and you'll not get credit for it."

The boy took the sheet back and put it against his chest.

"You've been in this yard two months," he said. "You gave a man a deal on the forty-third morning where he told you a name and you promised not to write it, and you kept it, and it cost you more than anything else you did that week and everybody saw it cost you. And you think I'm going to walk down that bank and ask him the same question and expect to be allowed to keep it. He'd tell me. That's the trouble. He gave it to you because you're leaving."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"You said you'd be gone in a year and you were right about that on the thirty-ninth morning, so don't do it again."

---

"There's a man at the end of that table," the boy said, "who has been in this yard nine years and has never once been on a page in this district, and on the forty-third morning he gave a name to a person and the person did not write it down, and I have been sitting on that step for two days trying to work out what a record is supposed to do with that, and I have worked out three things and I do not like any of them."

"Go on."

"One: it is lost. Not kept — lost. It is in a man's head in a room and it comes out of that head the day he dies and it was never anywhere, and everything this yard has spent nine years being proud of is the fact that things go in a book, and this did not go in a book, on purpose, at a price, and that price is the only reason it is still a name and not a rumour."

"Two."

"Two: it is not lost, because I have got a row for him, and a row for him is not nothing." He held the sheet up. "And the row has a word in it where the name goes, and the word is *the man*, and I put it there, and I did not ask him, and I have not told him, and if you ever tell him I will take the sheet into the lane and burn it and you will not get it back and I will not be sorry."

"Three," Adrian said.

"Three is that I have been doing the district's trick in a coat pocket for two years and I have been *proud* of the trick, and there is a difference between me and the lot book," the boy said, "and the difference is that the lot book was written by a clerk who was told the words, and I invented mine, and inventing it is worse."

---

He went up the bank at about a quarter past ten and asked for the yard, which nobody in that district had ever done, and about nineteen people came and stood about, and he stood on the boards of the first table with the sheet under his arm and said the whole of it in a voice that shook twice and did not go anywhere else.

"There is a row on my page with a word in the first column where a name goes," he said, "and I am not going to say whose row it is, and I am telling you it is there because I found it this morning and it is mine and I would rather say it out loud than have one of you find it in a year and think I'd planted it."

He got about nine words into the next part and the man who digs loam said, "Aye."

Not loudly. Just *aye*, the way a man says a thing that has been true for two years and has just been confirmed by somebody who was not him.

Everybody in that yard turned round and looked at the far end of the second table, and the man at the far end of the second table did not move, and his hands stayed on the near rail, and about nine people watched a man not move and worked out that the word was about him.

Mara Quill's pencil stopped.

"Entered," she said, and it took her a moment to get to it. "That a person said a thing out loud in that yard, and that a second person said one word out loud, and that I am entering no row and no name and no word, and that I have never entered a word in this district and I am not going to start on a morning like this one, and that the reason is that a word is not a thing a person said, it is a thing a person did not have."

---

He came down the bank at about eleven and stood four feet off the near end of that table and did not have anything in his hands, which Adrian understood was the entire point of the four feet.

"I wrote *the man*," he said.

"I know."

"Two years ago. On a morning you were not here." He put one hand on the boards. "I did not do it to be cruel and I did not do it to be kind. I did it because a blank was worse. You know what a blank does in a column. A blank is a hole and a hole is a question and a question about a man who has no name is a question about a man. And I filled it. With two words. And then I was able to sleep, which is the whole of the reason."

"You should have told me when I asked you for it."

"You didn't ask me for it," the man said. "You asked me to hand it over. That's not the same question and you know it isn't, and you did the same thing to me nine mornings ago in the other order, and I have been thinking about the order of your questions all week and I would like to stop."

"Right."

"I'm not angry." He said it flatly, and it was true, and it was worse than anger. "I am going to be in this yard in about eleven weeks when whoever is standing where you are standing says a number out loud that I cannot get out from under, and I have spent nine years being a line on a book, and there is now a second record of me in a boy's coat that I did not agree to and that the district has just been told about in a yard, and neither of those things has a name on it and both of them are about me, and there is not one thing in this basin that can tell the difference between a record of a man and a thing that happens to a man."

He looked at the boy, who was nine feet up the bank with the sheet against his chest.

"Don't you dare come down here and apologise," he said. "I mean that. If you come down here and say you're sorry then you've made me a favour and I have to decide about it, and I have got enough decisions on that table. You put a word in a column when you were about fifteen and you did it because a blank is worse. I know why you did it because I did the same thing with a line in a book when I was about twenty-two. We are the same instrument twice."

---

The boy did not come down the bank for the rest of that day, and Adrian did not go up it, and at about half past two the boy found him at the shop and put the sheet on the counter and asked for the one thing he had asked for on the fortieth morning and had been refused.

"The fortieth morning you asked me to stop a man so that a page could be written down, and he said no, and I have not got a page for him, and you did not ask me anything I wanted," the boy said. "I want to be asked. Not by her and not by you. I have been asked nine things this week and not one of them was that. So here it is, once, and then it's asked."

"Go on."

"Next time somebody comes down that lane with a page in their hand, I want you standing in that yard, and I want you to say nothing at all," the boy said. "Not a figure, not a reading, not your name, nothing. Nine people and a clerk and a wall. I want to watch what happens to a thing when a man with a name in a book is in the room and has nothing to say, because I have got four years of a page and I have never once had a person in a room with me while a thing happened, and I have absolutely no idea what it looks like, and it is the last thing on that page I cannot do on my own."

Adrian thought about it for longer than the boy expected.

"You want a witness," he said.

"I want a *witness*," the boy said, and it came out of him like something that had been sitting in his chest, "and there is not one in this basin. Not one. The clerk is a record. A stranger is a man with a page. You are the only person anybody here has ever seen stand in a room and say nothing, and you have never once done it in this yard, and I have noticed, and I have been not-noticing it on purpose for two months because I did not have anybody to give it to."

"All right," Adrian said. "Yes."

"Write it down."

"I'll not write it down."

"You'll write it down," the boy said, "on your own paper, in your own hand, and you will not give it to me, and you will not give it to her, and if anybody asks you tomorrow why you stood in that yard saying nothing you will be able to say that a boy of seventeen asked you to on the forty-seventh morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, and that is not nothing. That is the entire thing. That is what the column is for."

---

He wrote it at about half past three on the back of a page from his own coat, in a hand that was not the hand he wrote figures in, and folded it once and put it in the inside pocket on the side away from the wire.

"You've had that wire in your pocket for about two months," the boy said, at the door.

"Yes."

"What is it for."

"It is for a gap in a fence about four miles from here that is about the length of my two hands," Adrian said, "and I have not been to it, and I have known that every morning, and I have not been to it, and I have not thrown it away, and that is the whole of it and I have never said it out loud before and I would rather you did not put it in the sheet."

The boy did not put it in the sheet.

The count of the five things this district does not have is still five and it was not paid. Four conditions have no end on it and the count did not move, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them and it was on that book working exactly as it was written, and a word that is not a name went into a yard this morning in front of about nineteen people and is on no page in this district and cannot be.

The clerk had entered the bid at about a quarter to eight and had entered the figure on the sheet at that gatepost underneath it, in that order, and had made no remark about the two of them being the same number, and it was going to be a problem in about nine mornings.
