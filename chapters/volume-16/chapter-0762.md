# Chapter 762: The Forty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man At The Far End Of The Second Table Went Down To The Low Wall At Half Past Three And Asked A Question That Nobody In Nine Years Had Put To Him

He had not been down there in nine years.

The man who digs loam was at the far end of that second table at about eleven with his spade against the leg of it and two hands on the near rail, and at about half past three he put the spade down flat, which he had never once done in that yard, and went down the last eleven feet of the bank on foot, and about nine people watched a man walk to a wall he had been able to see from where he stood all morning.

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it did not move this morning and it did not move yesterday. Five hundred and one days is the age of that figure. The board carries six hundred and sixty. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-six days. Nobody has entered anything for six hundred and ninety days, and six hundred and fifty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and ten days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run. Three hundred and sixty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and seventy days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and forty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and forty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-nine days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and nineteen marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and it did not move, and a page came down that lane on the thirty-seventh morning and a page went back up it yesterday afternoon, and neither of them was a month. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move this morning, and the woman who keeps a scale was in her shop with a weight on the door and the third of those four places has a page lying on it that is not hers. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and it is four and not five, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank seventy-six mornings and moved this morning from seventy-five. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for twenty-six mornings. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-six, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-seventh of those mornings, and at about four, for the first time in four hundred and one nights, there was another man on that wall, forty feet off, standing up off the stones with his coat over his arm, on his four hundred and first night of that run and having slept on four hundred of them.

---

Adrian got to the bottom of the bank at about ten past three because he had watched the spade go down flat, and by the time he was on the boards the man was already at the stones.

"I'll ask him," Adrian said.

"No."

It came out of the man without any weight on it at all, the way a thing comes out when it has been sitting ready for two days.

"You said on the forty-third morning you would tell me a thing in a room," Adrian said. "You said you wanted somebody to have it and not that table. I've been standing up there for three mornings waiting for you to say you wanted to say it out loud."

"I'm not saying a word of it out loud," the man said. "And you'll not be asking it either. You stood four feet off that end of my table nine mornings ago and asked me if I wanted to put my name in a book and you gave me the right to say no before you asked, and that was the first decent thing anybody has done in this yard in nine years, and I have thought about very little else since. And I am not going to spend that."

"I'm not offering you anything."

"You're offering everybody everything since the thirty-fifth morning and you don't stop," the man said. "You offered yourself to a room in Alder Reach in front of nineteen people and a woman of fifty-eight had to come and tell you what that had cost. So you'll forgive me for being careful about what I'm the one who spends."

Adrian stood there and had nothing in his mouth and did not fill it, which was the hardest work he did in that district and the only thing in it he was actually good at.

"All right," he said.

"That's it, then. Stand there and be quiet and don't write anything down, and if you've got a pencil in that pocket take it out and put it on the boards where I can see it, because I did that for you nine mornings ago and I'd like the day back."

He took the pencil out and put it on the boards about four feet from the man who keeps a tally, who had followed them down without being asked and was standing at the top of the eleven feet with his arms folded and the expression of a man watching a figure he had entered come out wrong in front of him.

Then the man who digs loam turned round and faced the stones, and the stones had a man of about sixty-four on them with his coat folded beside him and his hands on his knees, and he had been there since about half past three, and he had heard every word of it.

---

"You've been on that wall four hundred and one nights," the man said.

"I have."

"I've been at the end of that table nine years and I can see it from where I sit, and I've never once come down here, and I have thought about why about four times in nine years and I never came down."

"You've had a good reason."

"I've had a good reason about nine times a morning and I'm tired of it," the man said. "What's the run."

He said it in the ordinary voice. He did not make it a question with anything on the end of it. He said it the way the man of fifty-six says four figures, and Adrian, forty feet up the bank, put his hand flat on the boards and understood that he had watched the whole week arrive at a stone.

The man of about sixty-four looked up for a while.

"That's the first time," he said.

"What is."

"Four hundred and one nights." He took his hands off his knees and put them back. "Nine years, and about four thousand people have walked past this wall, and a man of fifty-six stands at the other end of it twice a morning and says four numbers out loud, and I have heard every one of them, and nobody has ever turned round. Not once. Not in nine years. And I have not said one word either, and I want you to understand that that was a bargain and I made it too."

"I understand that."

"You don't," the man said, kindly. "But you're about to."

---

"I go at about half past four," he said. "I come back at first light. There's a turn and a culvert and then about two miles of nothing and then a building, and I do not know what is in the building and I have never been inside it and I am not going to be."

"How long is it. The whole thing."

"Out and back is most of the night." He said it the way a man says the price of something. "I have been paid nothing. I have been given nothing. I have been asked nothing, and I have kept the second of those three better than I have kept the first, and I am aware that that is a stupid order to keep them in."

"You've slept on four hundred of them."

"You've been counting me."

"My clerk has been counting you. She's been counting you since you sat down and she has never once said so out loud, because she is a careful woman and because you never asked her to."

The man of about sixty-four made a sound that was not a laugh.

"Aye," he said. "Well. That's the first sentence anybody has said to me in nine years that was about the arithmetic and not about me. I have four hundred and one nights and I have slept on four hundred of them and there is one night in the middle of that I could not tell you about and I have never been asked and I am not going to be now."

"You're not owed anything."

"No," the man said. "That's the part I cannot get anybody to hear and I have had four hundred and one nights to say it plainly. I am not owed anything. I have no household. I am not named on anything. There is no line in that book at the top of this bank and there is no line anywhere else in this district and I have never once been a person a page could be held to, and that is the whole of what I have, and I have never spent it, because nobody has ever come to this wall."

He turned his head.

"Ask the man at the end of your table what his line says."

"I've read it."

"Then you know it's a standing offer of work with no end on it, with no pay and no way of stopping it." The man of about sixty-four said it flatly, without any weight, and Adrian felt the shape of the next eight months arrive in that yard and he could not move. "I have got no line and I have got no end either, and I have got no pay, and I have never been asked, and I have never been able to stop. You want to know the difference. There isn't one. There is one line in this basin and no line, and I am on the side with no line, and I have been on it for every night of that run, and the only reason your man is standing at that table and I am sitting on these stones is that somebody with a pen wrote his down fifteen years ago and nobody has written mine down at all."

---

The man who digs loam stood very still.

Then he said: "I did not come down here to tell you what you are owed. I came down here because I have been on a line with no end on it for fifteen years and I have worked out this week that a line is the only reason I am findable, and I have spent nine years being glad of it."

"You came down here to ask me a question," the man said, "and I have answered it, and I would like it noticed that nobody has ever once in nine years asked me anything, and that it took eleven seconds."

"It took about nine hours," the man said. "I sat at that table until a quarter past three and I worked out that you were the only man in this district I could ask without being a thief, because you have got nothing on me and I have got nothing on you, and because you have never once asked me what the fifth line is for, and everybody else in this yard has."

"That is a very good reason and it is the wrong one," the man said. "The right one is that you've heard four hundred and one nights of that yard and nothing has ever been said to you, and you've got to the age where a thing like that is harder to carry than being hungry."

He got up off the stones. He was slower than Adrian had expected and he picked his coat up off the stones and did not put it on.

"I have one thing to ask you for," he said. "And it is the only thing I have ever asked anybody for."

"Ask."

"Don't write it down."

---

Mara Quill came down the bank at about ten past four with her book flat against her chest and stood at the top of the eleven feet, and the man who keeps a tally was still there, and the man of fifty-six was at the wall, and about nineteen people had found reasons to be in that yard in the space of forty minutes and none of them had come down.

"Entered a claim," she said. "That's what it is. You are asking me to enter that a man at the foot of a wall has said he is owed something."

"No," the man of about sixty-four said. "That is the opposite of what I asked. I am asking you *not* to enter it. If you enter it, I am a man with a claim on this district, and a man with a claim can be refused, and being refused is a thing that happens to men who are written down. If you do not enter it, I am a man on a stone."

"That is not an argument," the clerk said, "that is a preference, and I have never in fifteen years refused a person on the ground of a preference."

"Then I have come to the wrong clerk," the man said, "and I am going to be here in about twenty minutes, and you can decide in twenty minutes whether you are the wrong clerk."

---

She decided in about nine.

Adrian was not there for it and found out about it at about six, from the boy, who had been standing at the top of the eleven feet the whole time because nobody had told him to go away, and who had the whole of it in the margin of his own sheet in about forty words.

She did not enter it in the lot book. She wrote it on the last page of her own book, on a page with nothing ruled on it, in her clear small hand, and turned it round so that the man of fifty-six could read it standing up.

> *Entered at the top of this bank at about ten past four on the forty-sixth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, on the word of M. Quill, and not entered in the lot book, because the lot book has five lines on it and the count of the five things this district does not have is five, and there is no line in that book for a man who is owed something by a person he cannot name, and there never has been, and I have looked twice.*
>
> *A man of about sixty-four has said out loud, at the foot of the low wall, that he goes out at about half past four and comes back at first light, and that he is paid nothing, and that he has been asked nothing in nine years, and that he has four hundred and one nights of it and has slept on four hundred of them.*
>
> *I am entering this in my own book and not in that one. I am entering no name. He asked me not to enter it and I am entering it, and I would like it known that I did it against his wishes and that I would do it again, and that those two facts are going in next to each other, because one of them without the other is a lie, and I wrote that down four mornings ago and I am now finding out what it costs to have written it.*

The man of fifty-six read it twice and then read the four figures off the wall, and got them, and did not say one word about the page, and that was the second reading of that morning and the page was in the air twice.

When he had finished he stood where he was and looked at the eleven feet of bank going down to the stones, and from about nine feet away Adrian watched a man who had said four numbers out loud twice a morning for four hundred and eighty-seven mornings work out that he had never once looked to his right.

"I have been on that wall nine years," the man said. "Every morning at a quarter to eight and every afternoon at about four. And I have never once looked right, and there has been a man on those stones at about half past three every afternoon of that time, and I did not know, and I would not have known if a boy had not put it in a column."

"You knew he was there," Adrian said.

"I knew somebody was," the man said. "I have been saying four numbers forty feet from a man for nine years because I have never had to say them to anybody, and that is the best of it, and I did not know that was why either." He turned round to the wall. "Do not tell me anything else. I have got a run of mornings and I would like to get back to it."

---

The count of the five is still five. Four conditions have no end on it and there are four of them, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the run of a man of about sixty-four is not one of the four, because there is no line for it anywhere and it has never been on a book.

The clerk was right about that and nobody had asked her to be, and about nine people in that yard had it inside a minute: the two men at the bottom of that bank had been kept where they were by the same absence, and the only difference between them was that one of them had been written down.
