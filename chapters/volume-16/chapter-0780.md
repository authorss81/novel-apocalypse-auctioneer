# Chapter 780: The Sixty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Who Had Been In That Yard Eighty-Five Mornings Wrote Four Lines With No Figure And No Term On Them

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it does not care what morning it is, and it will be there on the next one. Five hundred and nineteen days is the age of that figure, and neither of the two of them has ever been in a yard.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-eight. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-four days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and eight days. Six hundred and sixty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty-eight days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page, and there is nobody in this district who has ever heard anybody propose anything else. Three hundred and eighty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and fifty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-one days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-seven days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move and did not move yesterday and is not going to move this month. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and has not moved all book. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four, and a page went up that lane this afternoon with nothing on it that would make a fifth. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and there is a clause nine miles up that hill with no end on it as well, and there is a seat with an end on it that was offered in a yard yesterday morning and not taken, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid on this morning and was not paid on any morning this district has had a lane with a figure at the end of it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-four mornings and moved this morning from ninety-three. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-four mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it, and he came through that yard at about ten this morning and did not stop, and there is a length of wood on the near side of that table that has nothing to do with anybody's kindness. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-four, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered, and that figure and the morning itself have been the same figure on every morning of this book, and nobody has ever been asked to make them differ, and nobody is going to be the one who asks. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and the count of things this district has made is still fourteen. The lane count is twenty-one, and did not move, and does not move for the other direction, and there is nobody in this district who has ever proposed otherwise.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fifth of those mornings, and nothing has been said at that wall except the four until this morning, and about nine people in that yard had made up their minds by the middle of the week that something was going to be said at about four, and not one of them had said so out loud.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and nineteenth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and eighteen of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the ninth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and that was all, and it was the ninth morning a man of sixty-one had been in that yard and the ninth afternoon and it had taken about nine seconds to arrange and about nine years to be worth arranging.

---

He had the page flat on the boards of the first table from about a quarter to eight, and it had four lines on it, and it had no figure on it and no term on it, and he had been looking at it for about an hour and a half before Iven Tallow came and stood about four feet off him and read it upside down without asking.

"You will get the same answer you got the first time," Iven Tallow said.

"Yes."

"You know that?"

"I know the room reads the last two words on a thing and ignores everything before them," Adrian said. "I know because I wrote the first one as a district and had *we* in it seven times, and I know because on the fiftieth morning a woman of fifty-eight said a name out loud in that yard that she had carried nine years in a bag, and I did not write it down, and she watched me not write it down. And when I put one to you on the fifty-ninth morning you refused the sentence and you did not refuse the man, and both of those things were true and only one of them helped."

---

"What is different about this one."

"There is no last two words that could be a price," Adrian said. "There is a figure: none. There is a term: none. And there is a name at the bottom and it is not *we* and it is not a market, it is a man, and if a man puts his own name at the bottom of a page then the person who cannot be paid is a person and not a district, and a room can refuse a person and can have an argument about a person."

"That is better by about a mile than the first one and it will not work," said Iven Tallow.

"Then it is better by a mile and it will not work and I would rather send it."

---

The man who drives the cart was at the top of that bank at about half past two with his near wheel dragging and he had his hands in his pockets.

"I'm not carrying it up that lane for nothing," he said. "I've carried four things up that lane in nine years and two of them were people and one of them was a question and one of them was a page a woman with a scale read out loud in a yard, and I've not asked about any of them, and I'd rather go on not asking. So what's in this one."

"A question," Adrian said.

"I've carried a question before," said the man of about twenty-nine. "It went up about nine years ago and it came back down as nothing and there was nobody on the road who could say where it had gone."

---

"Then here is what I want," the man said. "I want it said in that yard, before I put it in the cart, that this thing went up the lane in my hands and what is on it. Not my name. I don't want my name and I've spent nine years not having one in this district and I'm not starting now." He took his hands out of his pockets. "I want the yard to be able to answer whoever asks them where it went. Because if a stranger comes up that hill in about four years and asks a boy of seventeen where a page went, then a boy of seventeen who can say *it went up that lane in a cart and the yard knows* is a different animal to a boy of seventeen who has to shrug. That's all. Nine words."

"Nine words and it is yours," Adrian said.

"Nine words and it is mine," said the man of about twenty-nine, "and I have been the figure twenty-one in a yard since the forty-ninth morning and I did not stop to be a person, I stopped because a boy asked me to, and I would like one thing in this district that is mine by being asked rather than by being counted."

---

They did it at about ten to three.

Adrian read it himself. He stood on the boards of the first table with the sun behind him and about nineteen people in that yard and he did not ask anybody to do it and he did not hand it to a woman with a scale, and about four people in that yard understood at once why he had not handed it to her and about nine more understood about an hour later.

> *Sent from Lot Seventeen, at the foot of a bank, on the sixty-fourth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month. Figure: none. Term: none. Neither is sent, and neither can be sent from here, and both have been sent from here before, and a figure was read off the back of the last one and written down as a price.*
>
> *Ask instead, and answer in a yard if the answer can be made true in a yard: what is the founder's clause; who holds it; whether it has an end on it; and whether the person who holds it can be named in front of about nineteen people who have never held anything.*
>
> *The market of this district cannot answer the whole of that, and has said so out loud. One of the people whose consent the term requires is not in this district, has not been asked, and cannot be found by anybody who comes up this lane. That was said out loud in this yard on the fifty-eighth morning and it is being said again here so that whoever reads this has it in the same breath as the question and not after it.*
>
> *Signed by a person and not by a district. Adrian Vale. Clerk of this district, entered no part of it, and I will enter no part of it.*

---

The clerk did not enter it.

"I am not entering it," she said, when he had finished, "and I am not entering the fact that you read it out loud either, and I would like the yard to notice that I said *read it out loud*, because the column for whoever read a thing out loud is four feet from my elbow and it has one name in it and it is not getting a second, and there is a rule that was made on the forty-fourth morning that says a conversation is not a record and I am the clerk of this yard and I have decided that a thing you read in that yard is a conversation."

"Nobody asked you to decide it," said the boy of seventeen, from about nine feet below the boards.

"Nobody has ever asked me to do anything except the work," said Mara Quill, "and that is a different thing, and I have been getting it wrong since the fortieth morning, and it is nearly over, and I am not going to be one of the things this yard loses."

---

It went up that lane at about half past four in a cart that pulled to one side, and the yard was told what was on it in about nine words, and the man of about twenty-nine said the nine words himself without being asked, which nobody had expected, and then said that he had not been asked and would like that noticed too.

The lane count is twenty-one and did not move. Nothing has come down that lane with a page in his hand since a cart stopped four feet below the top of that bank about fifteen mornings ago, and a page went up it at about half past four this afternoon, and that is the other direction and the count does not care about the other direction and there is nobody in this district who has ever proposed that it should.

---

At about four the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall.

Six hundred and seventy-eight. Nine hundred and ninety-four. Seven hundred and eight. Six hundred and sixty-nine.

He got all four of them, in the ordinary voice, to about nineteen people, and the clerk's pencil went into a book, and about four people said *as sent* under their breath because they had been saying it for a fortnight.

Then he took his hand off the stone.

"Eighty-five," he said.

---

Nobody in that yard had asked him to say anything except four figures, and the whole yard looked at each other, and the boy of seventeen came off the boards.

"Mornings," said the man of fifty-six. "And there is nobody to ask me why I have got it right and I would like that said, because I have been saying four numbers off this wall to whoever is standing there twice a morning for five hundred and five mornings and I have never once been asked for a fifth, and a boy of seventeen counted it on the boards about nine mornings ago, told the yard that his own method had a hole in it, and then went and found the hole himself, and I have been listening to him do that for nine mornings and this morning I would like to be in it."

"Who asked you to read it out," said the boy.

"Nobody asked me," said the man of fifty-six. "Nobody has asked me for anything since this wall."

---

He stood with his hand on the stone for about as long as it takes to read four figures.

"I said on the fiftieth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month that I wanted nothing to happen and that I was going to be frightened for about a year," he said, "and I have had fourteen mornings of it, and about four people in this yard have been very careful with me, which I noticed and did not want. And I have just found out the part I did not expect, which is that the frightening is not being asked. It is being the only thing in that yard that anybody is sure of." He took his hand off the stone. "So go on. Ask me things. I will read you whatever you want read and I will get it right and I will not know what any of it is for."

---

The boy of seventeen wrote it in pencil on the back of his own sheet, in a box he had ruled about two mornings ago at about twenty past four in the afternoon, in the box headed *mornings on which a thing was said out loud in this yard and was not entered anywhere*.

He wrote the morning and not the number, and then he stopped, and then he wrote the number, and then he said out loud that he was not entering it anywhere and that the rule he had argued for on the forty-fourth morning forbade anybody entering it, and that he had written that rule and would write it again.

Mara Quill did not ask him for the sheet. Nobody in that yard asked him for the sheet.

---

The books were shut at about six.

The second table was bare with the stone lying on it face up and the hollow an inch deep in the middle and nothing at either end, and the rail was on the near side with nothing on it and nothing on it in the way anybody in that yard holds their hands, and the lot book was on the boards at the top of that bank with five lines on it and a margin in the fifth line and a column with one name in it, and a page with nothing ruled on it at the back with three lines on it now and none of the three in the lot book.

Adrian Vale had been in that yard eighty-five mornings and had not been given a bed, a wage or an office, had not asked for any of the three, and had been offered on the fiftieth morning a mile and a half of nothing in daylight with a book at each end of it, and had not taken it and had not said no, and there is a length of fencing wire in the pocket of his coat about four miles from a gap of a mile and a half and he has not been to it and did not go to it this morning.

---

At about ten past six Iven Tallow was at the top of that bank with the case under his arm and Adrian was about nine feet off the end of the first table, and the two of them looked down eleven feet at a low wall and two stones and a second table with nothing on it.

"Eighty-five mornings," said Iven Tallow.

"Eighty-five."

"And no bed, and no wage, and no office, and a road keeper who offered you a mile of nothing and told you the three words out loud so that you could not say afterwards he had offered you one." He shifted the case. "I have been the thing that holds up every room in this basin for seventeen years and I have not slept in one of them. Do not put those two facts next to each other. People will."

"I'll be careful."

"You will not," said Iven Tallow. "That is the whole of what I have learned and I am saying it at the top of a bank in about nineteen people's hearing so that if it happens there is one morning on which somebody else said so first."

---

Then he went up the lane on foot, in the dark, with the case under his arm, and everybody at the bottom of that bank watched him go and nobody followed him and about four people at the top of it had already decided they were going to be at the bottom of that bank again in the morning, and the man who drives the cart was two hundred yards up the lane by then with a page in the cart that had no figure on it and no term on it and a question on it, and the number of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move and does not have to.
