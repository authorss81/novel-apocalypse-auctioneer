# Chapter 789: The Seventy-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman With A Key Asked The Man Of Fifty-Six To State For A Record What He Reads And For How Long And Did Not Ask Him Which Of The Four Was Wrong And A Boy Of Seventeen Counted A Man In Front Of Her And Gave Her Two Figures And Refused The Wrong One

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and a man in that yard has now read four figures off a wall in front of an appointed administrator for the five hundred and fourteenth time, and she wrote down all four and did not ask him which of them was wrong, and there is no instrument in this basin that would have let her. Five hundred and twenty-eight days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and three days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and seventeen days. Six hundred and seventy-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-seven days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and seventy days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and six days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six, and it moved on no morning of this book, and it will not move this month. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and this morning the fifth of the five was said out loud in that yard as a fact about one person and not as a sentence about a district, and it is still not paid, and the count is still five.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and three mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and two. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-three mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-three, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, entered before he said it this morning and after he said it on every other one, and he said it out loud at about ten in front of about nineteen people, with a woman holding one of the oldest keys in this basin standing about four feet off him, and he entered it at a quarter to eight before he said a word of it, and entered no reason for either. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards, five lines on it, and a column with one name in it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fourteenth of those mornings, and the first of those two was in front of a woman with a key and a page and about nineteen people, and he did not know that was different and would not have read them differently if he had.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-eighth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-seven of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the eighteenth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and about four people at the top of eleven feet had stopped talking, and nobody had asked them to stop and nobody knew when it had started.

---

"I am going to ask you four questions and I want them all answered in the ordinary voice with nothing left out," said Sabra Holt, "and I want to say the fourth one now, before I ask the first, so that you know it is not coming. I am not going to ask you which of those four is wrong. You have been asked that in this yard and you have not been able to answer it, and I have read the reason, and the reason is better than anything I could have asked for, and I would have lost the thing I came here for if I had asked it."

"Then ask the other three."

"The first. What do you read."

"Six hundred and eighty-seven. One thousand and three. Seven hundred and seventeen. Six hundred and seventy-eight." He said them the way he has said them twice a morning for five hundred and fourteen mornings, in the order they are on the wall, at about the same speed, and got all four of them. "That is the order and that is the wall and I have never once gone round the other way."

"The second. How long have you been reading them."

"Five hundred and fourteen mornings, at about a quarter to eight and again at about four, and I have never once been away on the mornings, and there is nobody in this district who could tell you which morning I started and I could not tell you either, and I want to say something about the number and then I want to say something else and I would like both in whatever book you are keeping."

---

"Go on," said Sabra Holt.

"The number is a number of mornings and I have got it the only way anybody in this yard gets anything, which is by doing it, and I have never once been asked to show anybody, and I have never once been checked, and I have wanted to be checked every morning since I began and I have not wanted it badly enough to ask, and I have just said that out loud in a yard in front of about nineteen people and a stranger with a key, and I would like it entered in that order and not tidied."

She wrote it in that order.

"The third. Who do you read them to."

"Whoever is standing there." He looked at the yard. "For the first four hundred mornings, and more than that, that was nobody. For about thirty mornings it was whoever wanted to know what a yard was for. For seventeen mornings there have been people in that yard every morning at about a quarter to eight who did not want to know and stood there anyway, and I have read them to those people, and I have never once read them to a person because nobody has ever asked me to read them to a person."

---

"And the fourth," said Sabra Holt, "which I did not promise you because I promised you three and I am going to owe you the truth about that before the morning is out. Has any person in this district ever been paid for reading."

"No."

"Has anybody ever been paid for anything in this yard that anybody could see."

"No."

"Has any person in this district ever been offered anything for reading them."

---

The man of fifty-six put his hand on the stone, and about nine people in that yard looked at the place where his hand was, and about four of them had a fair idea of what was about to happen and about four of them did not.

"I have been the thing that anybody in this basin can rely on since before there was a lane with a figure at the end of it," he said, "and I have never been paid one copper, and I have never asked, and I would like the yard to hear the difference between those two things put next to each other, because I have listened to a woman with a scale say it out loud on the sixty-eighth morning and I have not been able to put it down since and I am not going to pretend this morning that I did not hear it."

---

"I am going to write that exactly as you said it," said Sabra Holt, "and I am going to write it as a fact and not as a fault, and I am going to write who said it, in the shape of a description, because you have not got a name in this district that anybody has ever used and I am not going to be the first and you have not asked me to be. And then I am going to tell you what I am going to do with it, because you have earned that and because you are the only person in this district who has earned it."

"Tell me."

"A person in a market yard in this basin states that he has read four figures off a wall twice a morning for five hundred and fourteen mornings, and that nobody has ever been paid for it, and that he has never asked to be." She shut the book. "That is going up that hill before that room sits and it is going to be read out in it, and I am going to be in the room, and the room is going to hear a district that has a man who can be relied on and cannot be paid, and the room will do what rooms do with a thing like that, and the room will be right to do it and the room will be wrong about the reason, and both of those are going to be in the same sentence and the second one is going to win."

---

"That is the fifth of the five," said the woman of fifty-eight, from the boards.

Everyone turned. She had not been in that yard for a whole morning before and she had come up the bank at about half past ten and had stood at the near end of the boards.

"Yes," said Sabra Holt, "and I am going to say the thing nobody else in this yard is going to say, and I would like it noticed that I am saying it and not having it said at me. That is the fifth of the five things this district does not have. A way to pay a person who is not in a household. It has been the fifth of the five on every morning of this book and it is the fifth of the five this morning. The count of the five is five. It is not paid. It is not going to be paid by anything said at that wall." She looked round the yard. "And I have been in this district eight mornings and it is the first time I have heard the fifth of the five said out loud as a fact about one person instead of a sentence about a place, and I would like the yard to notice that this morning the thing that is unpaid has a shape."

---

Nobody said anything for a while and the boy of seventeen came off the boards.

"I have a figure," he said, "and I have got two of them, and I am going to say both out loud before she writes either, and I would like the yard to know that I have decided which one she is going to write and that I have decided it while you were walking down that bank and I am not going to be argued out of it."

Sabra Holt put her pencil down. "Go on."

---

"Ninety-four mornings," said the boy. "That is how many mornings a man of thirty-eight has been in that yard, and that is what my page says, and I have got it off four years of my own sheet and off a second sheet I keep behind that one, and it is the same method I used on the fifty-fifth morning when I got seventy-six and told the yard my own method had a hole in it."

"And the other one."

"Ninety-one is what a person in this yard would count, and about four people in this yard would get ninety-one, and I have got ninety-one because he has been visible from those boards on ninety-one of the mornings, and the three that are missing are three mornings he was in that yard and not on the boards and not where a person standing up here would have seen him, and I know which three and I am not going to read them out because they are mine and not the yard's."

---

"Which one do you want me to write."

"Ninety-one," said the boy of seventeen.

---

The yard heard it and about nine people in it looked at Mara Quill, who had come down the bank at about eleven and had been standing about four feet off the end of the second table for the whole of it with her book shut.

"Write ninety-one," he said, "and write the reason, and the reason is that a boy of seventeen counted him twice this morning and gave you two figures and told you which one was wrong. And then write this, because I have thought about it for about four years and I am the only person in this district who can say it: I have said the right number out loud and I am not giving it to you, and I am not taking the wrong one off my page either, and both of those are decisions and one of them is about what is true and one of them is about what a person can be asked about afterwards, and I do not know which of them is the better one and I have never known and that is the whole of what I am."

---

Sabra Holt wrote for a while and then read it back to herself and not to the yard.

"I have written one figure and one reason and one sentence," she said, "and the sentence is *on the word of a boy of seventeen, who states the correct figure and does not offer it*, and I have never in nineteen years made an entry in which the number and the person disagree and the person is the more reliable of the two, and I am going to carry that sentence up that hill as well as the number, and I would like whoever reads it in that room to know that it is not a mistake in the arithmetic."

---

Adrian Vale had been about nine feet off the near end of the boards the whole time with his hands in his coat pockets and he said nothing at all, which is the thing that about nine people in that yard found hardest to believe and which about four people in that yard had expected.

He had the sentence in his mouth. He had the correct figure and he could have said it, and a man of fifty-six had read him a fifth figure out of nothing on the sixty-fourth morning and put him in a column nobody could check, and here was a page going up a hill with a figure on it that was three mornings wrong, and it was wrong in his favour, because a smaller number makes a man look more replaceable, and the whole of his life had been a man who could not look at a fact without putting a term on it and this was the first fact in about ninety-four mornings that he wanted to put a term on and could not, because the term would have been for him and there were about nineteen people in the yard.

He did not say it.

"You did not correct it," said Mara Quill, at about ten past four, coming up the bank beside him, and she said it quietly and in the ordinary voice and not to the yard.

"No."

"I could have done it from the front of my mouth. I have been a clerk for fifteen years and I have corrected about four hundred figures out loud and I have never once been asked to and I was not asked to this morning either, and I stood about four feet off that table the whole time with my book shut and I let a man of thirty-eight go into a woman's book three mornings wrong."

---

"Tell them that in a yard," said Adrian.

"No." She did not look at him. "I am not going to tell them that in a yard, and here is the reason. I have had it since the sixtieth morning, when I said a sentence to you on that lane that I have not been able to put down either, and it is a sentence about you and not about me, and I am going to use it against you for about four years, and I would like you to know I am using it. A thing you did to somebody is not a thing about you. If I stand up in that yard and say *I let him go in wrong and here is my reason*, then in about four years a man in a room is going to say *the clerk of Lot Seventeen let a man of thirty-eight into a record three mornings wrong, and she had a reason, and the reason was something he said to her on a lane*, and that is a sentence with you in the middle of it, and I will not do it. I have known since the sixtieth morning. I have said nothing about that for thirteen mornings, and on the seventieth I read a line off a page of my own out loud in this yard, and that was a different thing, and it went into the book before I read it. I am saying this now because you asked me to say it in a yard, and I am refusing, in the ordinary voice. I would like the boy of seventeen to hear me refuse it, because he is the only person in that yard who would understand the shape of what I have just refused."

---

The boy of seventeen did hear her, from about nine feet below the boards, and he did not write it down, and that was the fifth morning in a run and the first one in four years in which something had been said in that yard at about ten past four and not written by him, and he noticed that, and he noticed that he had noticed it, and he left the box alone.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and the clerk's pencil went into a book, and about four people repeated *as sent* under their breath, and a woman with a key wrote them down in a second book, and for the first time anybody in that yard can remember, two people wrote down the same four figures at the same time and neither of them said anything about the other one.
