# Chapter 786: The Seventieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Clerk Of That District Read Out Loud In A Yard What Was Written About A Man At The Foot Of A Wall And He Asked For The Asking To Be Put In As Well

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and a woman with a key has now been in the same yard as it for five mornings without asking it anything, and nobody in that yard has ever asked her about a figure, which is the reason she has got nothing out of five mornings in that yard. Five hundred and twenty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and fourteen days. Six hundred and seventy-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-four days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and eighty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and three days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and it did not move this morning although a clerk read a page out loud in that yard. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and a clerk read a page out loud in a yard this morning and that did not make a fifth, because a page a district already owns was not a new document this morning and has not been one since it was ruled. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid and has not been paid on any morning this book has run.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred mornings and moved this morning from ninety-nine, and nothing happened on the morning it went to a round figure and nobody said so. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-two and did not move, and it moves only when one comes down that hill with a page in their hand and a boy says the figure before a clerk moves her hand. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and eleventh of those mornings, and about four people in that yard this morning heard the second reading standing at the bottom of the bank instead of at the top.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-fifth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-four of them, and he had been told at about eleven o'clock in the morning what was written about him in a clerk's own book, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fifteenth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and for the first time the man of about sixty-four said it back before the other one had finished.

---

The boy of seventeen has been at the bottom of that bank for two mornings running, which is twice more than he came down it in the whole of the four years he has been counting anybody, and on the first morning he said good afternoon to a man on some stones and on the second morning he said nothing at all and stood about nine feet off and looked through the gap in the hedge, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet noticed and about four of them decided it was none of their business.

---

Mara Quill came down the bank at about a quarter to eleven with the lot book under her arm and stopped about four feet off the second table, and there were about nineteen people in that yard because Sabra Holt was in it and the man at the end of the second table had come up the bank, and nobody had called a meeting.

"I am going to read something out," she said, "and I am saying first that nobody asked me to and I have been thinking about doing it since the sixty-fifth morning and that the reason I have not done it is that I did not want to and the reason I am doing it this morning is that there is a woman with a key in that yard and I would rather it came from me in front of about nineteen people than from her in a room."

She did not open the book.

"It is not in the lot book. There is nothing on any page a stranger can walk up to and read. It is in a page of mine at the back of it with nothing ruled on it, and there are three lines on that page and none of the three are in the lot book, and it is the fourth line I have entered on a page of my own in about nine years and every one of them is about a person and not about a figure."

---

"Go on," said the man of about sixty-four, from the stones, without turning round.

He had heard her come down the bank. He had known somebody was coming down the bank for a minute and a half, because a man who has been on a wall for four hundred and twenty-five nights knows the difference between one person coming down eleven feet and another.

"It is what you told a road keeper and a boy of seventeen at the foot of that wall on the sixty-first morning," said Mara Quill, "and you told them it nobody had asked you for, and you said so out loud, and about nine people in that yard can now be asked about it. It is a lamp in a window lit at about half past four. It is a cart loaded from inside by between four and nine people. It is a blanket folded at a culvert on the eighth mile that somebody puts back on some nights and takes on others. It is also your reason for standing about two hundred yards off in a hedge for nine years, which is that you are not going to be a man standing in a road at half past five in the morning."

---

The yard did not make a noise.

"Which of those did you not ask me about," said the man of about sixty-four.

"None of them. I entered all four and I entered that you told two men and that you said that nobody had asked you, and I entered the reason you asked for, which is that a half a reason is worse than none, and then I entered the reason, and I did not ask you whether any of it was true or whether you had said it or whether you wanted it there." She turned a page over without looking at it. "And there is a fourth thing on that page and it is older than the other three. It is a figure of eleven seconds and it has been in that book for nine years and it is against a description of you and it came off a copy of a page in a corridor in a building nine miles up that hill, and you have never seen it and you have never been asked whether it is right."

---

He turned round on the stones, and it took him long enough that about four people in that yard stepped back from where they had not been standing.

"I have asked for it to be kept," he said.

"I know you have. You said so eleven mornings ago at the foot of that wall to a man of thirty-eight and a man of forty-three, and I was not in that yard and I was told about it afterward, and I did not enter any of it, and I am not entering any of it this morning."

"That is not what I meant." He put his hands on his knees. "I have been told about that figure since the moment you said the first sentence. I want to know one thing and it is the only thing I am going to ask anybody today, and I am asking it to a clerk and not to anybody else. Does that book say that I asked to keep it."

---

"No," said Mara Quill.

"Then it is a record of me and not of what I said," said the man of about sixty-four, "and I have been on a page for about nine years in a district of about nineteen people and a book, and nobody read it to me. That is the whole of what I have come down here for. I have no complaint against you and I want that said in a yard, and I want it said before I say the next part, because the next part is for her and not for you."

He looked up the eleven feet at Sabra Holt.

"I have been asked two questions in my life," he said. "One of them was asked by a man of about forty-three who was told about my nights for two years and came down that bank and asked me what the run was, and the other one I asked myself, and a man of thirty-eight answered it and did not know. And this morning I have been asked a third question, by a clerk, about a page, and it is the only one of the three that was about me, and there have been three questions in four hundred and twenty-five nights and two of them were not asked of me, and the whole of what I have learned in the three is how to answer when I am asked."

---

"What is the next part," said Sabra Holt.

"You asked me a question at eleven o'clock that was not about the lamp." He did not get up. "You asked me how many other people in this district are on a page they did not agree to. And everybody in this yard heard the question and about nine people in it looked at the woman of fifty-eight, and I have been on a wall for four hundred and twenty-five nights and I know what it looks like when about nine people look at one person, and I am telling you that you have just been told something and that I am the last person in this yard anybody should be taking it from, because I am the only one of the nine who is standing at the bottom of a wall."

---

"I have been told it," said Mavis Dorr.

She said it from the top of the bank. She had not come down. She had been in that yard since about half past nine because she had come to hear what was read out, which is the same reason about eleven other people had come, and nobody had said so.

"It is nine names," she said. "I named nine holders of the nine coppers out loud in that yard twelve mornings ago and I told him the price before I said them, and the clerk entered no name, and the price was that anybody who hears a name said in a yard can be asked about it afterwards. The ninth is Nell Cray. She is two miles from here and she is on a working list in a corridor nine miles up that hill and she has never been asked about anything by anybody, and about four people in this yard have found her and about four people have not, and the man of fifty-six is right that I did not know whether that was a good idea and I still do not."

---

"I can find her," said Sabra Holt.

She said it standing at the top of eleven feet with a book in her own hand and she said it the way she had said *nineteen days*, without any weight on it at all.

"I am not saying that to frighten anybody and I am not saying it to threaten anybody. I am telling you what an appointed administrator with the third of nine keys can do, because before that room sits somebody with a key is going to be asked whether the district of Lot Seventeen can produce a person for a term, and the honest answer is going to be that it cannot ask one of the people it represents, and the question that will follow in a room is *why not*, and the answer to that is going to be a list of nine names, and eight of them can be found by anybody who walks down a hill."

---

Mavis Dorr sat down on the boards of the first table, which she has not done in that yard for longer than anybody standing there could remember, and about four people moved out of her way without being asked.

"Then find her," she said.

Nobody in that yard had expected it and about nine people in it made a noise of one sort or another.

"Do not do that as a favour," said Mavis Dorr. "Do it as a Council and not as a kindness, because if you do it as a kindness then in about four years somebody will say that a woman with a key went into a corridor nine miles up a hill and asked a person on a working list a question because a woman of fifty-eight in a market yard said her name out loud in front of about nineteen people, and that is a sentence with a person in the middle of it, and I would rather it were a sentence with a key in the middle of it."

---

"I will do it as a Council and I will do it in writing, and I will do it whether or not anybody here agrees with me, and I would like the yard to notice that I have just said that in a yard." Sabra Holt shut her book. "I am going to ask one person a question in a few mornings and I am going to ask it her and not you, because a person who was named in a yard is owed being asked by somebody who is not the yard. That is not a promise I can make stick and I am making it anyway."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and the man of about sixty-four was not one of the people who heard the second one, because he was on the stones at the time and about nine feet of low wall was between that yard and him, and he heard four figures come over the top of it and could not make out the words.

Mara Quill entered the asking.

She entered it at about half past four, on the page at the back of the lot book with nothing ruled on it, in her own hand, in the ordinary voice, and she read it out before she wrote it, and it was the first line anybody in that district has entered in a year at the word of the man it was about, and it went on the fourth line of a page that is not in the lot book and there are now four lines on that page, and the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and the count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a boy of seventeen wrote the morning in the box on the back of his own sheet and did not write what was on it.

---

The man of about sixty-four got up off the stones at about a quarter past four and picked his coat up and did not put it on.

"I go out at about half past four," he said, to nobody in particular, and about nine people in that yard heard it. "I have gone out at about half past four for nine years and I am going out at about half past four tonight, and this morning a woman read out to about nineteen people what is written about me, and none of it has changed the four o'clock. I would like somebody in this yard to notice that, because it is the only thing about this morning I am able to say out loud, and I have had the whole of the day to think of something better."
