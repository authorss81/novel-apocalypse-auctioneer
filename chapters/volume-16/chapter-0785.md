# Chapter 785: The Sixty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Boy Of Seventeen Refused A Woman With A Page In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People And Then Asked Her To Write Down That He Had Refused

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and nobody in this district has ever asked it anything and there is a woman with a key three hundred yards off it who has now been in the same yard as it for four mornings, and those are the only two things anybody did about that figure in that yard yesterday. Five hundred and twenty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-three. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and thirteen days. Six hundred and seventy-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-three days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and eighty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and two days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and stands. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and a rule and a refusal and a page in a Council's book are three of the four and not one of the four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a rule was made out loud in that yard yesterday morning and is not a payment, and a boy said no in that yard this morning and is not a payment either.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-nine mornings and moved this morning from ninety-eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-nine mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-nine, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and it did not move yesterday when a rule was made out loud in that yard, and it will not move this morning when a boy says no in it, because a thing four people made is not a thing two people did in a yard. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and there is no more in it this morning than there was yesterday morning.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and tenth of those mornings, and he was in the yard for both of them this morning, and about nine people in that yard heard the second one, and he said afterwards that it was the first morning in five hundred and ten that anybody had heard the second one who was not about to write it down.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-fourth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-three of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the fourteenth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of sixty-one said it first again, and the man of about sixty-four said it back, and the whole of what has happened between those two men in fourteen afternoons fits in those two words and both of them know it and neither of them has said so.

---

"I have to say the reason," said Orren Pike, "because you are going to write down that I said no, and a no on its own is a decoration, and I have spent four years finding out what those are worth."

"Go on," said Sabra Holt.

"You are going to write down that a boy of seventeen in a market yard has been in that yard every morning for four years and has heard everything said in it, and that boy is me, and the thing you are going to call a witness is a person who has understood the terms." He had the sheet in his hand and he was not reading it. "I have understood them the way you understand the weather. I know there is a ninth line. I know it wants one person and one figure and one length of time and the money has to be there on the day. I have been able to say those things back to you since I was about thirteen. And if I stood up there this morning and said *yes, I understood the terms*, you would carry a boy up that hill who has never once been asked what any of it costs, and that is not a witness. That is a person standing near a thing, and a man of sixty-one told a man of thirty-eight exactly that sentence at the bottom of a wall ten mornings ago, and it was the only sentence anybody said down there that I have thought about every morning since."

---

"Say the rest of it," said the man of fifty-six, from about nine feet off the wall.

"There is a row on my page," said the boy.

He said it in a different voice and about four people in that yard came a step nearer the boards without deciding to.

"There is a row on my own sheet with a word in the first column where a name goes. It is not a name. It is about a person in this yard and he is in this yard at this moment and I am not going to say his name out loud and I have not said it out loud. I have had that row in front of me going back about four years and I noticed what was wrong with it on the forty-third morning, which is twenty-six mornings back, when I was on these boards and I was working out what to do about it, and I worked out what to do about it, and I did not do it, and I walked away from it up that lane, and I have not been able to make myself do it since."

---

"You are talking about me," said the man at the end of the second table.

"Yes."

"That row is mine and I put it there through you about two years ago and I told you so in this yard seven mornings ago and I asked for it to be kept and it was read out and it was entered nowhere." The man did not move. "So what you have just done in a yard this morning is put a second thing about me next to a first thing about me, and you have done it in front of a woman with a key, and you have done it because you were asked a question about a term, and I would like to know what you think the difference is."

---

"The difference is that the first one was in my own page and this one is in a stranger's," said the boy. "And I have been trying for twenty-six mornings to work out how to say this and there is one way and it is the whole of it. If I say yes up that hill, then in about four years somebody is going to ask me what I saw on the forty-third morning. And I saw it. And I did not write it down. And a man is going to make me say that in a room, and I will have to say it, and I will be a boy of twenty-one who has stood in a yard and taken a witness's place with a word in a column where a name goes that I noticed and passed over. That is the whole of why I cannot be your witness. It is not that I have not understood the terms. It is that I have got one thing in my life that I saw and did not write down, and I cannot carry a person up a hill who has got that in him."

---

Nobody in that yard said anything for a while, and about four people looked at the man at the end of the second table, and he looked at the boards.

"Then say the word," he said. "In a yard. This morning. Now that there is a woman here who will have it and a clerk with a pencil and about nineteen people who can be asked, and say the word is me, and let it be entered somewhere, and then you can go up that hill in a week with your two hands clean."

"That is exactly what I am not going to do," said the boy, "and I would like somebody to write down that I said that as well, because in about four years it is going to sound like arrogance and it is the opposite. If I say his name now, in a yard, with a key in it, then the row gets a name in it, and the row is a row, and the row stops being the thing I noticed. And the only reason I have never told anybody is that the row is the only part of that page that says *look again* instead of *this is settled*. You cannot want it to be a name. The second you want it to be a name it is a name and it is settled and it is the most ordinary thing in this yard and it is worth nothing to anybody nine miles up a hill."

---

Sabra Holt had her book out of her own coat by then and it was open on her forearm.

"I am going to write down two facts," she said. "One: that a person in this yard said no to being a witness and gave a reason, and the reason is his. Two: that a person in this yard said there is a row on a page of his own where a word stands in a place a name goes, and that he noticed it twenty-six mornings back and did not write it down, and that the row is about a person standing in this yard." She looked up. "I am not going to write a name and neither of you is going to give me one, and if either of you does I am going to leave it out, and I would like that said now so that neither of you is doing me a favour later."

---

"Enter it," said the boy of seventeen.

Mara Quill had the lot book open on the boards before he had finished the sentence.

"I am not entering it," she said. "And I want the yard to hear the reason, because the reason is the best thing that instrument of mine has ever done and it cost a boy his road in the first week it was written. That rule was made on the forty-fourth morning and I wrote it and I argued for it and I would write it again. A paper a person is carrying may be written on by anybody standing near them when a thing is said out loud, and it may not be entered in that book by anybody, including me. His sheet is his sheet. That is not a figure in the rule, that is the whole of the rule, and I wrote it in about nine words and a boy made me write it. And I am not entering his refusal either, and the reason is not the rule."

---

"Then what is the reason," said the man who digs loam.

"That a woman with a key has it in a book nine miles from here that nobody in this yard can get at, and that my rule was never about what is good for this district." She shut the lot book. "It was about what a person can refuse. And there is nothing in it that stops him refusing me and asking her instead. If I entered it as well then he would be in two books, and a person in two books cannot get out of either one, and I have watched what that does to about four people in about nine mornings and I have never once been able to put it right afterwards."

---

"Then it is mine and not yours," said Orren Pike, "and I have to tell you what it costs, because you are a person with a key and you have been honest for three days and I would like to hand you a true thing instead of a grateful one."

"Go on."

"You came down that lane four mornings ago and the lane count moved to twenty-two and I said it out loud before she wrote it, and you have been in this yard three mornings without asking anybody for anything, and everybody in this yard has spent those three mornings working out what you want. This morning you have got it, and I have said no, and the no is in your book, and I am seventeen, and I am the only person in this district that you have asked to do a thing and who has refused you, and that is going to travel." He put his hands flat on the boards. "I have been findable since the fifty-ninth morning because I said yes to things out loud and I counted them. I have never once been findable because I refused somebody, and I did not know what that was like, and I would like it noted that I worked it out while I was standing here."

---

"I will write one line about that too," said Sabra Holt, "and it will not be flattering, because I do not flatter. I will write that a boy of seventeen in this yard refused to be a witness, gave a reason that was his own and not an instruction, and named the reason, which was a thing he had seen and had not written down. And I will write the second half of it, which is that a person in this yard said that a record he has kept for four years is the only instrument anybody in this basin has that cannot be used to keep him out of a thing, and that he said that in front of a woman holding one of the oldest keys, and that I am not going to pretend I did not hear what he was actually doing."

---

"What was he actually doing," said the man of fifty-six.

"He was showing me what he is, before I decided what to write about it." She shut her book. "A person who has an instrument and no name on it is not a person you can use. That is either the most useful thing anybody has shown me in four days or the cleverest, and I have not worked out which, and I am not going to work it out in a yard, and I am going to go up that lane now with both halves of it in the book."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and the boy of seventeen wrote the morning and the four figures on the back of his own sheet and entered it nowhere.

Then he got down off the boards and went down the eleven feet of that bank for the first time in about nine mornings, and stood about nine feet off a man of about sixty-four at the foot of a low wall, and said good afternoon out loud, and had no idea at all what he was doing there.
