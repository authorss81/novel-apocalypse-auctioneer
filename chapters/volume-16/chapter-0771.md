# Chapter 771: The Fifty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Boy Of Seventeen Counted A Man For The Second Time In His Life And Got A Different Answer

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it did not move. Five hundred and ten days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and sixty-nine, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and eighty-five days, and nobody has entered anything for six hundred and ninety-nine mornings, which is to say that a board in this district has not been written on since before the year before last and there is a man in Alder Reach who has read that figure every morning of the last nine years and has never once wondered who is keeping it from moving.

Six hundred and sixty days separate the second of January and this morning. Four hundred and nineteen days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and seventy-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and nothing correct has been written beside it.

Three hundred and seventy-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and forty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and eighty-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twenty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and fourteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move, and the fifth would be a shop counter. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank eighty-five mornings and moved this morning from eighty-four. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for thirty-five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it, because the man of fifty-six forbade it in a yard and the yard agreed. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is fifty-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-one and did not move this morning, and the twenty-first of those is a man with a cart who stopped four feet below the top of that bank about six mornings ago because a boy asked him to, and not one of the mornings since has a stranger come down that lane with a page in its hand, and nobody in this yard has decided what to make of that.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and ninety-sixth of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundred and tenth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and nine of them, and a clerk's own book has a line in it about him and a lot book has nothing, and he has been asked one question in four hundred and ten nights and has answered it, and he has not been told that it was written down.

---

Orren Pike was on the boards of the first table at about a quarter to eight with the sheet open on his knee, and Adrian had been in the yard since about seven and had been watching him not do it for three quarters of an hour.

"You have been sitting there a long time," Adrian said.

"I have been sitting there a long time."

"You had the sheet open on your knee yesterday and you did it for about a minute, and today you have had it open since before I got up the bank, and that is a different length of time and I would like to know what it is before you say it."

The boy turned the sheet round without being asked, which he had done twice in a fortnight now and had never done in four years before that.

"Seventy-six," he said.

---

The clerk's pencil was about four inches off the page. She did not write it and she did not say anything for long enough that a woman at the top of that bank stopped doing what she had been doing.

"Say the number again," Adrian said.

"Seventy-six." The boy had his pencil down on the boards, not in his hand. "I counted it on the boards. Every morning for four years I have counted the mornings that a person was on this bank, and I counted it out of my own page, and on the fifty-fourth morning I stood up here and said seventy in front of about nineteen people and I did not take fifty off it, and fifty is not wrong, and seventy is what was there when I finished counting."

"Both of those can be true."

"They cannot," the boy said. "Not both of those. That is what I have got wrong and it has taken me a night and a half and I have not slept."

---

He had brought a piece of paper folded in four, and he opened it on the boards, and it was the back of his own sheet and there were figures on it in pencil, and the figures were in a column, and there were no names.

"One morning since I said seventy," the boy said, "and I have gone back through my own page for the mornings I was in this yard and not on these boards, because on the fifty-fourth morning I made a box on the back in pencil for mornings I was *present* and I put it in for you and then I would not enter it anywhere, and the box has been sitting there ever since with six mornings in it that are not counted by the thing I counted with."

"What six mornings."

The boy turned the paper about a quarter of the way round so that Adrian could see it without coming to his side of the boards.

"This one," he said, and put a finger on the top figure. "The morning the page came back down that lane. I was behind the counter in the shop with a weight on the door, holding it flat while she read it, and I was in this yard in the sense that the lane is this yard, and I was about nine feet from you the whole time."

"That is a long way from this yard."

"It is nine hundred yards and it is the same morning," the boy said, "and that is the first thing and it is the smallest thing, and if you want to argue with me about it then argue about it after I have said the other five, because if I say them in the wrong order you will only pick the one you can pick."

---

He said the other five.

He had been at the foot of that bank on four mornings, on the second table's near side, four feet off the end of it, on the two mornings when a man had said a thing out loud that the man he was about was in the yard for, and on the two mornings when he had been at the top of that bank in the gap where the boards give, looking down at a man's hands and not at the boards.

"And the sixth one is at the top of the lane," the boy said, "at about a quarter to eight on the morning a page went up that hill in a cart, and I was standing in the lane watching the cart go, and you were in this yard, and I was not, and if you are about to tell me that the lane is not the yard then I would like you to say it in that form and not in any other form."

---

Adrian stood very still for about the length of a walk down to the second table and back.

"Where does seventy-six come from," he said. "Out loud, with the arithmetic."

"Seventy, and six." The boy did not put the pencil away. "And I am not taking seventy off it. Seventy was true on the morning I said it and seventy-six is true this morning and the difference between them is six mornings, and one of those six is yesterday and one of those six was a fortnight ago and four of them are inside eleven mornings, and I would like it entered that I found the hole in my own method before somebody with a better method found it in me, because that is the whole of what I have got and I would like somebody in this yard to hear me say it."

---

Then he turned to the wall, where the man of fifty-six had been standing long enough for the light to change on the near side of his coat, with his back to all of it and his hand flat on the stone.

"I would like you to say the number," the boy said. "I have to say that part out loud first, because I have been in this yard four years and I have just found out that I have not got a name for you and never had one."

"No," said the man of fifty-six, without turning round.

"You said on the fiftieth morning that you would do it if somebody asked you in a yard."

"I did say it." The man took his hand off the stone. "And on the fifty-fourth morning a boy asked me and I said it, and it is the first figure I have read in my life off anything that was not that wall, and I have not been able to put it down since, and that is the first morning of it, and I said five mornings ago that I was going to be frightened for about a year, and I meant it, and I did not think it would start with a boy standing on some boards. I did not say for about eleven days."

He turned round. He looked at the boy and not at the sheet.

"You have been the only person in this yard saying numbers out loud since I found out that I could," he said, "and I have watched you do it three times in eleven mornings and every one of them was a figure about a person, and I read yours because a boy stood on some boards and asked me to and I am not going to be asked twice inside a fortnight by a boy I have never once refused anything. That is the whole of the reason and it is not a good one and I would rather you had it than not."

"Then you will not say it."

"I will not say it," said the man of fifty-six, "and I would like it noticed that I did not say *no*. That is a different word and this yard has had a great deal of it this month."

---

Adrian did not go down the bank for about an hour, and when he went down it was at ten and he stood four feet further on than where he stops, which is the end of the second table, and he noticed that he had done it and did not think about it until the man noticed.

"I want to ask you the hardest thing anybody has asked anybody in this yard," Adrian said, "and I am telling you what I want first, because you taught me that on the forty-third morning and I have not stopped using it."

"Go on."

"A page has come back down that lane wanting a name, a figure and a term, and the name is mine and it is in a column and it cannot come out, and the figure is forty coppers for a year and a man in this yard said it out loud and cannot pay it. The term is the only one of the three I have ever believed I could answer. A term is a year with money on it and then it stops, and the only way to stop one is for the people it covers to say out loud that they will start it again."

The man at the far end of that table did not move, and neither did the spade against the leg of it.

"So I have to ask about nineteen people in this yard whether they consent to a year," Adrian said, "and there is no instrument in this district that can ask nineteen people anything."

---

"There is one," the man said.

"There is not."

"There is one and you have been using it for eleven mornings and you do not like the shape of it," the man said. "It is a yard. That is the instrument. There is no other one and there is not going to be one in your time and if you spend the rest of your life making one, this yard will carry nine coppers for a year in a bag."

"Then tell me how to use it."

"I have used it," the man said. "I have been in four yards in this fortnight and every one of them made somebody findable, and one of them was me, and I told you the price when I put it in the margin and I am telling you the same price again in a different weather. You cannot ask a question in this yard without the answer going into about nine mouths, and about nine people can be asked about the answer afterwards, and that is not a flaw in the yard. That is what a yard is."

"Then how does anybody ever consent to anything."

"In a room," the man said, "one at a time, with the person, over about nine years. That is the entire of it. That is why a district cannot send anybody to a room and that is why a district can ask nineteen people a question and cannot ask one of them whether they meant it."

---

Adrian put his hands where the man could see them, which was an old habit from a life spent standing in front of frightened rooms, and which the man at the end of that table had watched him do on every single morning in eleven without ever once saying so without ever once having been the man in front of whom it was done.

"There is a woman in this yard who goes up that bank at ten and down it at ten to six and carries for people who are not on anything," Adrian said, "and she has a name, and she gave it out loud on the fifty-fourth morning, and she is the only person I know of who could go into about nineteen kitchens and come back with a yes or a no that means something."

"I know who she is."

"I am going to ask her to do it."

"No you are not."

---

"You do not get to tell me that."

"You do not get to tell me that either, and that is the difference between us and it is the only one and it has been the only one for eleven mornings," the man said. "You are going to ask her, and you are going to tell her what you will do with the answer afterwards, because that is what you did on the fiftieth morning and it is the one thing you do well. And you are going to stand there and find out afterwards what it cost her, because you do not know what it costs, because you have never once in your life been the person a thing was done to in order to get a thing done."

"She will want it said out loud before she says yes."

"She will want it said out loud before she says yes," the man agreed, "and you will say it, and you will say it well, and then you will go up that bank at ten to six and forget it by the time you have got your coat off, and she will not remind you, and that is how it will be done."

---

The clerk was at the near end of the boards at about eleven with her book shut in front of her and her hands flat on it, and Adrian came back up the bank and stood about nine feet off the end of it and did not sit down.

"Say the three things," she said.

"A name, a figure and a term."

"Say the fourth thing."

Adrian looked at her.

"A page has come back down that lane wanting a name, a figure and a term," he said, "and it came back down that lane yesterday afternoon and I have treated it as a list of three things to be found ever since, and it is four, and the fourth one is that the term has to be agreed to by about nineteen people and there is no instrument in this district that can ask them, and a man at the end of a table has just told me the only one there is and it is this yard and it is the thing that has made six people findable in eleven mornings."

Mara Quill did not write that down. He noticed, because he had spent eleven mornings learning what she wrote down.

"No," she said.

"You are the clerk of this district."

"I am the clerk of this district," she said, "and I did not write it down because a man said it out loud in a yard at about eleven on a morning when about nine people heard him, and if that is what goes in my book then in about four years anybody can say a thing near a table and make it a fact about nineteen people, and I have been the clerk of that yard for fifteen years and I have never once done it and I am not going to learn it this morning."

She put her hand flat on the cover.

"Go and ask her," she said. "And when you have asked her, come back and tell me what she said, and do not come back and tell me what you thought she meant, because I have been getting that one for five mornings and I have had quite enough of it."
