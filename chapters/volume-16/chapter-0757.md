# Chapter 757: The Forty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Who Puts Tables Up Was Not There And Four People Drew A Map And Left Out The Only Place Anybody Had Been Looking At

He was not there.

That is four words and it took up the whole morning, and Adrian understood by about half past nine that this yard ran on that man's absences the way a body runs on a limb, and that in nine years nobody had ever found out because he had never once been missing.

The clerk read the morning out, from the boards, without looking up, in the voice she used for figures.

"Four hundred and ninety-six days is the age of the figure on the sheet at that gatepost. The figure is four hundred and eleven and it did not move. The board carries six hundred and fifty-five, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-one days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-five days, and six hundred and forty-six days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of mornings that second table has stood at the foot of that bank is seventy-one and it moved this morning from seventy. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for twenty-one mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-one and it did not move this morning."

Four hundred and five days is how long that bid has been open, and it was not run, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and sixty days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it. Three hundred and sixty-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and that counts the mornings that have gone by and not the mornings a month has in it. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-eight days back, it is open, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-four days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fourteen marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are three hundred marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-second of those mornings, and he did it in the ordinary voice, and nobody in this yard has ever asked him why the ordinary voice.


She went along that line and stopped where it stopped.

"The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-one," she said, and she read it off the page instead of off the top of her head, which was not a thing she had ever once had to do before this morning. "And it did not move this morning. And he was not at the foot of that bank this morning at all."

Adrian read it over her shoulder and said nothing, because at about a quarter to eight that morning it had been true, and a sentence that is true at a quarter to eight and false at about eleven is not a sentence anybody in that yard had ever had to think about, and the woman of fifty-eight had stood four feet off the fourth line of that book every working morning for fifteen years reading a line that had never once changed under her feet.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his three hundred and ninety-sixth night of that run, having slept on three hundred and ninety-five of them, and by the time the books were shut that afternoon there was a page face up on a trestle table nine feet above his head with a hole in it, and nobody looked down.


"Enter the reason," Orren said.

"I am entering the reason."

"Enter it *well*." The boy was reading over her shoulder, which he is not supposed to do. "A man who keeps a tally put that in at seven this morning and did not say that. He put in a figure. You took the figure. Neither of you went up that hill and looked, and now the page says a thing that is not true on the face of the page, and the page is going to say it tomorrow as well."

"Then what do you want me to put," the clerk said, "because there are three possibilities and two of them are ugly. I can put *he was not there*, which is a reason I have not got a source for. Or I can put *he was not there as far as anybody in this yard knows*, which is true and is nine words long. Or I can leave the number alone and let a figure sit on a page for a morning after the fact."

"Put the middle one."

She wrote it. Then she sat and looked at what she had written for about a minute and a half, and Adrian, who had spent fifty mornings learning to read that particular stillness, understood that she had just been shown a defect in her own instrument by a boy of seventeen and had taken it.

---

He had come up that morning with a job. It was gone by about eleven.

The notice asks a market to send one person, one figure and one term, and Adrian had assumed, on the way up the hill, that a market is something that exists. He had said so to a man of fifty-six in the last four mornings and had been wrong every time, and on this morning he sat down on the boards of that first table with his elbows on his knees and tried to write four lines saying what Lot Seventeen was, in order to say it out loud to a room in Alder Reach, and he got as far as the first line and stopped.

*A market, said Adrian out loud, to nobody, at about eleven on the forty-first morning, is a thing that has—*

"You haven't got a word after *has*," the clerk said.

"A market is a thing that has some goods and some buyers and a place they agree to meet."

"That's a market *square*," she said. "That's not what we are." She put her pencil down. "We have a book with five lines on it and a wall with four numbers on it and a second table at the bottom of a bank with a stone on it, and you have been here fifty mornings and you could not write me four lines this morning because there is nothing to write. We are a *place where four numbers get read out twice a day by one man that nobody has asked why.* That's it. That's the whole of the business and it isn't even a sentence anybody has said out loud before, and it is not a market, and the reason you can't write it down is that it has got no second person in it."

Adrian put his forehead on his forearms for about four seconds.

"Then draw it," he said.

---

It took the back of the morning and the whole of the afternoon, and the argument was the useful part.

Adrian had said *draw it* as though it were an instruction. By about ten past eleven it had become four people standing at the near end of those boards with a stranger's page turned to the blank side and none of them agreeing about anything, and it stayed like that until the light came off that wall, which is the longest single thing that has happened in that yard in nine years.

"Who's doing what," Adrian said, for the third time.

"I'm doing the top," the man of fifty-six said. "The yard, the boards, the wall. It's the only part I know from inside and from about two hundred feet away, and there are four figures on that wall and I am not going to write them as a mark, because a mark goes up and a figure goes one a morning and those are different animals and I'd like the paper to know that."

Somebody said, "Then I'm doing the legs."

Everybody looked at the near end of that table, and the near end of that table was empty, and it had been empty since before eight.

And not one person at the near end of those boards said one word about the fact that a page at the top of that bank was now carrying a sentence that was not true, and the clerk was one of them, and she was the one person in that yard who had spent the whole morning being careful about the difference between a figure and a reason.

"He's not —" the clerk began.

"He came down at about half past ten," the man of fifty-six said, "and he said *legs*, and he did not say anything else, and he has gone back up." He turned back to the paper. "I've been on that bank for nine years and I have watched that man take a rail off a table in his own time with nothing in his hands and no pay, and if you lot are drawing this place then somebody's going to do the legs and it will not be me, because I cannot draw a leg. I can tell you that a leg is there. I cannot tell you that it is about four foot."

Adrian looked at the far end of the bank.

"Who's the man at the end of that table," he said. "Do we know his name?"

The clerk did not answer, which was an answer, and Adrian let it go, because he had learned that week that letting it go was sometimes the only available move and that it was going to cost him.

The woman of fifty-eight said no first and out loud, which nobody expected.

"No," she said. "Not me. I've spoken that fifth line for fifteen years and I'm not drawing the yard." She set her bag down on the boards. "I'll tell you what I'll do, and I'll do it once, and then I'm going down that bank. Put a mark where the third line's man stands at eleven o'clock. That's the only fixed thing I've got. It's fixed because it's the same place every morning for fifteen years. And put the fifth line under it, because that line goes where he goes."

"You could do the foot," Adrian said.

"Don't be kind at me in a yard," the woman said. "Somebody's got to draw the top and the top's where the book is."

---

The woman who keeps a scale said she had a shop and a counter and three pages on it and no time, which was true and did not matter, because nobody was asking her for time.

"You've drawn the top of that bank," the man of fifty-six said, about an hour later, "which is the yard and the boards and the wall and is about a third of the paper."

"It's a third of the *people*," the woman said. "Nineteen people, and sixteen of them come up that hill, and eleven of them are in my shop, and one of them is on that lane twice a day, so draw the hill, and the hill's a third of the map, and it isn't my fault that this district does its living at the top of a slope."

"I'm not saying draw less of the hill. I'm saying I can't tell you what's at the bottom of it."

"I know you can't. Draw the third line's man at eleven o'clock and the fifth line under it and that *is* the bottom of it."

"It's a man standing at a table," the man of fifty-six said. "There's no line for the table's legs."

"There are legs on that table and they came off a cart and one of them is about a foot longer than the other because that cart's near wheel drags, and if you're drawing this place properly then that foot is on the paper, and that's a real thing and I've watched it for nine years."

"That is not what a map is."

"That," said the woman who keeps a scale, "is the first thing anybody in this district has said to me about my trade that isn't a question about the price of something."

The man who puts tables up did the legs of everything that stood up, which is not what a map is, and which turned out to be the most useful offer anybody made, because legs are what you find out last and then cannot fit without starting again. He had gone back up about eleven and he was on it again before twelve and he was still on it at about three, and the four of them had not once agreed about anything in the whole of the four hours of it.

And the man of fifty-six did the top, because he was the only person in that yard who could stand in one place and say out loud what was in every other place, and having said four figures off that wall four hundred and eighty-two mornings he had the whole shape of the district in a place nobody had thought to look.

And there was a hole in it about four inches across, on the bottom edge, in the middle of the page.

"There were four of us drawing it," the man of fifty-six said, about two o'clock, when Adrian asked him about the hole, "and I can tell you exactly why there is a hole in it. Every one of us was standing at the top of that bank, or in this yard, or in the shop, or on that lane. In nine years I have walked down that hill about nine hundred times and I have never once stopped, and neither has anybody else. There are about nineteen people in this district and not one of us is ever at the bottom of that bank at eleven o'clock except one man with a spade." He put one finger on the hole. "We drew it from the only place we could stand, and there is a hole in it, and the hole is the only part of this district that anybody has worked on for a month."

---

At about half past three the clerk came down the bank with her page flat against her chest, because a thing that four people had made had to go into the book by somebody and there was only her.

"The count of things this district has made is fourteen," she said, "and it moved this morning from thirteen. It did not move at a quarter to eight this morning, and that is the rule, and I have kept it for fifteen years, and the reason it did not move at a quarter to eight this morning is that at a quarter to eight this morning four people were standing at the top of a bank with a piece of paper and had not said one word to each other about it." She wrote it. "It moved because four people drew a map on the back of a stranger's page, and one of the four is a man of fifty-six and one of them is a woman of fifty-eight and one of them is a woman who keeps a scale, and the fourth is a man who puts tables up, who did the legs of it, and who has not been thanked for that and will not be. And the map has a hole in it, and I have entered the reason for the hole, because I am now entering reasons."

Then she said it out loud, in the yard, in the voice she used for figures.

"The count of the five is still five."

---

At about four, in the shop at the top of the bank, the woman who keeps a scale put her elbows on the counter with the map on it, and Adrian came in out of the wet.

"Nine years I've been on that bank," she said. "Nobody's ever asked me one thing. Not the clerk, not you, and not the man in the coat with the fence wire, whose name you gave us in a yard on the thirty-fifth morning and which I have not used because I did not hear it from you."

"You heard it in a yard."

"I heard it from about thirty feet and a woman with a cart went past," she said. "Ask me about the three pages on my counter."

Adrian stopped.

"You've never mentioned the three pages."

"I've been on this bank nine years," she said, "and I have said the words *three pages* out loud about nine hundred times and not one of you lot has ever asked me a single question about them, and I've got about four minutes before I have to shut this counter and count the till, and I can't talk and think at the same time. Go on, then. Ask me."
