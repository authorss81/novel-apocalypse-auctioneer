# Chapter 761: The Forty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Rule Was Read Out Loud In That Yard The Morning Before And The First Person To Put It To Use Found A Hole In The Only Column That Mattered

The first thing about the forty-fifth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month was that four people looked up that lane before eight, and they did it separately, and not one of them said to another that they were doing it.

A page had gone up it the afternoon before, in the hands of a man with a cart that pulls to one side, and it had been the first thing this district had sent up that hill in nine years, and the lane had done nothing at all since.

Adrian came up the bank at ten past eight with the wire still in the pocket of his coat and the morning already in his head, because he had been doing the sum since half past five and had got a different answer twice, and about nine people had already been up that lane and back down it.

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. Five hundred days is the age of that figure.

He had come to think of those as two separate questions, in the way a man comes to think of a thing that has stopped being one thing. He had never once put them in one breath with each other, and he could not have told Mara Quill why, and she could not have told him either, and she had put the question in writing three times in nine years and had been sent four words back that added up to nothing.

The board carries six hundred and fifty-nine. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-five days. Nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-nine days. Six hundred and fifty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and nine days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and sixty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and sixty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood, and he had spent nine years being the reason that figure moved, and had never once thought about what it moved.

Four hundred and thirty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and forty-two days back, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and eighteen marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are three hundred and four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move, and a shop counter is a place and is not on that number. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five, and it was not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank seventy-five mornings and moved this morning from seventy-four. The near rail has been on the near side of it for twenty-five mornings. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and it has not moved on any morning since a map was drawn on the back of a stranger's page by four people who could not agree about anything.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-sixth of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundredth night of that run, having slept on three hundred and ninety-nine of them, and nobody in that yard went down the bank to ask him anything.

---

The clerk had the boards to herself from a quarter to eight until about nine, and she was still there when Adrian arrived, and she had not looked up, and he stood about nine feet off the near end of the first table and waited, because he had worked out in three weeks that this was the only hour in the district in which anything could be said to anybody.

"Two things," he said.

"You've been up that hill since half past five."

"I've been up that hill since half past five."

"Then you'll have two things and one of them will be a figure and one of them will be a feeling, and I'll take the figure first, because the feeling can wait and it usually does."

"Four hundred and nine," Adrian said. "The bid. And nothing this morning proposed closing it, and I want that entered as a positive, not as an absence."

She wrote it. She had been entering reasons since the forty-first morning and she had got fast at it and slightly worse at it, which he suspected was the ordinary price of a new habit.

"And the second thing."

"The lane is quiet," Adrian said. "Nothing's come down it. A page went up yesterday afternoon at about half past four and there is no rule anywhere in this district about what happens to a page that has gone the wrong way, and I have checked, and the only person who could tell me is the man who kept it."

"Eleven hours is nothing," the clerk said. "You'd be a fool to look."

"I'm going to look for nine more of them."

"You'd be a fool to look for nine of them," she said, "and then you'd be a fool to stop, and I have watched a man do that to himself for nine years and it is not a pretty thing to sit next to."

---

Orren came up the bank at about ten and went past everybody, the way he went past everybody, and stopped four feet off the near end of the boards and turned round so his back was to them.

"Nineteen," he said. "The number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand is nineteen and it did not move, and the number of pages that have gone *up* that lane is one, and those are two different numbers and I want somebody to say out loud that they are two different numbers, because in a week somebody is going to work out that one of them is a count of what comes in and one of them is a count of what goes out, and then there will be a rule, and the rule will be wrong, and I would like it on the record this morning that I said it first."

"You're seventeen," the clerk said.

"I'm seventeen and I've got a page."

"Entered," she said, and wrote it, and did not write a name, and never did.

---

The rule had been read out loud in that yard at about four the afternoon before, which is the only time anything has ever been read out in it that was not on a wall or in a book, and a man of fifty-six had read it because there was no other arrangement, and he had asked a question about it first, which is what he does, and he had got four lines out and then read the four figures off the wall as well, and about nine people in that yard had gone still in the middle of it.

Nobody quoted it back at Adrian. That was the first thing he noticed. Two readings of a thing in one afternoon and by the next morning about eleven people had it in their mouths and not one of them had it right, and one of them had it in a version with a dog in it that had not been in the original.

---

The first person to use the rule was the woman of fifty-eight, and she used it at about half past three, at the foot of that bank, with the bag at her feet and about nine people standing about forty feet further down the hill because the book is at the bottom of the bank now and she goes down it at ten to six and reads three lines standing up four feet off the fourth one.

"I have used it," she said. "Once. This morning. And I am going to read it out, and then the clerk is not going to enter it, and then it will have happened, and that will be the first time in fifteen years that a thing anybody said out loud in this yard has been written down by somebody who was not the clerk."

She held it up at the foot of the bank and then walked up it and put it on the boards of the first table at the top, and turned it round, and did not let go of the corner of it.

It had a heading in her own hand, and under the heading four words, and then a list.

> *What was said: a man gave his name to the man who mends fencing.*
> *Who was standing near him —*
> *The man who puts tables up. The woman who keeps a scale. A man who keeps a tally, four feet back and half a step behind. A dog that belongs to nobody.*

"That is what I saw," she said, "and it is three people and a dog, and I have written down three people and a dog, and the dog is on there because the dog was nearer to him than the man who keeps a tally was, and I have not put a fourth person down because there was not a fourth person, and I have wanted there to be a fourth person for about two days."

Nobody said anything for a moment, and Adrian understood, a second and a half too late, that he was about to be asked a question he could not answer, and that Orren had already answered it.

"There is a hole in it," the boy said.

"There is nothing in it at the bottom of the list," the boy said, "and there is not a hole in it, and I am going to say the same thing twice because I have said the first one badly, and here is the second one: the fourth column on *my* page is the only column on it that can be wrong in a way nobody can see, and it is the one I have not filled in, and I know exactly why I have not filled it in, and I am not going to write the reason down because I would be writing myself into a column and I have spent four years refusing to do that."

He had the sheet against his chest.

"I was nine feet above that bank and about forty feet off," he said, "and I was watching your hands."

---

The yard took that in the way a yard takes in a thing that is not about anybody in it.

Then the man who puts tables up said, "That's not a small thing. Go on, then. Why were you watching his hands."

"Because a man who has decided not to write a thing down has to keep his hands still," Orren said, "and I have never seen anybody keep their hands still, and I was watching to see what he was going to do with them, and I have been doing that since I was about eleven, and it is the single most useless thing I have ever done with a morning."

Nobody thanked him for it, which is how the yard works, and which is not a failing.

"And I have a row on my sheet for a man who said a thing out loud on the forty-third morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month," the boy said, "and the row has a figure in it and it has a column for who was standing next to him, and I have not filled that column in, and I never will, and the reason is that I was not there. That is the first entry I have never been able to make and I have been keeping that page since I was eleven years old and every other row on it has something in every box."

He looked round at the yard.

"So the rule works," he said. "That's the thing. The rule works and the first time anybody used it we got a record with a hole in it, and the hole is in the only part that would have been any use, and it is in there because of something I did, and I am telling you in a yard instead of writing it on a page because that is the only half of it I am allowed."

The clerk looked at her own book for about a minute and a half and then she said, in the voice she used for figures:

"Entered, that a person said a thing out loud in that yard, and that the thing was that a person had used the rule, and that a second person said a thing out loud about a column in his own keeping, and that I am entering no name beside either, and that no paper has been given to me, and that I have entered no name and I am not going to ask for one."

---

He walked down the bank at about ten past five to look up the lane, and there was nothing on it, and the cart came up it late with the near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it about nine inches off the ground with one arm and went on up the lane without stopping, exactly as he has done for nine years.

The number of mornings that second table has stood at the foot of that bank is seventy-five. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered, and the man who keeps a tally was about nine feet away and had not been asked to be anywhere.

The count of the five is still five. The bid is open at four hundred and nine days and was not run, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page, and a page went up that lane yesterday afternoon and there is nothing on the lane this morning, and the man who took it has not come back down to say whether that is good news.
