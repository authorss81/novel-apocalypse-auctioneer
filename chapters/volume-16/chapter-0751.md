# Chapter 751: The Thirty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Came Down That Bank At Ten Past Eight And Nobody Had Asked Him To

The man who puts tables up was at the foot of that bank at ten past eight on the thirty-fifth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, which was a full hour and a half earlier than he had ever come, and he stood at the near end of that second table with one hand pushed right down into his pocket and looked at the near rail the way a man looks at a fence he has already mended.

At the top of the bank the boards of that first table were bare and hard and dry and there was nothing on them at all. About four people had come up before eight and gone on to the top of the lane. By ten there were about nineteen in the yard of Lot Seventeen, and that was the two hundred and seventy-eighth morning after the count.

Adrian came down the bank at about ten with a length of fencing wire in his coat pocket that he had not looked at in fifty mornings and had no intention of looking at this morning, and he stopped about nine feet off the near end of that table, which is where he stops, and stood there.

The clerk was at the near end of the first table at about a quarter to eight with her page flat on the boards. She had entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. She had entered four hundred and ninety days as the age of that figure. She had entered that the number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move, and that the number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand since the sixth of the fifth month is eighteen and did not move, and that the count of counted months is six and did not move. She had entered that the number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is sixty-five this morning and that it moved this morning from sixty-four. She had entered that the number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is thirty-six, on the word of a man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, said out loud before she entered it. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move.

That last entry is the only thing in her book that depends on somebody else, and she has never written down whose word, only that it was said before she wrote, which is a discipline she invented and nobody else in this district understands.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four, and got all four both times, and the clerk entered them once. That was the four hundred and seventy-sixth of those mornings.

"The board carries six hundred and forty-nine, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and sixty-five days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and seventy-nine, and six hundred and forty days separate the second of January and this morning."

He said it without looking at the wall. He has never once looked at the wall while saying it. On about four mornings out of fifty somebody has asked him why not, and he has said that if he looked at it he would be reading it, and if he read it he might be wrong, and he has not been wrong in four hundred and seventy-six mornings and he is not going to start.

---

Adrian wanted to ask the yard one question.

He had thought about it for eleven days, which was longer than he had thought about anything in a long time, and the reason it had taken eleven days was not that the question was hard. The question was four words long and had been sitting in his mouth since the morning a stranger came down that lane with a page and read five lines and said nothing to anybody. *What is that fifth line for.*

What had taken eleven days was working out that he had to ask it out loud, in that yard, to about nineteen people, in front of the man it is about, and that there was no version of the question that was only a question.

The clerk's name — her name was not something the yard had, and Adrian had been calling her *the clerk* for so long that he had a whole vocabulary built on the word, a way of holding her at arm's length that was made of grammar. He had noticed this about himself on about the twentieth morning and had done nothing about it, because doing nothing was free.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning," she said, and did not look up, because she was at that point in the morning where the numbers came and the people came after.

"I want to ask the yard something."

"You may." She turned her pencil over. "I'm not stopping you."

"I'd like a minute of everybody at once."

"That's two things and one of them's a request." She wrote something. "You can have the request. The minute of everybody at once you'll have to be worth, and I'll know whether you were worth it, and I'll go on writing either way, so don't think you're buying a silence."

He had been getting that for eleven days too, and he was about due some.

Down at the bottom of the bank the man who puts tables up took his hand out of his pocket and put it flat on the near rail.

"Don't," he said.

He said it quietly, and it carried anyway, the way a thing carries in a place with a bank on two sides of it.

"Don't what."

"Don't ask it here." The man put his thumb under the rail and pressed up, the way you test a post you fitted yourself a fortnight ago and know is sound and check anyway. "That book's been sitting on that table with five lines on it for fifteen years, and four of them mean something, and every morning for fifty mornings about nineteen of us have looked at the fifth one and not asked what it's for, and that's not us being daft. That's us working out that if one of us asks what it's for, then somebody's got to answer, and the answer's going to be that it's a *condition* with no end on it, and if that's what it is then it's a sentence about the man it names, and then it isn't a condition any more, it's a *fetch*."

He turned round and looked up the bank at the man nine feet off the near end of the table, and then at the man at the far end of it with his spade against the leg.

"Fifty mornings," he said. "Fifty mornings a page from somewhere has been able to come down that lane, read that line, and walk up that bank to that man, and there is not one blessed reason written on this table why a page can do that. I have been down here five mornings a week for nine years. I have fitted two rails and cut one length of wood. If that line was a fetch I would have seen a stranger walk up to him about four hundred times. I have seen four strangers. And every one of them came for the *number*, not the man, and went away again, and I have thought about that every single morning, and here is what I have thought."

He took his hand off the rail.

"It comes for the number. And it does not know it has come for the number. And neither does he."

He said *he* without pointing, and he did not need to.

---

Adrian stood there and let that land and did not rescue it.

That was the thing he was worst at, and he knew it, and it took him about nine years to be able to stand in a yard and let somebody else make the argument he had come to make. He wanted to say *that's exactly right, which is why it has to be settled before somebody settles it for us.* He wanted to say it and he did not, because the man who puts tables up was not wrong, and the trouble with being right about a thing is that it feels like an attack on the person who has been defending the status quo, and this man had been defending the status quo with his own hands for nine years and had a hole in his thumb the width of a pencil.

So Adrian said: "That's fair. You've earned that. I was going to say the other thing and I'm not going to."

"You were going to say it and you want credit for not saying it."

"I want credit for it later, when it turns out I was right."

"When it turns out you were right, it'll be because you stopped talking about it and started doing something," the man said, "and that's the day I'm interested in, and it isn't today."

The man at the far end of the table said nothing. He had a spade against the leg of it and both hands flat on the rail and he said nothing, and he said it the way a man says nothing who has decided in advance that he will not be in this.

Adrian said, "Then I'll ask it to the top of the bank."

"You'll ask it to nobody," the man said, "and that's not me being clever, that's me telling you what will happen. You stand up there at eleven and you ask nineteen people what the fifth line is for, and about nine of them will say it's a condition and about nine will say it isn't one, and both of those are answers, and the moment either of those answers is said out loud that line becomes a sentence with a person in it. It's already a sentence. It's a sentence in a clerk's hand. But everybody here has agreed for nine years not to *read* it out loud, and that's the only protection this bank has got, and you're about to take it away to win an argument."

He looked at Adrian's hands, which were empty, and then at his coat, where the wire was.

"You've had that in your pocket fifty mornings," he said. "You don't mend anything. You just stand here. So what is it you're actually doing?"

"I'm standing here," Adrian said, "because I'm the only person in this yard who's read a book like that one before, and I haven't told anybody that, and I have been waiting for a morning where saying it out loud cost me less than not saying it, and this morning is it, and it isn't."

"You've had fifty mornings."

"I've had fifty mornings," Adrian said, "of a district that has not once asked me a single question, and which has therefore had to invent me from scratch, and got me wrong." He felt it go, and he did not stop it, because it was the only true thing he had come down the bank with. "I'm about thirty-four, is what you'll say. I'm thirty-eight. Nobody in this yard asked me, so one of you guessed, and it wasn't a good guess, and I never corrected it, because I have spent nine years standing in this yard saying out loud that a figure may not be corrected, and it turns out I've been applying it to myself."

Nobody at the bottom of that bank made any sound at all.

The clerk's pencil had stopped. He could hear it not moving, which was a thing he had heard from her exactly twice in fifty mornings, and both of those times had been bad.

---

He did not ask the question.

He went back up the bank at about eleven and stood at the near end of the first table and said: "The clerk is going to put something in the book, and I want you all to look at it while she does it."

The clerk said, "I was not going to put anything in the book."

"You're going to put something in the book," Adrian said, "or I'm going to put something in the book, and if I do it, it'll be in a hand that isn't yours, and there will then be two hands in this book, and the second one will be mine, and it will be the last thing any of us needs."

"That," she said, "is the first sensible argument anybody has made in this yard for eleven days."

She took her page flat. She turned it over and drew a line under the last entry, and Adrian watched her hand and understood — with the particular clarity of a man who has spent his life reading objects instead of people — that she had drawn that line every morning for fifteen years and never once put a mark under it, and that she was frightened of the space.

Then she wrote, in the clean small hand that every document in this basin that anybody can still read is written in:

WHAT IS THE FIFTH LINE FOR
AS AT THE THIRTY-FIFTH MORNING AFTER THE THIRTIETH OF THE EIGHTH MONTH
NO ANSWER ENTERED. NO ANSWER GIVEN. NO NAME ENTERED.

And she turned the page round so it faced the yard, which she has never done, and stood back from it.

About nineteen people looked at it. Nobody said anything. Nobody struck it. Nobody corrected it, and the clerk of fifteen years — who has never left a damaged record uncorrected in her life and is the reason this district has a wall with four numbers on it instead of a memory — stood next to a page with a hole in it, on purpose, in her own hand, and let it be.

The man who digs loam looked at it from the far end of the second table for a long time, and then he took his hands off the rail and picked up his spade and went up the bank, and he did not say anything to anybody, and nobody asked him.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his own hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given. That was his three hundred and ninetieth night of that run and he had slept on three hundred and eighty-nine of them, and about four people within twenty feet of that wall have said since that a man has now gone three hundred and ninety nights without being given one thing and without being asked one thing, and that nobody in this district has ever worked out what the run is.

At about three the cart came up that bank with its near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it about nine inches off the ground with one arm and went on up the lane without stopping.


The road keeper came up that lane at about half past one, the way he came up that lane twice a day, and he read the four figures off the wall as he went by, because he always did, and he got them right, and he was not asked about the eleven miles.

He stopped.

He had never stopped. Adrian, who had been standing in that yard for fifty mornings and had learned to recognise every single thing that happened in it, had never once seen the road keeper stop between the wall and the top of the bank.

"How long's that bid been open, then," the road keeper said.

"Nine years and some," Adrian said. "Three hundred and ninety-nine days on this morning's count. It's the one on the sheet at the gatepost, near enough. It's been open since before I was in this basin."

"Right." The road keeper shifted the weight off one foot. "I'm not asking because I want to know. I'm asking because I've got eleven miles of road between here and the Verge and I have to plan them, and I can't plan a road for a thing that doesn't have an end on it. I can't tell a district what's coming if the district doesn't know whether the bid's open or shut. I've been going up that lane for six years not asking, because I thought one of you would say something one day." He looked at the bottom of the bank, at the second table with the stone on it and the open book. "You lot don't say anything."

"No," Adrian said. "We don't."

"Mm." The road keeper went on up the lane.

That was the whole of it. He did not wait for an answer and Adrian did not have one, and it sat in the yard for the rest of the day, and it was the first question anybody had ever asked Adrian Vale in that yard, and it was not about the fifth line.

---

Three hundred and ninety-nine days is how long that bid has been open, and it was not run on this morning, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and fifty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it. Three hundred and fifty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and twenty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and that figure counts the mornings that have gone by and not the mornings a month has in it. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-two days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and sixty-eight days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard watched it, and nobody has worked out a day on which it will arrive. Three hundred and eight marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are two hundred and ninety-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for fifteen mornings.

And there is now a question in that lot book with no answer under it, which is the first thing in fifteen years that has been entered in it by a person who was not copying a figure somebody else produced.
