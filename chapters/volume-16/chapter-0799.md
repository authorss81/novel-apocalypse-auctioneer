# Chapter 799: The Eighty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man At The End Of The Second Table Asked On His Own Asking Out Loud In About Nineteen People's Hearing To Be Entered In The Column And The Rule Was Satisfied And A Clerk Said No Because A Column Cannot Take A Person It Is Not About And Then He Asked Who Ruled That Column And Was Told That Nobody Knows

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and thirty-eight days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and ninety-seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and thirteen days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twenty-seven days. Six hundred and eighty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and forty-seven days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Four hundred and two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and seventy-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and eighty days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and sixteen days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and forty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and this morning is the morning the yard found out that the reason is a heading and not a rule, and a heading is not a payment and is not going to be one.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and thirteen mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and twelve. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for sixty-three mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is eighty-three, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-three and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and nobody in that yard knows who ruled it and most of them had believed for years that they did.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and twenty-fourth of those mornings, and at about eleven this morning he was about eleven feet off that wall and heard a man ask for the thing he asked for twenty-three mornings ago and did not get, and he said nothing about it then or afterwards, and that was the whole of what he gave it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and thirty-eighth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirty-seven of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the twenty-eighth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of about sixty-four said, without being asked anything, that he had been four hundred and thirty-eight nights and that he was going out at about half past four in the morning and that it was nine miles up that hill where he was going, and the man of sixty-one said nothing at all for about the length of four figures read off a wall and then said, *I know*, and neither of them has mentioned it since.

---

"I am in this yard until the light goes off that wall and then I go up that hill and I do not come down it tomorrow," said Sabra Holt, at about half past nine, at the top of eleven feet, to about nineteen people. "I am going to say the one thing and not the second thing, and I told you both of those on the eighty-first morning and I am not going to change which one it is. That is all. You have the whole of this morning of me and I would like somebody to use a corner of it."

---

The man at the end of the second table used one.

He got up off the rail at about half past ten, which is a thing he has done about nine times in fifteen years, and he came about four feet along the boards to the place where the light was, which is a thing he does, and he stood in it and looked at about nineteen people and did not say anything for long enough that the boy of seventeen put his hand flat on his own sheet as though he were about to write something down and then took it off again.

"I am going to ask for something," he said. "I am going to say the request in the words the rule was made in, because I made the rule and I am not going to use a better version of it than the one I made out loud in that yard in the light fifteen mornings ago. And I am going to do it standing up, in this yard, in front of people, on my own asking, and not at anybody's word, and I am going to say that now so that nobody can say afterwards that I was put up to it."

---

"Go on," said Sabra Holt.

---

"A person goes in that column when they ask for themselves, out loud, in a yard, in front of people, and not when somebody asks on their behalf, and what is written down is the asking and not the reading. I am asking for myself, out loud, in this yard, in front of about nineteen people, and nobody has asked on my behalf and nobody is going to. I am the man at the end of this table. I have been at the end of this table for fifteen years. I want to be entered in the column in that book for whoever read a thing out loud."

---

He stopped there, in the light, with his hands at his sides, and about nine people in that yard understood at the same moment that he had satisfied the rule perfectly, and that two of them understood the rest of it at the same time.

---

Mara Quill opened the lot book and looked at the column and did not pretend to be thinking.

"No," she said.

---

"I thought you might say that."

---

"I am going to say it anyway and I am going to give it to you in the ordinary voice because you made the rule and you did not make it so that a clerk could hand it back with a shrug." She did not shut the book. "That column is for whoever read a thing out loud. I have been the clerk of that book for fifteen years. I have gone back through every entry in it, and there are five lines and a margin and a page at the back and one column, and the column has one name in it, and the name is a man who read nine lines out loud in this yard on the thirty-eighth morning. That is what it is for. And what you did on the seventy-first morning was describe a thing you wrote yourself, and a description is not a reading, and I went looking for a place on that page to put the difference and there is not one. I looked. There is not one to enter."

---

"There is not one to enter," the man agreed.

"I am not going to pretend that I looked and found nothing to refuse. There is nothing to refuse, and that is a different thing and it is worse."

---

"Then the rule is not the problem," said the man at the end of the second table, and nine people in that yard heard him say it and two of them heard what came after it.

---

"Say what is," said Adrian.

---

"A rule is a thing a person says out loud in a yard and it works," said the man at the end of the second table. "I made one fifteen mornings ago and it is perfect and it has never refused anybody because there has never been anybody for it to refuse. And a column is not a rule. A column is a heading and some lines, and the heading says *whoever read a thing out loud*, and it has said that for fifteen years, and nobody in this district can ask you to change a heading, because a heading is not a figure and I do not think there is a person in this basin who can strike a line under a heading."

He turned round and looked at the clerk.

"Who ruled it," he said.

---

The yard did not make a noise. It went further than not making a noise, in the way a yard does when every person in it has just understood the same thing at the same moment and nobody wants to be the first one to be standing still.

"I do not know," said Mara Quill.

---

She said it the way she says figures, and she said it twice, and the second time she had the book open at the back page where nothing is ruled at all.

"I have been the clerk of that book for fifteen years. I was nineteen when it was opened. I have written in it every morning since and I have read in it, and there are five lines on the first page and a margin in the fifth and a page at the back that I ruled myself, and there is a ruled column on the far side of the last page that was ruled before I was put in front of it, and I cannot tell you who ruled it, when, why, or out of what, and I have never once in fifteen years looked at it as though that was a thing I did not know."

---

"You knew it was there," said the man who digs loam.

---

"I knew it was there. I entered *not asked* fifty times over the first fifty mornings of it, and I entered it four inches away from that heading, in the column on the first page for who said it, which I ruled myself, and I wrote the rule about not asking myself, and I have said for fifteen years in this yard that a clerk who writes down what she has been told is the only instrument this district has, and I never once carried it those four inches." She closed it. "That is not a confession. A confession is about a thing I did. That is about a thing I did not do, and I have had it in my hand for as long as it takes to say a sentence and I am going to put it on the page at the back because I have promised in that yard that a rule that is not in a book is not a rule, and this is worse than a rule."

---

"Enter it," said Adrian.

---

"No," said Mara Quill. "Not this morning. Yesterday morning I entered something against myself in that yard and I have had about one night with it, and I am not going to enter a second thing against myself before the light goes and then look like a person who is being gracious about it. I am entering what I did not do when that room has sat. I am entering it in my own hand and reading it out. And if there is nobody left in this basin who can read it by then, then that is one more, and I have stopped keeping the number."

---

The man at the end of the second table turned back round to the yard.

"Right," he said. "So it is a heading. Fifteen years old, nobody's hand, about a thing and not about a person, and every single one of us has been standing under it for fifteen years treating it as if it were the truth about us, and I have spent about nine years thinking the trouble was that nobody could ask for anything. It is not that. Nobody can ask for anything because the thing they would be asking for is not the thing that is on offer."

---

He came about four feet back along the boards and stopped beside Adrian, which is a thing he has never done.

"What is in your pocket," he said.

---

Adrian did not pretend.

"A length of fencing wire," he said. "It is for a gap of a mile and a half about four miles from here, and I have had it in that pocket since the middle of the winter, and I have not been to the gap, and I have said yes and I have said no and I have not done either."

---

"I am not going to ask you what it is for," said the man at the end of the second table. "I have not been not asking. I have been not asking, and I am going to keep not asking, and I would like that noted, because in about four years there is going to be a person writing down that this yard could not ask each other anything, and I have been in this yard for fifteen years and that is not what happened this morning."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and the man of about sixty-four got up off the low wall he had been leaning against for the last hour, at the foot of the bank and about nine feet below the two of them, and picked his coat up and did not put it on.

"I go out at about half past four in the morning," he said, "and it is nine miles up that hill where I am going, and I have been going out at about half past four for nine years, and I am not going to stop for a room."

He said it to about nineteen people, which he has not done before, and then he went up the lane on his own, and the man of sixty-one watched him go the whole of the way to the top of the bank and did not follow him and did not stand up off those stones to do it.

---

Sabra Holt went up the lane at about seven with the folded notice in her coat, and at the top of the bank before she went she turned round and looked at about nineteen people who had spent the last two hours being told something about a heading by a man who is on no page in that district.

"Whatever I say in that room tomorrow," she said, "I am going to say it once and I am going to say it about a piece of paper and not about anybody in this yard, and if it turns out afterwards that I said it about somebody in this yard then somebody in that corridor wrote it on my hands."

---

The lane was empty at about six and the man of fifty-six went down it saying the four to nobody at all, and got all four of them, and behind him the boy of seventeen kept his pencil in his pocket the whole way to the top of the bank.

Adrian stood at the top of eleven feet and did the arithmetic of it, and it came out the same four ways. He had the sentence. It could not be entered. It could not be carried up a hill. And there is no instrument in this basin that would put a true thing about a record into a record without making it a record.

He had one morning left to say it out loud.
