# Chapter 756: The Fortieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Boy Who Counted Everything Explained What He Was Counting And Why It Was Not What Anybody Thought

The morning went in at the boards. Four hundred and ninety-five days is the age of the figure on the sheet at that gatepost. The figure is four hundred and eleven and it did not move. The board carries six hundred and fifty-four, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-four days, and six hundred and forty-five days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is seventy and moved this morning from sixty-nine. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-one, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for twenty mornings.

Four hundred and four days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and fifty-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and sixty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-three days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirteen marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are two hundred and ninety-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Nobody in that yard could say afterwards what was different about the morning after a name went into the book, and everybody could see it.

The clerk was at the near end of the first table at about a quarter to eight. The man who puts tables up was at the foot of the bank at about half past nine with his hands in his pockets. And about nine people who had said *good morning* to the man who mends fencing fifty mornings running, and had said it to a shape about nine feet off the end of a table, said good morning to Adrian, and then about four of them stopped, and looked, and went on anyway, because it would have been worse to make a thing of it.

He had been at the top of the bank for three hours before any of it, doing nothing, being the most conspicuous idle man in the district.

---

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and eighty-first of those mornings, and about four people standing within hearing of that wall have said since that he has now said those four figures out loud nine hundred and sixty-two times this month and that nobody in this yard has ever asked him to say any of them a third time.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his three hundred and ninety-fifth night of that run, having slept on three hundred and ninety-four of them, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything.


He got the boy at about ten, at the near end of the boards, with the sheet under his arm.

"Orren."

"Don't."

"I said your name and you didn't flinch, which means you're used to hearing it in your own head about four hundred times a day, and that's not the same as being called it." Adrian put his hands where the boy could see them, which was an old habit from a life spent standing in front of frightened rooms and worth nothing here and worth doing anyway. "I'm not going to take it. I want to talk about the sheet."

"That's a way of starting the same conversation with extra steps."

"Yes," Adrian said. "It is."

The boy looked at him for a while. He had a face that was going to be very good at this in about nine years and was currently a face that had been doing it for three, which is the same thing with worse luck.

"You want it," he said.

"I want to know what it is. Properly. You said the fourth column's who's standing next to them. That's the one I keep coming back to. What is it *for*?"

"That's the stupidest possible reason to want a record," Orren said, "and it's the right one, and it's the only one anybody's ever asked me for, and I've been waiting four mornings for somebody to ask me for it instead of asking me for the sheet."

He did not open it. He held it the way he had held the stranger's page.

"A figure on a wall is a figure about nobody," he said. "Six hundred and fifty-four is nobody. It's a number on a wall and it means the same thing to the man of fifty-six and it means the same thing to the man who's never come up this hill, and neither of those men has to be in the room for it to be true and neither of them can be got at by it. Right."

"Now say a thing out loud, in a yard, with other people standing there. It's still the same fact — but now there's a fact about who heard it."

"And who's standing next to a man when he says a figure is the only thing that decides whether the figure is worth anything, because in about six years one of them is going to have to be asked, *were you there when he said that*, and if there's a page in this district that says who was standing there, then the question has an answer, and if there isn't one, then the only people who can answer it are the people who were going to be asked anyway."

Adrian had been in four hundred lots and had never once been shown a better instrument.

"That's a witness list," he said.

"It's the closest thing to one this district has had since the Fracture," the boy said, "and I built it out of a pencil because I counted things out loud for fun, and I have never once had to explain it, because there was nobody here to explain it to."

---

"And the first column," Adrian said. "You've had two jobs. Which of them is yours?"

"Sela Pike," Orren said. "That's not a job, that's — sorry. That's the first thing."

He turned round and looked for her, and she was not in the yard, and he looked at the book on the boards, and Adrian understood that he was checking whether the clerk was listening.

"She's at the first table," he said. "Go on. She'll hear it. I'd rather she heard it from you in daylight than worked it out in a fortnight and came at me at four in the morning about procedure."

That was, Adrian thought, the single most impressive thing anybody had done in that yard in nine years, and it was done by a boy who was seventeen.

"Sela Pike had a bridge charter," Orren said. "Sixteen years ago, on the Coast, and the charter assigned her toll to the household. She was the household. The toll came out of her and the book called it the household's, and she wasn't one — she was one woman with two children and a house she did not own — and when she went to the registrar about it, the registrar wrote back that the toll had been assigned to the household and that she was the household. Which is *true*, that's the thing nobody at Alder Reach could ever get round. It's true and it's a door."

He did not look up.

"And then she went out onto the bridge in the dark about eleven weeks after that letter and nobody found her and nobody looked for her past the first three days, because the record said she was a household and households don't get looked for. Dad said she went back to argue about it. Dad's been wrong about that for sixteen years and I've never once been able to tell him he's wrong, because I can't prove she went back to argue about it, because the record doesn't have where she went, because the record has *the household* where a person was." He put a finger on the sheet. "So there's a row. And in the row there's a date and a figure and the words *the household*, and nothing else, and no name, because she was never entered under her own name, she was entered under a *word*, and I found that out at eleven years old and I've been stealing pieces of paper out of rooms ever since."

---

Adrian stood very still, because he could feel the shape of what was being offered him and he was about nine seconds from taking it.

"You've got my row," he said.

"Since the second morning."

"With the column."

"With the column."

"Then publish it."

Orren's face did something Adrian had not seen it do.

"What?"

"Your row. My row for Adrian Vale — every morning I've had you, what you said, how long it took, and who was standing next to you when you said it. Publish it. Put it on the boards at the top of this bank, all of it, every figure, my hand, my name, this morning." Adrian put both hands flat on the boards. "Not in a book. On the boards. And I'll stand next to it while you do it and I'll read it out loud at four o'clock the way he reads that wall, and every morning after this, and you'll have the column and everyone will have read it and nobody can say a man made a favourable record about himself in a closed room."

"That," said the boy, "is the worst possible idea anybody has had in this yard in nine years, and I want you to hear why."

"Because it's — "

"Because your row's only worth anything while you don't know it's being kept," Orren said. "That's the entire point. A man's in a yard and he doesn't know four people heard him, and what he says is *what he would have said*, and it can be compared against everything else he's said for nine years, and if it holds up then he's a man who means it. The second there's a page, and the page is public, and he knows it's public — you're not a man in a yard any more. You're a man *performing being a man in a yard* for the four people holding the page. And you'd do it honestly, and it would still be a performance, and I would not be able to tell, and neither would you, and neither would anyone." He put the sheet against his chest. "You've had a name in a book for a day and a half and you already want to put it on the boards."

Adrian opened his mouth, found there was nothing in it, and shut it.

"So no," the boy said. "Not yours. Not mine. Not anybody's. Not yet. Maybe not ever."

"What would it take?"

"Nothing anybody's thought of yet." Orren went to sit down on the boards. "Come and sit down. You've been standing for nine years, and I'm not your man, but I'm seventeen and I'm tired, and I want to tell somebody the rest of it, and there's nobody in this district I can tell."

Adrian sat down.

---

"What you do with it," Orren said, "is nothing. That's the whole answer, that's it, and you can put it in your list of five things. You do nothing. You never mention it to anybody. You never ask me for it and you never hint and you never do the thing where you say *just to be clear* in a voice. And one day I'm going to be the one who decides what it gets used for, and by then I'll be old enough and you'll be gone or you'll be somebody else, and that's fine, that's what I want."

"That's not how records work."

"That's *exactly* how records work," the boy said. "That's how every record that ever mattered worked. Somebody wrote it down, they didn't hand it over, and then they died, and then about forty years later somebody with a legitimate grievance went and got it out of the room it was in."

He looked up at the wall.

"Except," he said, "that's the thing about this district. There's no room. There's about nineteen people and a bank. So I've either got to trust you or I've got to give it up, and I don't trust you, and I don't know how to give it up to somebody who'd deserve it, and I don't know who to ask, and *that's* what I've been doing for four years, and that's what the column's actually for. It's a list of who I might be able to ask."

He looked away.

"Sela's row says *the household*. And this is a list of about nineteen people who have been in the same yard as me, and one of them is going to have a household, and one of them is going to have a word where a person should be, and when I find out which one it is, that's the row I hand it to."

Adrian had nothing to say for about a minute.

Then he said: "That's the best thing anybody has said to me since I came down that hill on the first morning, and I want you to know that I am not going to be one of the ones on the list."

"You already are," Orren said. "You were on it before you knew there was a list. That's how lists work. That's not a warning, that's just how a week works."

---

The cart came up that bank at about three with its near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it about nine inches off the ground with one arm and went on up the lane without stopping, and nobody in that yard who had spent the morning talking about a column looked up.

Orren did.

He stood at the near end of the boards with his sheet under his arm and watched the cart go, and Adrian, who had spent the whole of the last two hours being told what he was not allowed to publish, watched him do it.

"Sela," the boy said, to nobody.

"What?"

"The cart," Orren said. "Nine years. It comes up that bank at three and it has come up that bank at three for nine years, and there is about four people in this district who have ever counted how many times it has come up, and one of them is me, and I have not got a page for him." He tapped the sheet once, without unfolding it. "He's been in that yard nine hundred times and I can't tell you who was standing next to him one single time, because he never stops, and I count things that stop."

"Would you like to count him?"

"I'd like to count him," the boy said, "and I'd like to be asked by somebody who is not the clerk and not the man who mends the fence, and I've been asked by both of them about nine things this week and not one of them was that." He put the sheet under his other arm. "That's not a complaint. That's just what's been on offer."

---

Adrian went down the bank and asked him, because the boy had said he wanted to be asked and somebody had to do the asking and there was only one person in that yard who could go and do it without it looking like an order.

The man of about twenty-nine stopped the cart about halfway, which he had not done in nine years, and Adrian had to walk up the lane to him to be told anything.

"I'm not stopping up here," the man said.

"I wasn't going to ask you to."

"You were going to ask me to stand still long enough for a boy to write down what time it was," the man said. "I know what that's for. It isn't for me. It's so that a page can say a man was standing near me. I've had that asked of me twice in nine years by two men who wanted to know where I'd been the day before, and I've never once known, and I'd not have told them if I had."

"That's not—"

"It's not for me," the man said. "And you can have it anyway, because there is nothing on me to stand near. I've no household and no page, and the only reason anybody in this district knows my face is that this cart is late every day of the year and they have all learned to step round it. You stop me, I am a man standing still. I am a man somebody chose to put in a place."

He got the last of that bank up one-handed.

"Don't ask me again," he said. "And don't ask the boy. And when the next one comes down that lane with a page in his hand, which will be sooner than you'd think, tell him I said I was here and I said nothing."

---

Adrian went back up the bank and gave the boy the answer as it stood, which took four sentences, and the fourth one was the one that cost him.

He told him the man had said no, and he told him why, and he told him the man had never been asked anything before and had worked out on his own what a page wants a man for, and then he told him the last thing, which was that the man had asked him to pass on the fact of having been there and having said nothing.

Orren took that without moving his face at all.

"Right," he said. "That's a figure of its own, that. A man refusing to be on a page is a man on a page. He just doesn't know which column."

"I know."

"You knew it at about eleven this morning," the boy said. "You only didn't want to be the one who had to say it out loud in a yard, and now you have said it, so it's yours, and I'm not taking it off you."

The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud had one name in it at a quarter to eight that morning and had had none at all the day before, and that is the only figure in this district that has ever moved in both directions.
