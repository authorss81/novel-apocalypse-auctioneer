# Chapter 772: The Fifty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Sixty-One Came Up That Lane With A Leather Case And Nobody Stopped Him

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it is on no page in this district. Five hundred and eleven days is the age of that figure, and it is not the age of anything that can be added to, and the two of them are on two lines in a clerk's book every morning for fifteen years and have never once been on the same line in a sentence.

The board carries six hundred and seventy. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and eighty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred days. Six hundred and sixty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and seventy-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood, and it was made in that yard by a clerk at about one in the afternoon at the word of a boy of seventeen, and he has argued for it since and it has cost him the use of his own instrument and he has not withdrawn it.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-three days back. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and eighty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twenty-nine marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are three hundred and fifteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six, and it stands, and nothing that has happened in this yard in a fortnight has been long enough to be a month. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank eighty-six mornings. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for thirty-six mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is fifty-six, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered, and the man who keeps a tally said it this morning before he had looked at the book, which is a thing he has never once done in nine years. The count of things this district has made is fourteen. The lane count is twenty-one, and a man came up that hill this afternoon at about half past two with no page in his hand at all, which is not what it counts.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and ninety-seventh of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him, on his four hundred and eleventh night of that run, having slept on four hundred and ten of them, and there was a clerk's line about him on a page with nothing ruled on it and no line in the lot book, and the clerk entered that line against his express wish and has not told him and has entered that she has not told him.

---

The turner came up that lane at about ten and went straight through, which he does twice a day and has done for nine years, and this morning he did not stop at the wall.

That was the whole of the difference and three people noticed it, and one of them was a boy of seventeen who wrote it on the back of his own sheet under a box he had ruled the day before, in pencil, for mornings he had been standing near something and could not see the boards.

Nobody came up the lane again until about half past two.

---

He came up it on foot and alone and he was not in a hurry, and he was sixty-one or about it, and he carried his weight on the right side in the way of a man who has had a hip for a long time and has stopped mentioning it. His coat was better than anything in that yard. He had a leather case under his arm about the length of a fair copy, worn brown at the corners, and he had it under his arm the way a man carries a thing he has carried for forty years and has never once been asked to open in a street.

He came to the top of that bank and looked at the yard the way a man looks at a room he has been told about.

Nobody stopped him. It had become a rule in that yard in about a week and Adrian could not tell whether that was the best thing that had happened to it or the most frightening, and he had been unable to ask anybody about it, and a man of forty-three had walked into the same yard at about eleven one morning and spoken before anybody else had and it had been the best argument Adrian had been in since he came down the hill.

---

The stranger did not have a page in his hand.

The boy checked. He checked twice, from about nine feet, the way he checked everything, and then he said out loud, to nobody in particular and to about nineteen people who had all stopped what they were doing, "He has not got a page in his hand."

"That is not a count," said the clerk, without looking up.

"It is not a count and I am still saying it out loud," said Orren Pike, "because you said on the forty-ninth morning that the lane count moves when one comes down with a page and that nobody gets to move it but me and first, and there is a man coming down that hill who is not one of those, and I would like it in the air that I looked."

---

The man came the last four feet into the yard and stood about nine feet off the near end of the second table, which is where Adrian stands, and Adrian was not standing there, and the man noticed that and looked at the boards instead.

"Right," he said. "Lot Seventeen. Fifty-six mornings on that bank. Two parties, because you have not introduced me and I have not asked. The term is a day. I will take what is offered and I will not come back up this hill about it."

Nobody in that yard had ever heard a man open like that, and about four people understood at once that he had done it several thousand times.

"I am here about a room," he said.

"There is no room," said the man at the end of the second table, without turning round.

"There is a building nine miles up that hill with a column in it and a name against the column and nobody in the room," the man said. "That is not a room. A room is where a person sleeps and I have not slept in one since a morning I could tell you about."

---

Then he looked at Adrian, and Adrian looked at him, and for about two seconds nothing happened in that yard at all.

It was not his face. Adrian had carried a version of the face for seventeen years and the version was younger and it was a version of a man standing in a doorway in a bad coat with a stamp case under his arm, and what was in front of him was an ordinary man of about sixty with a good coat and a hip.

What Adrian recognised was the way the man had said *lot*.

---

"Iven," Adrian said.

The yard did not know what to do with a first name.

"Tallow," said the man. "Iven Tallow. Say the rest of it, because there is a clerk in this yard and I would like her to have it from the air rather than from you."

"Iven Tallow," Adrian said.

And the man nodded once, and it was the first thing he did that had any warmth in it at all, and then he took the leather case from under his arm and held it in front of him with both hands, the way a man holds a thing in a room he has been in before.

"You were told I was dead," he said.

"I decided you were dead."

"That is a different sentence and it is the better one," said Iven Tallow, "and I have thought about the difference between those two sentences for about sixteen years, so you will forgive me if I notice it."

---

Mara Quill stood up off the boards.

It was not a decision. She had been the clerk of that yard for fifteen years and had not once stood up off the boards in front of about nineteen people, and she stood up, and then she sat down again in a different place, having got as far as the end of the boards and no further, and Adrian understood that she had gone to put her book somewhere it could not be reached from where she was sitting.

"I will enter one thing," she said.

"Enter it."

"That a man of about sixty-one came up that lane at about half past two this afternoon, that he gave his own name in this yard at about ten to three, and that he did not have a page in his hand." She had her pencil out and she was not using it. "I will not enter what you are. There is a document in Alder Reach about that and it is not mine, and the number of documents this district does not own is four, and I am not making it five because a man sat down in a yard."

"That is correct," said Iven Tallow. "I would like you to know that I have spent forty years watching what a frightened clerk does with a thing that has just been said out loud in front of her, and that the answer changes more on the day it is asked than it does for the next nine years, and that I have watched it change in four rooms and lose in four."

"And you would like me to be the first kind."

"I would like you to be whichever kind you are," he said, "and I would like to know which one before I have been in this yard for two days, because I am going to ask things here that I have not asked anybody since before your lot was opened, and I would like to know what I am standing in."

---

"I am not here about the bid," he said, at about half past three, from four feet off the near end of the second table, which he had not asked for and had been given, because the man at the end of it had gone down the bank with his spade at about ten and had not come back for more than an hour. "I have not come to run anything. I have not come to close anything and I have not come to say anything about the figure on your wall, and I am aware that there is a man on this yard who reads four numbers off that wall twice a morning and has never been asked about it by anybody including me, and I did not come up that hill to take that from him."

"Then what is it," Adrian said.

"I hold an obligation with no end on it," said Iven Tallow. "It is the oldest one in this basin. I have held it for seventeen years and it does not have a term on it and I am sixty-one and I would like to be asked about it in a yard by somebody who is not me."

---

"There is no instrument in this district that can hold that," Adrian said.

"I know there isn't. I have known it since about the fourth morning of this month and it is the reason I am standing on a bank instead of sitting in a room nine miles up a hill." He turned the case over in his hands. "I am going to sit on those stones."

Adrian looked down the eleven feet of bank at the place where a man of about sixty-four sat at half past three every afternoon with his coat folded beside him, and then he looked at the wall, and then at the lane.

"You have been up that hill," Adrian said. "There is a room with my name in a column in it and it has been sitting there seventeen mornings and a man in it has told a shopkeeper about me."

"You have not been told that by anybody who came down here."

"I have been told it by the man who keeps every road in this basin, in a yard, out loud, at about a quarter to six on the fiftieth morning, and he said he had got it from a shopkeeper and that he would not say it again."

The old man was quiet for a moment.

"Then I have been discussed," he said. "Good. I would rather be discussed than waited for. I have spent seventeen years being waited for and I can tell you exactly what it is like and it is a room with a person's name in a column and nobody in it, and I did that to you before I came up the hill, and I would like that entered somewhere by somebody, because you are going to find out that I did and I would rather you found out from a boy than from me."

---

"You cannot have the end of my table," said the man at the end of the second table, coming up the bank behind them with his spade.

"I did not ask for your table."

"You asked for a room and there are two things in this yard and one of them is a table at the foot of a bank with a book on it and the other is a wall at the bottom of it with a man on it, and you have been looking at the wall since you got here."

Iven Tallow turned round and looked at him properly for the first time, which took about as long as it takes to read a page.

"You are about thirty-seven," he said. "That line has been on that book for fifteen years and it is the only line in it with a man behind it and you put it there when you were about twenty-two, and you have been standing at the end of it ever since, and about nine mornings ago you put four lines in a margin saying a man was asked for an end and said no."

The man at the end of that table did not move at all.

"Who told you that."

"Nobody told me that," said Iven Tallow. "There is a copy of that book in a room nine miles up a hill and I have read it. I have read every fair copy of that lot in this basin twice a year since before it was opened, and I know that a clerk of nineteen ruled a column and wrote a heading on it in one afternoon and has not read the heading properly since, and I know that a boy of seventeen has admitted in a yard that he put a name in it, and I know about the margin, and I know there is a line in a clerk's own book about a man on a wall who did not ask to be in it."

He put the case down on the boards and straightened it with the side of his hand.

"So I am going to sit on the stones," he said, "and I am going to be about four feet away from a man I have never met and be spoken to in front of about nineteen people, and if that is not the condition you have spent fifteen years on then tell me now and I will go back up the lane and be silent in a room for another year."
