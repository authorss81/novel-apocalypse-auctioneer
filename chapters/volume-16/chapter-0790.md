# Chapter 790: The Seventy-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman With A Key Carried Four Things Up That Lane And Would Not Take A Fifth And The Clerk Of That District Entered A Sentence About A Column With One Name In It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and the person who read the founder's clause out loud in that yard on the seventy-second morning gave it no number and it has not got one, and nobody in that yard has ever been able to put one figure beside another, and nobody is going to this morning. Five hundred and twenty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and four days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and eighteen days. Six hundred and seventy-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-eight days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page, and there is nobody in this district who has ever heard anybody propose anything else, and a woman with a key has been in that yard nine mornings and has not asked anybody about it once. Three hundred and ninety-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and seventy-one days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and seven days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six, and it is not going to move in this weather and nothing in that yard is going to make it move. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and a woman went up that lane at about half past four with four things written in her own book and none of them is ours. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid and was not paid yesterday when a man said it out loud as a fact about himself, and it is not paid this morning either.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and four mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and three. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-four mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-four, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and it did not move yesterday when a man counted another man twice in a yard and gave away the wrong figure on purpose, and a man of thirty-eight was ninety-four mornings in that yard yesterday and is ninety-five this morning, and neither of those two figures was said out loud this morning. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and there is still one name in it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and fifteenth of those mornings, and he has said them five hundred and fifteen times to about nine people at a time and to one woman with a key and to nobody, and not one of them has ever asked him what any of it is for, and one of them has now written down in a book nine miles up a hill that he has never been paid.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-ninth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-eight of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the nineteenth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a boy of seventeen said good afternoon from about nine feet off, and that boy has been at the bottom of that bank on six mornings now and has said good afternoon to that man on five of them, and did not say it on the second one, and neither of the two of them has mentioned that, and nobody in that district has ever written down what any of it means.

---

Mara Quill entered the sentence at about eleven o'clock and read it out before she wrote it, in the ordinary voice, in a yard with about nineteen people in it, and it is the first thing she has entered in fifteen years that is not a figure and not a want.

> *Entered on the seventy-fourth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, at about eleven, by M. Quill. A rule about who may be entered in the column in this book for whoever read a thing out loud was made out loud in this yard on the sixty-eighth morning, in about nineteen people's hearing, before any person was refused, in the words of a person whose name is on no page in this district, and I did not write it and I have entered it now at the word of the person who made it and I am entering it about six mornings after he made it and I am entering the interval because I am not going to pretend to a book that I was there at the time and I was not.*
>
> *Entered that the column in this book contains one name and that the name in it does not satisfy the rule made on the sixty-eighth morning, because that name was entered on the thirty-ninth morning at the word of a boy of seventeen asking for a place to put a name and not at the word of the person the name belongs to, and because a figure in this district may not be corrected, struck, replaced, superseded or taken out, and I am therefore not able to take it out, and I have been the clerk of this book for fifteen years and I have never once entered a thing about that column that was not a name.*
>
> *Entered that the rule made on the sixty-eighth morning refuses the man who reads four figures off that wall, at his own word and with his condition met, and that he did not argue, and that a woman with a scale said out loud in that yard on the same morning that it was not the fright and that it was not an accident, and that I entered neither the rule nor the sentence, and that I am entering them this morning because a rule that is not in a book is not a rule, and a clerk who says that a rule is not a rule because she has not written it down is a clerk who has decided that the only instrument in this district is the one she is holding.*

---

Nobody in that yard said anything for long enough that the man of fifty-six, who had said the four figures at about a quarter to eight and had been standing about eleven feet off the wall ever since with his hand on the stone, took his hand off it.

"Enter me," he said.

"I am not going to."

"I am not asking to be entered. I am asking you to enter the sentence you have just read out, because you have entered everything else in it twice and you have entered that one once, and I have been in this yard since the middle of that winter and I could not tell you which morning of it it was and I have not tried, and I have never once had anybody enter a thing I said twice."

---

"Neither have I," said the man of fifty-six, "and I am going to be honest about the reason and the reason is that I do not know whether I would be able to stand it, and I have had six mornings to find out and I have not been able to, and the woman with the scale has already told me what the difference is between not asking and not wanting, and I am not going to be able to do it in a room nine miles up that hill, and it does not get easier on the morning we go."

Nobody in that yard had expected the second half of it and about four people in it looked at the ground.

---

Sabra Holt came up the bank at about half past three with the case in her hand that was not hers, which is a thing she did not explain, and stood at the near end of the first table.

"I am going up that lane at about half past four," she said, "and I am going to take four things and I am not going to take a fifth, and I would like the yard to hear the list and the fifth, because in that room somebody is going to ask me what this district said and I would like the two of those to be the same thing when I say it."

"Go on."

"One: that a rule about who may be entered in a public record was made out loud in this yard six mornings ago by a person whose name is on no page in this district, and that I have written that down and cannot produce the person, and that I am not going to be asked who it was by anybody who has not earned it." She counted on her fingers and about nine people in that yard watched her do it. "Two: that a boy of seventeen refused to be a witness to a term and gave a reason that was his own, and that the reason was a thing he had seen and had not written down thirty-one mornings back, and that I have written the reason and not the thing. Three: that a clerk of this district said out loud in this yard that she is the market of Lot Seventeen and that she cannot pay a person, and I have not entered the sentence and I have entered that she said something and would not give it to me. Four: that a man in this yard has read four figures off that wall twice a morning for five hundred and fifteen mornings and has never been paid and has never asked."

---

"And the fifth," said Adrian.

"The fifth is a man of fifty-eight's ninth name, and I have not asked her, and I said in this yard on the seventieth morning that I was going to, and I am going to, and it is going to cost her and it is going to cost the woman and neither of them is going to thank me for it." She shut the book. "I am not carrying that up that hill this morning because if I carry it up this morning then that room will hear about a woman on a working list before it has heard about a rule, and a room will make a decision about a person in the time it takes to write a figure down, and it will make it about a list, and that is the order things go wrong in and I have watched it happen four times."

---

"You are leaving a person in a corridor to be the thing that is argued about," said Mavis Dorr.

"I am leaving her to be asked by somebody who is not a yard, which is the only thing I have got that this district has not already done to her." She did not soften it. "And I am going to do it in writing, and I am going to do it whether or not anybody here agrees with me, and if she says no I am going to enter that she said no and I am going to carry that up that hill as well, because a no from a person is a better thing in a room than an absence, and this district has been putting absences in rooms for about thirty mornings and I have watched what it does."

---

Then the man who drives the cart came about nine feet along the second table and said the thing he had come to say, and he had it in a cart's voice and not a low one.

"You have been in that yard nine mornings and you have asked about four people and every one of them has said no to something," he said, to Sabra Holt, without looking at her. "There is a woman of fifty-eight who named nine out loud and a boy of seventeen who named a row and a man at the end of a table who has said what a thing cost him, and about four of them are not one of the four people who walk down here, and I am not one of the four either, and I have been the figure twenty-one in that yard since the forty-ninth morning." He stopped. "I am saying it because you are the first person who has come up that lane in nine years who has left without a person, and I did not know until about a week ago that that was a thing that could be done, and I would like somebody in this district to know that I noticed."

---

Sabra Holt did not answer that and did not look at him for a moment longer than was comfortable, and then she said, "I noticed too," and went up the lane at about half past four with a case that was not hers.

The lane count is twenty-two and did not move, and does not move for the other direction, and there is nobody in this district who has ever proposed that it should.

---

Adrian came down the eleven feet at about ten past five, which he had done twice in the whole of the run of mornings since the day he arrived and never once without a reason, and stood at the far end of the second table and waited until the boy of seventeen had come up off the bottom of the bank and was on the boards again, and then he asked the question that had been on his own page for ten mornings.

"You can name him," he said. "That is the fourth of the four things I asked for on the sixty-fourth morning. Whether the person who holds it can be named in front of about nineteen people who have never held anything."

---

There was nobody at the end of the second table, because the man who sits there had gone down the bank at about ten past five as he does, and the answer came from the stones at the foot of the low wall, and it came in a voice about nine feet off the boards, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet had to lean over to hear it and did not lean.

"Yes," said Iven Tallow, without getting up off the stones. "I am sixty-one. I am standing in this yard, or near enough to it, and there are about nineteen people in it and not one of them has ever held anything. I said my own name out loud on the fifty-sixth morning at about half past two before anybody asked me for it, and I have said it as many times in this yard as it has taken, and not one of them was asked for. I am the fifteenth person in this district that anybody can look up and I did that to myself on purpose."

---

"And the reason," said Adrian.

"The reason is that if I am going to be findable in a yard then I had better be the one who says it. That was true on the fifty-sixth morning and it is still true. It has cost this district about nine other people their choice, and I have had about eighteen mornings to work out whether that was a gift or a way of taking something off nine people who cannot be looked up." He did not get up. "I have not worked it out. I will be at the bottom of this wall at about half past three tomorrow and so will he, and neither of us is going to say anything, and that is the entire of what I have in this district. It is enough for me. I would like it said that I have said it is enough for me, and that I know it is not an answer to your fourth question."

"It is the answer to my fourth question," said Adrian.

---

"You are a worse man than I have been giving you credit for," said Iven Tallow from the stones, "and I would like that said at the end of a day when a clerk has entered a sentence about a column and a boy of seventeen has given a stranger a wrong figure on purpose. Those are the two things in this district I would want read in about four years, and I have been in this yard for about eighteen mornings and I have not said either of them out loud before."

From about nine feet below the boards, the man at the end of the second table said the other half of it into the middle of the yard, and he said it to nobody, and about nine people heard it, and he had not said a whole sentence in one in a very long time. "A man has been in that yard about eighteen mornings and has not said one thing out loud in it, and I know exactly why, and it is not a very complicated reason, and I have got four years to say it in and I am not going to be hurried."

---

At about ten past five the boy of seventeen got out the pencil and turned his own sheet over against the boards, holding it down at one corner with his thumb because there was nothing but air between the boards and the wind, and he wrote the morning in the box headed *mornings on which a thing was said out loud in this yard and was not entered anywhere*, and he ruled a space under it and left it empty.

Then he wrote a figure in the margin of the box, in pencil, in a hand that got worse when he was tired, and it was ninety-five, and he did not say it out loud, and he did not offer it to anybody, and about four people at the top of eleven feet were already deciding what they were going to do about the sitting and none of them knew that a figure about a man of thirty-eight had just been written nine feet below them by a boy who had decided this morning to give a stranger the wrong one on purpose.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about four and again at about six on his way down the lane to nobody at all, and got all four of them both times.

At about a quarter past six Adrian Vale was still at the top of that bank. He has been in that yard ninety-five mornings. He has not been given a bed, a wage or an office, has not asked for any of the three, and has a length of fencing wire in the pocket of his coat about four miles from a gap of a mile and a half that he has not been to and did not go to this morning.

And not one of them had said so out loud, and the boy of seventeen did not write it down, because a box on the back of a sheet cannot hold what nobody has said.
