# Chapter 784: The Sixty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man At The End Of The Second Table Made A Rule Out Loud In About Nineteen People's Hearing Before Anybody Had Been Refused And The First Person It Refused Was Himself

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and there is a woman with a key in this district as of the sixty-fifth morning, and nobody in that yard has ever put the two of them together in any form, and that is not going to happen this morning either. Five hundred and twenty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-two. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twelve days. Six hundred and seventy-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-two days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and eighty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and one days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six, and it is still six, and it did not move yesterday either. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and the number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four, and a rule made out loud in that yard this morning is not one of them and is not a fifth. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid and has not been paid on any morning of this book.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-eight mornings and moved this morning from ninety-seven. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-eight mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-eight, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a rule was made out loud in that yard at about half past ten this morning, and a man of sixty-one said out loud in a yard on the sixty-third morning that a thing one person said in it is not the fifteenth. The lane count is twenty-two and did not move. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and there is no more in it this morning than there was yesterday morning.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and ninth of those mornings, and a rule about who may go in a column was made in that yard this morning, out loud, in about nineteen people's hearing, before anybody was refused, and the man of fifty-six was in that yard when it was made and it refuses him.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-third night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-two of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the thirteenth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of sixty-one said it in a voice that carried about nine feet and no further, and about four people at the top of that bank heard it and about nine did not.

---

She came up the bank at about ten with the page under her arm and she went and stood below the boards, which is a thing no person with a key has done in that yard in nine years, and about nine people noticed it and about four of them understood why she had done it.

"I am going to ask the boy a question," she said, "and I am telling the yard that I am going to ask it, and I am telling the boy, and neither of those things is a promise that he may refuse. I have been three days on this hill. There is one person in this district who has been in this yard every morning for four years and who has heard everything that has been said in it, including things said at the foot of a wall about nine feet below nine people, and you may draw your own conclusions about whether that is the qualification a sitting would take. I have not asked him yet. I am standing below the boards so that he is not the only person being looked at when I do."

---

"Before you ask it," said the man of fifty-six, from about eleven feet off the wall, with his back still half to the yard, "say one thing out loud for the yard, because I would like it said while there is still time for it to be true."

"Which thing."

"The thing you have just told us you came here for, three mornings ago, in a yard, in the ordinary voice, and that a boy of seventeen has not had once in his life." He turned round. "A rule about who may be entered, made out loud in that yard, in about nineteen people's hearing, before anybody can say no to anybody. You wrote the condition into that book on the sixtieth morning on my word and you entered that I asked for it, and you entered that I am not in that column until it exists, and about nine people in this yard have told me this month that nobody in this district can write it."

---

"I did not write it," said Sabra Holt. "I am not going to, and I will not pretend that I have not thought about it for three days, because I have, and I will tell you why I am not going to."

"Tell us."

"Because I have a key and I have been an administrator for nineteen years and a rule about who may be entered in a public record, made by a person who has a key, is a rule that a room can take apart with a line of its own." She shifted the page under her arm. "And because I have watched four districts do this and in three of them the rule was better than the thing that came before it and in the fourth one it was the best-written rule anybody in that basin has ever seen, and it was made by nine men who had decided that the trouble was that people were slow, and there has not been a person in that district who could refuse anything since. I am not going to make that rule and I am not going to let anybody in that yard mistake me for somebody who could."

---

"Then it has to be somebody who can be refused," said the man at the end of the second table.

He had not said a whole sentence out loud in that yard since the sixty-second morning, which was six mornings ago, and everybody in that yard turned, and about four people did not turn because they had been listening to him not say anything for six mornings.

"I have been at the end of this table for fifteen years," he said, "and I have spent most of those fifteen being a line in a book, and I have been careful in a yard for nine of them, and I have not said a whole sentence in one for a very long time, and I am going to say one now and I would like it made before I start talking because I know what I am like."

He came about four feet along the second table to the place where the light was, which is a thing he does.

---

"A person goes in that column when they ask for themselves, out loud, in a yard, in front of people, and not when somebody asks on their behalf, and when they do it the thing that is written down is the asking and not the reading."

He said it in the ordinary voice and he said it once and then he said it again because about nine people had not heard the whole of the first one.

"That is the rule. I have made it out loud in this yard this morning, in about nineteen people's hearing, and I have made it before anybody has been refused anything, and if anybody in this yard wants to be refused afterwards they cannot now say that the reason was made about them, and if anybody in this yard wants to go in that column they cannot now say that a person with a key was in the yard while it was made."

---

"Say why," said the man of fifty-six.

"Because a thing asked to be kept is a favour and a favour can be refused, and a refusal of a favour comes down to the person being asked on the day," said the man at the end of the second table. "And because a blank is worse than a word. I have said that to a boy of seventeen and he wrote it down and he did not know he was writing down the reason for the next four minutes of his life."

---

"And now I am going to say the part that is mine," he said, "and I want the yard to have it before anybody starts being generous about the rule."

He put one hand flat on the second table.

"There is one name in that column. It is not mine. He is in it because a boy of seventeen stood on some boards and asked a clerk to look for a place to put a name, and the name he was asking about was not his. So Adrian Vale is not in that column under the rule I have just made, and I am the man who said he was not, and I am not going to sit here and pretend that is a small thing."

---

"It is not," said Adrian, from about nine feet off the near end of the boards, and he had not been asked.

"It is not," the man agreed, "and it is yours to say and I have taken it off you. Now the second part, which is mine and which I have not said out loud in a yard in fifteen years."

He looked at the man of fifty-six.

"You were asked. On the sixtieth morning, at about one in the afternoon, by a woman with a pencil, in a yard, and you said yes, and you set a condition, and she entered the condition and the refusal and the fact that you asked for it, in that order, because that is the order it happened in. You are the only person in this district who has been asked for that column. Under the rule I have just made, you are not in it. You never asked for yourself. You were asked, and saying yes to being asked is not asking, and if I made a rule where it counted, I made it wrong."

---

The man of fifty-six stood with his hand on the stone for about as long as it takes to read four figures.

"That is right," he said.

"You may argue with it."

"I will not argue with it and you may not tell anybody in that room that I did." He took his hand off the stone. "I asked for that rule eight mornings ago for one reason and I have got it, and I have got what I asked for and it is not what I wanted, and I would like it entered that I knew it would not be and I asked for it anyway, because the thing I asked for was not the column. The column is a name on a page. What I asked for was that the next person refused in this yard does not have a reason written about them this afternoon."

---

"Then it worked," said Sabra Holt, from below the boards, "and I would like to say so before anybody in this yard starts arguing with a man who has just lost the only thing he has asked for in this yard."

"I lost nothing I had," said the man of fifty-six. "I have been in that column in every way that matters since the thirty-ninth morning and there is one name in it and it is a man who read four lines out loud in front of about nineteen people and was not entered for it. That is the whole of the argument and I have been making it out loud for three mornings and I would like somebody in this yard to notice that the argument got made in the wrong direction."

---

The woman who keeps a scale, from her own step, about forty feet off and not coming up the bank, said the thing that nobody else in that yard was going to say.

"No it is not the fright," she said, to the man of fifty-six, and everybody heard it, and nobody in that yard looked at her and everybody in that yard heard it. "You would have had to want it, and in every morning I have been able to see of you at that wall you have never wanted anything, and that is not a fault in you. It is also not an accident, and I am not going to let anybody in this yard make it into a virtue, and I am not going to let you make it into one either, because you have been standing at that wall reading four numbers to nobody for five hundred and nine mornings and wanting has not been one of the things on offer at that wall."

---

He did not answer her.

He picked his coat up off the stone and he said the four off that wall one more time, into the middle of the yard, with nobody standing near enough to want them, and got all four of them, and went down the lane, and about nine people in that yard stood exactly where they had been.

---

Sabra Holt looked at the man at the end of the second table.

"I am going to enter that rule," she said. "Not into anything of yours. Into my own book, in my own hand, this afternoon, with the fact that it was made out loud in a yard in about nineteen people's hearing, and with the fact that it was made by a person whose name is on no page in this district, and I am going to write down that fact because it is true and because in about four years somebody is going to ask who wrote it."

"You will not get a name out of me," said the man.

"I did not ask for one and I am not going to ask for one." She took the page out from under her arm. "I want you to understand what has just happened in this yard, because nobody else in it is going to say it. The first rule this district has ever made out loud in a yard is now in a book of the Council's, and it was made by a man they cannot name and refuse, and it cannot be taken off that page by anybody in a room. I did not do it. He told me at about half past nine that he would not give a name to it. About forty minutes later he was standing on that table in the light and he had made it anyway, and he has not said his name and is not going to."

---

"And now I am going to ask the boy," she said, "and the rule does not help him, and I would like the yard to notice that too, because I noticed it about two hours ago and it is the reason I told you all of that out loud before I did it."

"Why doesn't it help him," said the man who digs loam.

"Because that rule is about a column, and the boy is not going in a column, and I am not asking him about a column." She looked up at the boards. "I am asking him whether he has understood the terms, in a yard, out loud, in front of people, and he will answer yes or no, and either answer is a thing I can carry."

---

Orren Pike got up off the boards and stood on them, and he was seventeen and he had about nine feet of yard looking at him and a woman with a page at the bottom of it.

Then he said no.
