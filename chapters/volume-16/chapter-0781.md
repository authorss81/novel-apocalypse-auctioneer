# Chapter 781: The Sixty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month An Appointed Administrator Came Down That Lane With A Page In Her Own Hand And A Boy Of Seventeen Said The Figure Out Loud Before Anybody Had Asked Him To

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it does not move when anything happens on this hill, and there was a woman on this hill at about nine this morning and the figure did not move for her either. Five hundred and twenty days is the age of that figure, and the two of them have never been in a yard together.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-nine. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-five days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and nine days. Six hundred and seventy days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty-nine days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page on any of the mornings this week. Three hundred and eighty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and fifty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-eight days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move either. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid this morning and was not paid on any morning this district has had a lane with a figure at the end of it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-five mornings and moved this morning from ninety-four. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-five mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-five, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered, and that figure and the morning itself have been the same figure on every morning of this book and nobody has ever been asked to make them differ. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lot book is on the boards at the top of that bank with five lines on it and a margin in the fifth line and a column with one name in it, and the heading of that column says *whoever*, and nobody has read that heading properly since a clerk of nineteen ruled it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and sixth of those mornings, and about four people in that yard had made up their minds before the middle of the week that somebody was going to come up that hill who was not one of the four of them, and not one of them had said so out loud, and a boy of seventeen had a space ruled on the back of his own sheet and did not know yet what it was for.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twentieth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and nineteen of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the tenth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and that was the whole of it.

---

She came down the lane at about nine and she came on foot, and she was carrying a page in her right hand, and Orren Pike was on the boards of the first table with his own sheet open on his knee and he saw her at the top of the bank before anybody else in that yard did.

"Twenty-two," he said.

---

Nobody in that yard had asked him for anything. Mara Quill had her book shut in front of her and the pencil lying across it, the way it lay when she was waiting to be asked to open it, and she did not open it.

"How long have you been counting people coming down that lane," said the woman.

"Four years," said the boy. "It was eighteen the first morning I ever kept it and it has gone to twenty-one and nobody has ever come down it and been one of us, and this morning you have come down it with a page in your hand and you are not one of us, so it is twenty-two. I said it out loud before she wrote it because that is the rule I asked her for, and the rule is that the boy says the figure and then the clerk writes it, and not the other way round, and I have never once had to use that rule on anybody who was a stranger and I did not expect to start today."

"The clerk may enter it," said Mara Quill, and she opened the book, and she wrote the figure and nothing else, and she did not write a name, and she did not ask him for one.

---

The woman came the last four feet of the bank and stood at the near end of the first table and did not put her hand on it.

"My name is Sabra Holt," she said. "I am an appointed administrator. There are nine of us and I hold the third of the nine oldest keys, and a key is not a seat and a key is not a vote and I will not be described in this yard as either. I am not the Custodian and I am not the First House and I am not your market, and I have come down that lane on my own authority and not on anybody's instructions that I could show you."

"Are you one of the four on the wall," said the man who digs loam.

"No," said Sabra Holt. "I have never read those figures and I could not tell you what any of them is for, and I would rather say that in a yard than pretend. I have been an administrator for nineteen years and I have signed four hundred and something things and the great majority of them were a person wanting something, and the number of them that were a figure on a wall is none."

---

"A page came up that lane yesterday afternoon," she said, "in a cart that pulled to one side, and the yard was told what was on it in nine words spoken by the man who drove it, and I have that in my hand now because a page that goes up a hill in this basin is read by whoever is at the top of it before the man who sent it has got back to the bottom of the lane."

"It asked four questions," said Adrian.

"It asked four questions and it stated a figure of none and a term of none and it said neither can be sent from there and both have been sent before, and it said in its third line that one of the people whose consent the term requires is not in that district and cannot be found by anybody who comes up that lane." She looked at him. "I am going to answer the four. I am not going to answer them all today, and I am not going to answer them in a room, and I am going to tell you now what I will accept as an answer."

---

"Two things," said Sabra Holt. "Either one. Not both. A named bearer, with a figure and a term, that the market can pay out of its own funds at the first sitting in full. That is the answer your notice is asking for and it is the answer I would prefer, and I will say why in a moment. Or two fresh independent witnesses who understood the terms and were not given them by anybody. I do not care which you go for. I have carried both to a sitting and I have seen one of them work and I have seen the other one work, and they are not the same trade, and the second one is cheaper and it is much harder."

"Who decides," said Mara Quill.

"The sitting decides," said Sabra Holt. "Not me. I am nine people and a set of keys and I do not have a vote in the room and the room knows it, which is the only reason anybody in this basin has ever been willing to speak to me at all. What I do is take an answer up the hill, and I take what is in front of me, and I cannot put anything in it that is not already in it, and that is the whole of my authority and it is a small one."

---

"The sitting is in nineteen days," she said.

Nobody in that yard said anything at all, and about nine of them did the sum and about four of them arrived at a morning nobody would say out loud.

"Nineteen days is not mine either," said Sabra Holt. "I did not choose it and I cannot move it. If your district has a person it can pay, that is nineteen days to find out whether it has one. If it has two witnesses, that is nineteen days to find out whether it has two. I would like to be clear with you that I am not leaving on the eighteenth morning, and I am not coming back on the twentieth, and I am going to be in this yard every morning I can walk up that hill, and I would like that said now while it is cheap to say."

---

Adrian got about nine feet down the bank before anybody stopped him and then he stopped himself, which was not the same thing and which nobody in that yard pointed out.

"Give me the terms of the sitting and I will tell you in an afternoon whether this district can meet them," he said.

"No," said Sabra Holt.

"That is a fast answer for a thing you have carried down a hill yourself."

"It is a slow answer and you have made it fast by putting a price on it in a yard with about nineteen people in it, and I would have said the same words in about the same number of seconds if I had come down here with the light behind me." She shifted the page against her thigh. "I have read that page of yours four times on the walk down, and I read it as a document and not as a conversation, and I would not have come down this lane for a conversation. I came down because you wrote a signature at the bottom of it and there is a person at the bottom of it. Everything else in that district is a market and a yard and a man on a wall. A person at the bottom of a page is a different sort of thing, and it is the only thing I have had to work on in a fortnight."

---

The man of fifty-six had been at the near end of the boards at about nine and had gone to about eleven feet off the wall and then come back, and he stood about nine feet off the woman's left shoulder and did not say anything for a while, and then he said the four figures again, out loud, in the ordinary voice, to a yard that had not asked him to, and got all four of them, and about four people said *as sent* under their breath because they had been saying it for a fortnight.

"You have been in this yard about two hours," he said, "and you have not asked me one question."

"No," said Sabra Holt.

"Somebody is going to."

"Somebody is going to," she agreed, "and it is not going to be me at nine in the morning on a day I have just told you a date in. I will ask you things this afternoon and I will ask you things badly, and I want to say before I do that I am not asking you what any of those four is for, because a man in Alder Reach asked me that in a corridor in the spring and I did not have an answer and I have not got one now and I would rather say so than fill it in."

---

Nobody asked her what she was going to ask him about instead.

Then the man of about twenty-nine who drives the cart came up the bank at about ten with his hands in his pockets and stopped at the far end of the boards and looked at the page in her hand for long enough that the yard began to look at him looking at it.

"That is a page," he said.

"Yes."

"I have taken four things up that hill in nine years. Two of them were people. One was a question, and it came back down as nothing, and there was nobody on the road who could say where it had gone. One was a page, and I have never asked about any of the four of them, and I know what a page looks like from about nine hundred yards because I have been on the wrong end of this hill more than anybody in this district." He took his hands out of his pockets and then put them back. "Are you going to ask somebody in this yard to say something they have not said."

"Yes," said Sabra Holt. "Probably this afternoon, and I would like it to be somebody who chose to say it and not somebody I put in a position, and I have not worked out how to do that yet and I have nineteen days to work out how to do it."

"Then you have come to the right yard," said the man of about twenty-nine, "and I hope you have brought a pencil that is not interested in me, because I am the figure twenty-one in that yard and there is not room in that for a second name of mine, and I said that out loud on the sixty-fourth morning when a yard of about nineteen people heard it, and I am not saying it again this morning."

"Nobody asked you to," said Sabra Holt.

"Nobody asked me to. That is how it got said. And I want one thing in that yard on the record and I am going to say it once. The figure got into this district on the forty-ninth morning, sixteen mornings back, and I did not ask for it, and I have not asked for it since, and I have not been asked why by anybody including you, and I would like the wall to have that on it today because it is the one thing about me in that yard that has no question attached to it."

---

At about half past four the woman who keeps a scale came out of her shop with her hands wet and stood on her own step and did not come up the bank, and she said one thing across about forty feet of yard that everybody heard and nobody wrote down.

"You are the first person to come down that lane in nine years who has not come for a person," she said. "I have been waiting nine years for somebody who came down it without a person in their mouth, and I want you to know I have watched you stand in my yard for about seven hours and not want one, and I would like it on the wall if there is anywhere to put it, and I am aware that there is nowhere to put it."

"There is a wall," said the boy of seventeen, "and it is about nine feet off me and the turner reads it twice."

"Then it can go on the wall," said Sabra Holt, "and I will put it there myself at about five, and I will not ask the clerk to enter it, because a thing I write on a wall for a woman in a shop is a thing I have done and not a thing this district has done, and the difference is the only thing I have brought down that hill."

---

Orren Pike got down off the boards at about ten past four and got out the pencil and turned his own sheet over and put it against the stone, because the wind off that bank had been at about nine all afternoon and it took him two tries.

He wrote the morning in the third box. He did not write the figure. He wrote the morning and he ruled the space under it and left it empty, and he said out loud, for the yard and not for her, that the morning was the sixty-fifth and that the twenty-two was now a figure in a clerk's book and that a person had come down that lane with a page and that what was on it had not been said out loud in this yard and could not be entered anywhere, and that he had ruled the space for it anyway.

Then he put the pencil down and looked at Sabra Holt, who was watching him from about nine feet.

"You are going to ask me something," he said. "Not today. You said this afternoon and you said you had not worked out how to do it without putting somebody in a position, and I have been in about four positions this month and I am the only person in this yard who counts, and I would like it on the wall as well, because you have not asked me yet and I would like everybody to know the day you do."

---

Sabra Holt got up the bank at about half past five, slower than she had come down it, and the lane took her at the angle it takes everybody, and she went up it with the page in her hand and did not look back at the yard, which is a thing about her that about four people in that yard noticed and that the boy of seventeen entered in the box with the mornings in it, in pencil, in the space he had ruled and left empty, on the back of a sheet nobody is allowed to enter anywhere.
