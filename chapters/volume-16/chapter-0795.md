# Chapter 795: The Seventy-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Boy Of Seventeen Said One Hundred Out Loud In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People When Nobody Had Asked Him For It And Two Books Nine Miles Apart Then Held Two Figures About The Same Man And Neither Of Them Can Be Taken Out Of Either

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and thirty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and ninety-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and nine days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twenty-three days. Six hundred and eighty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and forty-three days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and seventy-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and seventy-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and twelve days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and two figures about one man went into two books this morning and the count did not move and there is no instrument in this basin that can make them be one figure.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and nine mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-nine mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-nine, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and there is no more in it this morning than there was yesterday morning.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and twentieth of those mornings, and at about eleven this morning he was in that yard with his hand on the stone and about nine feet of bank between him and the boards and no idea at all that a thing was about to happen at the top of it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and thirty-fourth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirty-three of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the twenty-fourth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of about sixty-four said it first for the first time in twenty-four afternoons, and the man of sixty-one said it back, and neither of them has ever mentioned that either.

---

The boy of seventeen came up the eleven feet of that bank at about eleven with his sheet in his hand and stood on the boards at the near end and looked round the yard the way he has looked round it four years, at about nineteen people, and did not say anything for long enough that a man of fifty-six looked at the sun and then at him and then at the sun.

Then he said a number out loud.

"One hundred."

---

Nobody in that yard understood it for about the length of time it takes to read four figures off a wall.

---

"He has not asked for anything," said the woman who keeps a scale, from about forty feet off, where she had been since the second morning she came up that bank. "Nobody has asked that boy for a figure since the seventy-third morning and it is six mornings back, and a man saying a figure out loud because he has been asked is not the same thing as a man saying one because he has decided to, and I am the only person on that step who can tell you they are different and I am telling you because nobody on those boards can."

---

"I know what it is," said the boy of seventeen. "I have got it on my page and I have got it in the margin in pencil and I have not said it out loud in front of anybody since I started keeping this, and I have been writing it down every morning for four years and there is not one person in this yard who knows that I write it down every morning."

---

"Orren," said Adrian.

"Do not use it. Not because of the rule — because it is not the part." The boy held the sheet flat against the boards. "I am going to tell this yard why and then I am going to stop talking, and the reason is that six mornings ago I gave a woman with a key ninety-one, and I told her that my own page said ninety-four, and I would not give her the right one, and I have had six mornings to work out whether that was about what is true or about what a person can be asked about afterwards."

---

"And this morning," said Sabra Holt.

---

"This morning I have decided to find out," said the boy of seventeen. "I have said a figure out loud in a yard in front of about nineteen people and every one of them can be asked about it afterwards, including the man it is about, and that is the thing I have been buying and I am paying for it out loud in front of the people who will be asked. That is the whole of what a figure is. I have known that for four years and I have never once paid it."

---

She wrote it, and she read it out before she wrote it, and she said what she was writing under it, which is that the figure is the word of a boy of seventeen, this morning, offered and not extracted.

"Ninety-one," she said, "is on the page where it was six mornings ago, in the same hand, on the same morning's entry, and it is still there, and you may all look at it, and it does not go away because I have written a second one this morning."

---

"No," said Mara Quill, from four feet off the end of the second table, and everybody in that yard turned, because Mara Quill had been holding that lot book shut for most of a week and had said whole sentences in a yard twice yesterday and once this morning and had entered all three.

"Listen to me for as long as it takes and then I am going to stop, because I have been the clerk of this book for fifteen years and this is the first morning in fifteen years that two of the figures in this district have been in an argument, and I have wanted to say something about that for six mornings and I have not had the two of them in the same room before."

"A figure in this district may not be corrected, struck, replaced, superseded or taken out. That is not a sentence I have written this morning. It has been the practice of that book since it was opened and I have never once had cause to state it in a yard. So: I cannot strike the ninety-one. I said out loud in that yard five mornings ago that I cannot take a name out of that column, and I cannot strike that figure either, and I am not going to pretend to a book that I can do a thing today because it is inconvenient."

---

"You could enter a second line under it," said Sabra Holt.

---

"I could, and it would be the same as what has just happened, and it would be worse, because then one book would have both of them in it and there would be a clerk who put them there, and the second one would be the clerk's decision and not the yard's." She shut the book. "Two figures about one man are now in two books nine miles apart. One of them is in mine. One of them is in hers. I cannot get at hers and she cannot get at mine and neither of us is going up that hill with both of them, because the room will use whichever is more convenient and it will be the smaller one, and it will be right to."

---

"Then which one goes up," said Adrian.

---

"Ninety-one," said Sabra Holt. "I am not going to put a second figure beside a figure that I am legally and practically unable to withdraw, in a room that will take whichever of the two costs me less. The boy offered me a better number and I am carrying the worse one, and I would like the yard to notice that I made that choice before the clerk's hand had moved and did not enjoy either half of it."

---

"That is correct," said the boy of seventeen. "That is the entire reason I said it out loud. If you had carried one hundred up that hill then in about four years there would be a number in a room that nobody could account for and the only person who could explain it would be me, and I have not got anybody to hide behind."

---

"You have not got anybody to hide behind at all," said Mavis Dorr. "You never had. I have watched you stand on those boards since before that woman with a key came up this lane, saying the truest things anybody in this yard has said since the wall started, and there is not one page in this district with your name at the top of any of it, and that is not modesty. That is the shape of you."

---

"Ninety-one is on a page nine miles up that hill," the boy said, "with my hand in the sentence about it. That is a worse hiding place than nothing. It is a hiding place with my own directions on it."

---

Adrian had been about nine feet off the near end of the boards for the whole of it with his hands in his coat pockets.

"I am not going to correct either figure," he said. "I want that said out loud by me in that yard this morning, because I said nothing six mornings ago and Mara Quill told me at about ten past four that afternoon that she had let a man of thirty-eight into a record three mornings wrong and had a reason for it, and she was right to tell me, and I did not do a thing about it then either, and I am saying it now so that there are two of us who have done nothing."

"You have nothing to do with it," said the boy of seventeen. "That is the first true thing anybody has said to me about this in six mornings and I am going to write it down, and you are going to let me, because I keep a column for who was standing next to a person when they said it, and a person cannot refuse being written down by a boy who is keeping the record of the room, and that is the one thing I have got and I have never once used it."

---

"Write it," said Adrian.

---

Orren Pike wrote the morning on the back of his own sheet, and wrote one hundred in the margin in pencil in the hand that got worse when he was tired, and wrote under the rule the sentence *a man of thirty-eight said he was not going to do anything about it and would not stop me writing it down*.

He left the space empty that was headed for mornings on which a thing was said out loud in this yard and was not entered anywhere, because this morning a thing had been said out loud in that yard and entered, and the box was not for it.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and the clerk's pencil went into a book, and about four people in that yard got the air out of their lungs in the ordinary way that people do when four numbers have been right twice, and a woman with a key wrote the same four figures in a second book, and neither of them said anything about the other one, which is what the two of them have done every morning for a week, and which is this morning the only habit in that yard that has survived something.

Adrian came down about nine feet of bank at about a quarter past six and stood at the far end of the second table.

"You said it out loud," he said.

"Yes."

"Do not do it again for me," he said. "I have had six mornings of being wrong in a book and I have made my peace with it, and if you say one hundred in a yard one more morning so that I can hear it, then in about four years somebody is going to work out that the boy said the number for the man and not for the yard, and that is the one thing I have been trying not to do since I got here."

"That is not how it works," said the boy of seventeen. "You do not get to spend mine."
