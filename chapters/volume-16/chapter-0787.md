# Chapter 787: The Seventy-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman With A Key Asked A Man At The End Of The Second Table To Describe The Thing He Had Been Standing Near For Fifteen Years And He Described It And Then Refused To Be A Witness On The Grounds That He Was Not Standing Near It At All

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and a woman with a key has now spent six mornings in a yard with it in it and has got nothing out of it, and a man at the end of a table has described the fifth line of a book in one, and those are the two nearest things this basin has come to in six mornings, and neither of them is a figure that anybody could check. Five hundred and twenty-six days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-five. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and one days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and fifteen days. Six hundred and seventy-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-five days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-eight days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and four days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-four marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and is where it was on the first morning this book has run. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and it did not move when a man described the thing he has been standing near for fifteen years. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and a man described that fifth line out loud in a yard this morning and it is still not paid, and describing a thing in front of about nineteen people is not the same act as paying for it, and this district has nine mornings of proof of that and got all of it the hard way.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and one mornings and moved this morning from one hundred. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-one mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-one, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a man talked in that yard for a good half of the morning and it is not a thing four people made. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the fifth of those five lines is a man and he described it out loud in that yard at about eleven this morning and it is still a man.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and twelfth of those mornings, and at about a quarter past eleven he said a fifth thing in that yard, and nobody had asked him, and it was not a figure about a wall.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-sixth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-five of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the sixteenth afternoon running, and a man from the top of eleven feet came down the bank at about half past ten and stood at the end of the second table and asked a question, and neither of the two men on the stones heard it asked and both of them heard it answered.

---

"I am going to ask you about the fifth line," said Sabra Holt.

She said it standing at the foot of the bank, at about half past ten, with the boy of seventeen about nine feet behind her and about nineteen people above eleven feet of bank who had come down about four feet each, which is as far as anybody in this district comes.

"You told a clerk on the fifty-second morning that you would not give it an end. I am not going to ask you again. I am going to ask you something else and I have wanted to ask you something else since the second morning I was on this hill and I have not asked it because the man who sits at that end of that table told a man of sixty-one eight mornings before I came down this lane that he had nothing to say to him and would rather be left, and I know that because a woman in a corridor told me, and it is the reason I have been careful with you for six days, and it is a reason you would not have given me, and I am going to stop honouring it this morning and I would like you to know that I am doing it deliberately and that I am telling you first."

---

"Ask it," he said.

"Nineteen years ago I have signed things. Nine years ago I have watched a district take a charter that was renewed without anybody being asked, and it took two years for the people in it to find out, and I have never found out whether that was the same thing. Three mornings ago I asked a man on a wall in this yard who other people are on a page in this district without their agreement, and a woman of fifty-eight said nine names out loud in this yard, and that is the amount I have." She turned the page over against her arm. "So: what is the fifth line, and what has it cost you, and I want the second part answered in your own words and not in the shape of a number."

---

The man at the end of the second table looked at the boards of the first table for a while and about four people in that yard started to leave and did not.

"I have been at the end of this table for fifteen years," he said, "and the fifth line is me, and I dictated it on an afternoon when I was about twenty-two, and it is a standing offer of my labour to this lot with no end on it and no figure against it, and it is four words long and one of them is *until*."

He did not stop.

"It has been working every morning since. That is what a standing offer is. It does not run out and it does not get paid and it does not get asked to. If this lot has something that needs doing and I am the one who can do it then the lot knows it can have me, and the lot has never once had to say so, and I have never once had to offer, and that has been the shape of about nine years of my life and I have not been unhappy in it, and I would like the yard to notice that I said that before I said anything else about it, because I have thought about this for about two days and the part I am proud of is the part I said first."

---

"What has it cost," said Sabra Holt.

"It has cost me the ability to want anything," he said. "It has cost me about nine years of being careful in a yard, and I did not notice losing them. It has cost me one thing, and I am going to say that thing and then I am going to stop, and nobody in this basin is going to ask me about it again, including her."

"Say it."

"I built it on purpose when I was about twenty-two, and I did not know what I was doing, and nobody told me, and I have been owed work ever since by a book, and I have never once been able to be owed anything by a person, because a person can say no and a book cannot, and I have been living inside that for fifteen years and it is the best thing I have ever made." He looked up. "And now the other half, because there is a person in this yard who has been waiting about two days for me to say only one of them. That is not the fifth of the five. The fifth of the five is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and this district does not have one, and the count of the five is still five, and I have not paid it and nobody in this yard has and nobody in this yard is going to. What I have got is a man who cannot ask anything at all of anybody, and that is mine, and it is about fifteen years old, and it is not a debt of this district and it is not a credit of this district, and it is worth exactly nothing to a room nine miles up that hill. And I would like every person in this yard to hear me say that out loud, because in about four years somebody is going to put those two things next to each other, and it is not going to be me that stops it."

---

The yard was very quiet and the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall from about eleven feet away, into the quiet, in the ordinary voice, and got all four of them, and about nine people in that yard who had come down about four feet each understood that he had done it on purpose and that it was the only thing available to him, and did not go back up the bank.

"I have one question and I am not going to insult you with it," said Sabra Holt. "I am going to ask it badly and quickly and in front of about nineteen people, because you have just told me something that makes you the only person in this district who could be a witness to anything, and I am going to be straight with you: I have thought about that all morning and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is that you are the only one who can, and you have just told me why, and I am going to make you say it anyway."

---

"Say what," he said.

"That you are not a witness, and why." She was looking straight at him. "I do not believe you can be one. I have been in this yard six mornings and you are the only person here who has said out loud what a thing costs, and the whole of my trade is that the second of those makes you useless, and a man of sixty-one said a version of it to a man of thirty-eight at the foot of that wall twelve mornings ago and I have not stopped hearing it."

---

He did not take long.

"I dictated a line into a book when I was about twenty-two," he said, "and I have not been outside a term since, and I have been inside one the whole time, and the day a room asks me *what does that term cost* the honest answer is a thing I have been living inside for fifteen years. So I am not a person standing near a thing. I am the person who wrote one. And you cannot put a man who wrote one in a room to watch a settlement close and call the room witnessed, because he will be watching his own handwriting go down, and a man watching his own handwriting go down is a man with a reason, and a reason is the one thing a witness is not allowed to have."

---

"That is what I am," said Iven Tallow.

He came down the bank at about ten past eleven with the case under his arm, which is not a thing he has done on any morning but two in this volume, and he came the whole eleven feet and stopped at the end of the second table and did not sit on the stones.

"I wrote the terms," he said. "Seventeen years ago, in about nine months, and I put the two safeguards in myself in the ninth month because I would not trust anybody else to write them, and I have been the man in the gap where the two witnesses should have been ever since, and I said so to a man of thirty-eight twelve mornings ago at the bottom of that wall and I have not been asked about it since and I am saying it now. I am the person who wrote it. If you put me in that room then you are putting the man who wrote the settlement in the room to watch it close, and I will have a reason, and I have had one for about forty years, and my reason is me."

---

"Then there is nobody," said the man of fifty-six, from about eleven feet off the wall, and about nine people in that yard agreed with him out loud.

"There is nobody," said Sabra Holt.

She shut her book and she did not make anything of it and she said it the way she had said *nineteen days*.

"I want to be exact and I want it in that order, because I have watched about four hundred people find this out the slow way. It is not that nobody in this district has understood the terms. It is worse than that and it is better than that at the same time. It is that every single person in that yard is already party to something, and a party to a thing cannot witness it, and the reason you cannot witness it is the reason you know what it costs. And that is the arrangement. That is how a settlement is built so that the only people who could stand in the room are the people it has already used."

---

"Say the other half," said Adrian.

"I have got it," said Sabra Holt, "and it is the half I would rather not have carried down that hill. The reason every person in this district is disqualified is that one of them told the truth to nine of them in a yard on the fifty-ninth morning. He did not lie and he did not soften it and he gave it away and he cannot have it back. If he had said it in a room, in about the ninth month, seventeen years ago, in his own hand, with nobody in it, then the two people he could not find in that hall would have been allowed to be two strangers off a road, and it would have closed, and none of the four of you would be standing at the bottom of that bank with a clause in it. One of you did a thing honestly in the wrong place and the wrong place was a yard, because a yard is the only place in this basin where a man of thirty-eight can be found, and you were all found."

---

Nobody in that yard argued with it and about four people in it looked at the man of thirty-eight, who had been in that yard ninety-two mornings and had not been given a bed, a wage or an office, and who did not have a bed, a wage or an office this morning either, and who had not asked for any of the three, and who had sent a page up that lane in a cart seven mornings ago with no figure and no term on it.

---

"Seven mornings ago," said the man of fifty-six, "a man of thirty-eight read four lines out loud in this yard and I did not know what any of it was for, and a clerk said she was not entering the fact that he read it, because a thing you read in that yard is a conversation." He had his hand on the stone. "I would like to say one thing about that and then I am going to read four figures and go home. That is the rule. I asked for a rule about who may be entered and a man made one out loud in this yard three mornings ago and the rule does not stop that, and it is not supposed to, and the man who made it said out loud in the same breath that it does not stop that. I have had three mornings to work out how I feel about a rule that is exactly the size of the thing I asked for and I have not worked it out, and I am going to say that out loud in a yard and let it stand there, because I have been the only thing in this yard that anybody is sure of for as long as I have been standing at that wall, and I would like there to be one thing in this yard that nobody is sure of, and that is it, and there it is."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and Mara Quill's pencil went into the book, and about four people said *as sent*. The boy of seventeen wrote the morning in the box on the back of his own sheet and wrote nothing else, because the rule about what goes in that box is the mornings and not the things, and he made that rule himself on the sixty-second morning, and a rule he made himself is the only instrument in that yard nobody can take off him.

Then, at about a quarter past four, the man at the end of the second table walked about nine feet along the wall and stood at the end of it and looked down at a man of about sixty-four on some stones.

"Two things," he said. "I have been at the end of this table for fifteen years and I have never once asked you anything, and that is not because I was told not to. It is because I did not want an answer I would have to do something about. And you have been out on that road at about half past four for nine years and I have been four hundred feet off you at about the same hour more times than either of us has bothered to count, and I am going to ask you the question I have been not asking."

"Ask it."

"What is it like," said the man at the end of the second table. "At half past four. On the road."

---

The man of about sixty-four thought about it for long enough that the sun came off the stones and came back on them.

"It is like being the only person who knows where he is going," he said.
