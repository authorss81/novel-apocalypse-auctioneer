# Chapter 754: The Thirty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month Somebody Asked The Man Of Fifty-Six What A Term Was And He Answered

The morning went in early and went in fast, which Adrian had not expected of a day that began with somebody opening a fold.

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and four hundred and ninety-three days is the age of that figure. The board carries six hundred and fifty-two, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and sixty-eight days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-two days, and six hundred and forty-three days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is sixty-eight and moved this morning from sixty-seven. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is thirty-nine, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for eighteen mornings. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move.

Four hundred and two days is how long that bid has been open, and it was not run, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and fifty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it. Three hundred and sixty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-five days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-one days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and eleven marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are two hundred and ninety-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and seventy-ninth of those mornings.


The second fold had been under the string all along, and the knot was already cut, and it wanted nothing but two thumbs. The man who puts tables up opened it with his own two hands without saying anything about it, which Adrian took careful note of, because in nine years he had never seen that man produce anything.

The second fold held one printed side and one smaller face printed on the reverse of it, and Adrian did not look at the smaller face until about four o'clock, and neither did anybody else. The printed side held the same heading and then it held the terms, and the terms were nine lines, and the ninth line was the one that did the damage.

> *9. The person sent forward is to be paid at a stated figure for a stated term. Payment is to be made from the funds of the market sending them, at the first sitting, in full, and not thereafter unless the term is renewed by fresh consent of the persons the market represents.*

Read out loud at about eleven in a yard of Lot Seventeen with about nineteen people standing in it, it took Orren eleven seconds and about four words were hard to get out.

Adrian read it twice himself, which the boy noted, and then said, "Right. I'll say what I think it says and you can all be angry at me if I've got it wrong."

"Do that," said the man who puts tables up. "Being angry at a summary is cheaper than being angry at a page."

Adrian said it. He stood on the boards of the first table at the top of that bank, which he had never done, and he read nine lines out loud in plain words, and he got one of them wrong on the word *market* and corrected himself, and nobody corrected him, and that was the first good sign.

When he came to the ninth line he stopped for a second.

"They want one person," he said. "One name. One number for what they're paid. One length of time. And the money's ours, and it's got to be there in full on the day, and then it stops, and it only starts again if the people we represent agree to start it again."

"And what's a term," said the man who puts tables up, "when you've just read me one."

"It's a bit of time with a bit of money attached, and it ends, and then somebody's got to decide whether it goes on."

"Goes on or doesn't," the man who puts tables up said. "Aye. I've spent nine years putting rails on that table for nobody and being thanked by nobody, and I've never once been asked for a term, and I'll tell you exactly what a term is. A term is the bit where somebody stops." He looked at his own hands. "I'd be all right with that."

"You'd be all right with that," Adrian said, "and there are about nine people standing in this yard who would not, and we're not going to get through the rest of the morning by me being fair at them one at a time."

---

The woman who keeps a scale was up that bank at about ten and had come out of her shop with her hands wet, and she got there late and heard about half of it, and she heard the half she heard from the man of fifty-six, who repeats things.

"I'm not going," she said.

"Nobody asked you to go anywhere. The term's a year or a season, they haven't—"

"A year or a season in Alder Reach is a year's bread for four households, and I've got a shop, and three pages on my counter, and a woman who comes down that lane once a week because she can't be in a house with a list on it, and if I'm in Alder Reach in a room for a term then she stands outside my shop for a term, and that's the arrangement, isn't it. That's the whole of what I've got." She was not angry. She was doing arithmetic out loud, which is what she did with a scale. "Say the next bit."

"The next bit's the whole of it, then," Adrian said. "One name. And out of the four of us in this yard who could stand in a room and be paid for standing in it, there is exactly one."

He had not meant to put his own name near the sentence and he heard himself do it and everybody heard it and the yard went very quiet in the specific way a yard goes quiet when everybody has arrived at the same conclusion independently and none of them likes it.

"No," said the clerk.

"It's a name, not a sentence—"

"It's a name, and it's *your* name, and I've watched you do the last four mornings." She put her hand flat on the boards. "You came down that hill with a list of five things. You stood in this yard and told about nineteen people a thing about yourself that had nothing to do with the question you came to ask, and you told it because you'd worked out that saying a true thing about yourself first makes the true thing about the book easier to swallow. That's a technique. I know the technique, I use it about four times a morning, I'm the clerk of this district and I've been doing it since I was nineteen."

Mara. He had said Mara twice in two days and both times it had come out with no weight on it at all.

"You're right," he said.

"I've been right for nine years," she said. "It doesn't get more tiring."

---

The man of fifty-six had said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and at about half past eleven he was still standing there, and Adrian went over to him, because Adrian had an instinct about where the trouble in a room actually was, and the trouble in that room was the man who could recite four numbers from memory and had never once been asked to explain them.

"Morning," Adrian said.

"You've said that."

"I'm going to ask you something and I want you to know before I ask it that I have watched you for fifty mornings and I have never once asked you what any of it is for."

The man of fifty-six turned his head. He had a long face and the kind of eyes that people describe as patient, and Adrian had been putting that word there for nine years and it was wrong; what they were was *finished*, in the way a road is finished, not in the way a person is.

"Four hundred and seventy-nine mornings," the man said.

"That's the four hundred and seventy-ninth of those mornings, and you said the four again at about four and got them, and it's the four hundred and eightieth morning at about a quarter to eight, which I don't think anybody's ever said out loud."

"That's because nobody's ever counted them past me."

"I have."

The man looked at the wall. He actually looked at the wall, which Adrian had never seen him do, and it was a strange thing to watch, a man checking a thing he could have said without it.

"Right," he said. "Ask."

Adrian thought about the page, and about the ninth line, and about one person and one number and one length of time.

"If they came up that lane and asked you for a term," he said. "An end. Would you be able to give them one?"

"Yes."

"Of a figure on that wall."

"The board's at six hundred and fifty-two this morning." He did not hesitate. "It moves one a morning and it has moved one a morning for longer than I've been standing here, and it isn't going to skip one because a letter came up that lane. It reaches a thousand in three hundred and forty-eight mornings and I can tell you the day of that month it lands on, because I can count, and I have never once been asked." He turned back to Adrian. "Or I could tell you which of those four is wrong."

The yard had gone from quiet to something else.

"Which one," Adrian said.

The man of fifty-six looked at the wall, and at the clerk, and at the open lot book at the foot of the bank, and he took his time, which he had never had to do before in four hundred and seventy-nine mornings because nobody had ever stood in front of him and waited.

"I don't know," he said.

He said it flatly, the way he said the figures.

"That's the answer and I have never given it out loud before," he said, "and I want it written down that it took me about four years to work out that it was the answer and about four years after that to work out that saying it out loud in a yard was cheaper than carrying it about. There is one of those four that is doing something I cannot describe. I have watched it every morning for about four years. I cannot tell you which one it is. I have gone back over it perhaps nine hundred times and I have never once been able to put my thumb on it, and I am not going to guess in a yard in front of nineteen people, because the day I guess is the day about nine of them stop looking at the first figure, and the first figure is the one that keeps this district fed, and by the time you lot have finished finding out which one I picked, something will have gone wrong along that road that I cannot see from here."

He took his hand off his pocket.

"I am not the wall," he said. "I am a man who reads it out loud twice a day so that if it changes, somebody has got to have *heard* it change. That is the whole of what I am for, and nobody has ever asked me for it, and I have never had to explain it, and I would rather go on not having to than have to explain it badly."

Nobody in that yard said anything.

Then the man who digs loam, at the far end of the second table, who had not said one word in that yard in four mornings, and had said one word in the whole of nine years before that, said: "That's the first sensible thing anybody's said in this yard in nine years and you had to wait for a stranger's page to make you say it."

---

The clerk said, at about a quarter past twelve, in front of everybody, in the ordinary voice she used for figures.

"The fifth of the five things this district does not have is a way to pay a person who is not in a household." She looked up. "That's the reason we can't answer that page. Not because we haven't got anybody. Because there's no instrument in this district that can put money in a stranger's hand for a year and take it back if the year doesn't happen. There are nine coppers in that bank and every one of them has a person attached to it who I can name. There is no way for me to write a figure against a *person* in this district, and there hasn't been since I was nineteen, and the count of the five is still five."

"So it's on the list," Adrian said. "Fifth of five. Still five."

"It's been the fifth of five for nine years," she said, "and for nine years the fifth of five has been a thing that embarrasses us. This morning it's the reason we're not going to Alder Reach." She put her pencil down. "You may write that down. I'd rather it was written down than said out loud again."

---

*On the reverse of the printed side, in a smaller face, which is the Tally's own hand and not any clerk's:*

**A lot has seven parts.** An object, a use, a toll, a bearer, a term, a release, and a remedy.

**Where a man is sent instead of an object, the six that can be filled in do not make a lot.** They make a notice.

**A notice is not a smaller lot.** It carries more than one, because it carries a person, and a person is not a part of anything.

---

At about half past four the man who puts tables up came up the bank, which he has not done in nine years, and stood at the near end of the first table with nothing at all in his own hands.

"Nine years," he said. "Four strangers with pages. Fifty mornings where nobody has asked me one single thing about the two rails I put on that table. Nine years of not being asked, and I've been perfectly content, and I'm telling you I've been perfectly content because I was building up to something and I've decided this morning to stop building up."

"Seth," Adrian said, and heard himself say it, and had no idea where it had come from, and then understood — the man had been introduced to him by Orren two mornings ago on the strength of nothing, in a yard, in one sentence, because a boy had thought it was time.

"Yeah," Seth said. "You can use it. Everybody else here can use it. I just don't use it on a page." He turned round to face him properly. "So which is it, then. You've been in this yard fifty mornings and you're the only one who hasn't told anybody anything. You've got a name. You've had one for years, I can tell, because you've got the look of a man who's been called one. And you're about to walk up that lane to Alder Reach with a piece of paper that wants a name on it, and you are not going to write *man who mends fencing* on it, are you."
