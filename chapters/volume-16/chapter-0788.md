# Chapter 788: The Seventy-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Sixty-One Said Out Loud In A Yard That He Was Not Going To Ask A Woman To Write A Page In Her Own Hand And Then He Wrote One Himself And Read It Out Loud To About Nineteen People And It Could Not Be Carried

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and the person who read the founder's clause out loud in that yard this morning gave it no number and gave it no name, and a man in that yard said out loud at about eleven this morning that he has been the only one holding anything for seventeen years, and nobody has asked him to explain it and nobody is going to. Five hundred and twenty-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and eighty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and two days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and sixteen days. Six hundred and seventy-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirty-six days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and ninety-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and ninety-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and sixty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and sixty-nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and five days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and forty-five marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and thirty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six, and nobody in that yard has counted a month since, and not one of them could. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and it did not move this morning although a clause was read out loud in that yard at about eleven, because a thing said in a yard is a conversation and a conversation is not a document, and the count of things this district has made is fourteen, and a thing read out loud in about nineteen people's hearing is not a thing four people made. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and two mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and one. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for fifty-two mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is seventy-two, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The lane count is twenty-two. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and thirteenth of those mornings, and he was in the yard for both of them, and about nine people in that yard heard the second one and about four of them had heard a man of sixty-one say a thing out loud at about eleven that cannot be taken back off the stones.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and twenty-seventh night of that run, having slept on four hundred and twenty-six of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the seventeenth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and the man of sixty-one said a thing he had come down that bank to say and had not said for nine mornings, and neither of them referred to it again.

---

"You have been on those stones seventeen afternoons," said Sabra Holt, at about half past nine, from the top of eleven feet. "I have counted, because I count things, and because you were the first person I met in this district who told me a number about himself without being asked and I have not known what to do with that yet. I am going to ask you three questions and I am going to ask them in the yard and in the order I have them, and the second one is the one I actually came for."

"Ask them in whatever order you like," said Iven Tallow. "I have been in about four hundred rooms in this basin and the order has never once mattered and the people asking have never once noticed that."

---

"First. Are you the market of Lot Seventeen."

"No."

"Second. You are the only person in this basin who could stand in a room and be asked what that clause costs, and I am not asking you to be a witness, because a man who wrote the terms is not a witness and you told me so yourself at the foot of that wall and a man at the end of a table told me so on the seventy-first morning, and I have written both of those down. I am asking you whether you are going to be in the room on the day of that sitting."

---

"I have not been asked," said Iven Tallow, "and I am not going to ask, and I will tell you both of those in that order because the second one is a decision and the first one is a fact and I would like a person with a key to have them the right way round."

"Why not ask."

"Because a Custodian who asks a room for a reading is a man asking to be relieved of it, and there are people in this basin who have been waiting seventeen years for me to be relieved, and I have watched about four of them wait. If I ask, the room hears the asking and not the terms, and the asking is the only thing in it that is news, and I have been managing the fact that nobody in this basin knows precisely what I hold for seventeen years, and I have not managed it in about nine days, and that is a cost and I have not worked out how to stop paying it."

---

"Third," said Sabra Holt. "The page."

"A page which."

"The one I asked you for on the fifty-seventh morning. One page I own, in my own hand, not a fair copy, that a clause exists. You said three or four days. The four days were the sixty-first morning and this is the seventy-second and I have had that page in the back of my head for eleven mornings and I would like it now, and I am asking you in a yard because that is what this district has instead of a room."

---

"It is not coming," said Iven Tallow.

Nobody in that yard made a sound.

"Give the reason," said Mara Quill. "Give me the whole of it, because I have spent four mornings deciding what to do about that page and I have not once got past *she asked for it* and I would like the rest."

---

"Three reasons and the third is the one that matters and I would like the yard to wait for it." He shifted the case under his arm. "First: it is a page in your hand. I cannot write in your hand. Anything I put on paper for you is mine and is therefore a fair copy of my own work, and a fair copy of a man's own work proving that he holds a thing is not evidence, it is a hobby. So the page cannot come from me at all. It has to come out of your own hand, and you are not going to write it, and I am not going to ask you to, because the day a clerk of a district writes a page at the word of the man who holds the thing, the page is his and the hand is hers and neither of those is worth anything in about four years."

---

"Second," he said. "It cannot go up that lane. There is no instrument in this district that can carry a page up that hill. The only carrier in this basin refused one on the fifty-eighth morning in the ordinary voice and gave a reason, and on the sixty-fourth morning a man of about twenty-nine came up that bank on his own and said nine words and got them and carried a page up that lane in his cart at about half past four, and I know that because I am holding the page," he said, "and I am not going to stand in this yard and tell anybody that a man did not go because nobody asked him, and I am not going to put a man who is already a figure in a yard in a cart, and I would rather this district's only page stayed where it is than go up that hill on a man I have to feel about afterwards."

---

"And third," said Iven Tallow, "and I have been on these stones for seventeen afternoons deciding whether to say this one."

"Then say it."

"For seventeen years I have held an obligation on the understanding that it was written down somewhere in a form that could be moved." He looked up the eleven feet at about nineteen people and did not lower his voice, which is the thing about him that about four people in that yard found frightening on the fifty-ninth morning and about nine have stopped finding frightening. "It is written down. It cannot be moved. Those are the same fact twice, and I have never once put them in the same sentence, and I have had about four days to do the arithmetic and the arithmetic is that I have been a custodian of a piece of paper that a boy of seventeen has made legally immovable, and I did not know that until this morning, and I have no complaint about the boy and I have not got a better idea."

---

"I have got a better idea," said the man at the end of the second table, "and it is not mine and it is not his. It is yours, and you have had it for about nine years, and you have not used it, and I have watched you not use it and that is the whole of why I am saying it."

He came about four feet along the second table to where the light was.

"A page carried up that hill by an appointed administrator is a page the Council has produced, and a room that is handed the proof that a clause exists by the Council will hear the clause as a Council document, and a Council document with no end on it is a Council instrument with no end on it, and I have watched that arithmetic done four times in about nine years and I have not liked it once." He looked at her. "So you will not carry it, and you should not, and I would like the yard to notice that the reason you should not is not that you are wicked."

"And what is the alternative," said Sabra Holt, "since you have just told me the other two are not possible."

"Say it out loud," said the man at the end of the second table. "In a yard. In about nineteen people's hearing. Not to me, and not to her, and not to the book, and not up that lane. Out loud here. And then about nineteen people can be asked about it afterwards, and there is no instrument in this basin that can stop a thing said out loud in a yard, because a thing said out loud in a yard is a conversation and a conversation is not a document and cannot be carried and cannot be entered and cannot be got back."

---

"Iven Tallow," said Sabra Holt, "I am not going to ask you to do that and I want to be exact about why, and the reason is the last thing I have got and I am spending it."

"Then spend it."

"I have watched about four hundred districts do this arithmetic, and the arithmetic is that a thing said in a yard is a thing a room can deny. A page is the better instrument. A page has a hand on it and a reading does not. A room can call a reading a conversation, and the room will be right about the reading and wrong about the clause, and those two will be in one sentence in about four years, and the second one will win." She did not raise her voice. "So I am telling you that the best instrument you have in this basin this morning is a reading, and it is worse than the page you have not written, and you know that, and I am not going to pretend otherwise in order to make myself useful for as long as this takes me to say."

---

"Read it, then," said Adrian.

He had been about nine feet off the near end of the boards for the whole of it and he had not interrupted once, which is the first morning since the sixty-third that he has let a man of sixty-one finish in that yard.

"Read it out loud in that yard, in the ordinary voice, in about nineteen people's hearing, and I will not put a term on it and I will not ask you for it afterwards and I will not be the man in a room in about four years who says he was given it by somebody. You have had it since the fifty-ninth morning and you have been sitting on those stones for seventeen afternoons and you came up that lane at about half past two on the fifty-sixth morning with a leather case and no page in your hand, and you put your own name in a yard before anybody asked you for it, and the whole of what you have been doing since is a man waiting to be asked for the thing he came up the hill with. I am asking you now, in a yard, in front of about nineteen people."

---

Iven Tallow did not answer for long enough that a man of fifty-six, standing about eleven feet off a wall, looked at the sun and then at him and then at the sun.

"There is a condition on the seat," he said, "and I am not going to name it. I said that in this yard on the sixty-third morning, in front of about nineteen people, and I asked for the withholding to be entered, and I am not going to take that back and I am not going to be asked again, and if anybody in this yard asks me again I will say no out loud and I would like that entered as well."

"That is not what I asked you," said Adrian.

"No," said Iven Tallow. "It is the only thing I have that is worth more than what you asked me, and I have kept it for about nine mornings and I would like the yard to know that I kept it on the way to saying yes."

---

He put the case down on the boards of the second table at about eleven o'clock and took out one sheet of paper, which was not a page, and which was about the size of a page, and which had nothing on the back of it.

"I am going to say this in the ordinary voice, once, and I am not going to say it twice, and I am not going to explain any part of it, and a woman with a key is going to write down that I said it and is not going to write down what I said, and that is the correct division of labour and I would like this yard to hear me say that I know it."

He read it.

It took as long as it takes a man to say four figures off a wall, twice, and it was about two hundred words and it was nine lines, and the ninth of them was the one about a term and about fresh consent and about the persons a market represents, and when he came to it he did not stop and he did not slow down, and about nine people in that yard had heard those nine lines read before, in about nine different rooms, over about seventeen years, and they all recognised it and not one of them said anything.

---

The count of things this district has made is still fourteen and it did not move this morning either, and reading a thing out loud to about nineteen people is not a thing four people made.

A conversation is not a thing four people made.

---

Mara Quill had the lot book open on the boards the whole time and she did not write in it.

"I am not entering it," she said, at about ten past twelve, "and the rule I have given for it is nine words long and I wrote it on the forty-fourth morning and a boy of seventeen made me write it, and the rule is that a paper a person is carrying may not be entered in that book by anybody, including me, and he has been standing at the bottom of that bank for four mornings, and I have not looked at him once this morning because I have been too busy not writing."

---

Sabra Holt wrote in her own book that a man of sixty-one had read nine lines out loud in a yard in about nineteen people's hearing on the seventy-second morning, and that she had not entered the terms, and that she had entered the reason, which was that a thing said in a yard is a conversation. Then she shut the book.

"I am going to say the last true thing I have got," she said, "and then I am going to go and find somebody who can be asked about a working list in a corridor, because I said in this yard on the seventieth morning that I was going to do that and I have not done it and I am not going up that hill with two things undone. You have given up the better of two instruments in order to have the one you can defend out loud. I do not know yet whether that was brave or the last habit of a man who has been holding something for forty years, and I have known four districts that could not tell either, and I am not going to decide it in a yard in this district."

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people in that yard were still standing exactly where they had been at eleven o'clock, which is where a yard stands when a thing has been read out in it. The boy of seventeen wrote the morning on the back of his own sheet, ruled a space under it, and left it empty.
