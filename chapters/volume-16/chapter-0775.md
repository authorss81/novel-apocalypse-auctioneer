# Chapter 775: The Fifty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Of Sixty-One Told A Man Of Thirty-Eight The Truth About The Long Fracture At The Foot Of A Low Wall

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move and did not move yesterday and will not move tomorrow. Five hundred and fourteen days is the age of that figure, and nobody in this district has worked out what either of the two of them is for, and the man who reads four numbers off that wall says so twice a morning to whoever is standing there and has never once been asked.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-three. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and eighty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and three days. Six hundred and sixty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty-three days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page, and a man of sixty-one who came up that hill on the fifty-sixth morning said in that yard, in the ordinary voice, that he had not come to run anything and had not come to close anything, and nobody has run anything or closed anything since.

Three hundred and seventy-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and fifty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-two days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and eighteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six, and it has not moved on any morning of this book. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and it has not moved either, and there is a page coming up that lane which is not one of them and has not arrived. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid and has not been paid on any morning this district has had a lane with a figure at the end of it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank eighty-nine mornings and moved this morning from eighty-eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for thirty-nine mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is fifty-nine, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and it did not move this morning when nine names were said out loud in that yard yesterday, and that is the second time in a fortnight that a morning which ought to have moved it did not, and the man who keeps a tally entered both of those and entered no reason for either. Nothing has come down that lane with a page in his hand for ten mornings and the lane count is twenty-one and still is.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundredth of those mornings, and the boy of seventeen noticed, because he noticed everything, and said nothing, and wrote the figure in a box on the back of his own sheet in pencil in the time it took to say so.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundred and fourteenth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirteen of them, and a man of sixty-one came down that bank at about a quarter past three and sat down about four feet off him, and every person at the top of eleven feet watched a man who keeps every road in this basin not come down, and he did not come down because Tarin Kest had said at about ten that he was not coming down until somebody asked him something, and nobody had asked him anything.

---

Adrian had been at the second table since about nine, and the page in front of him was blank, and at about ten he turned it round so that the blank side was facing up, which he had been doing on and off for a fortnight without knowing he was doing it, and Mara Quill came down the bank and stood about four feet off the end of that table.

"You have been holding that page the right way up since ten o'clock," she said, "and there is nothing on it, and I have watched you do it on about half the mornings of this book, and this is the first one I have come down the bank for."

"I have not got anything to put on it."

"I know you have not." She did not sit down. "He came and stood at the near end of those boards at about a quarter to nine and said four sentences to me and not one word to you, and I am going to give them to you, and you are going to hate two of them."

---

"Give me them."

"He is going to say a thing this afternoon that he has not said to anybody since before your lot was opened."

"Right."

"He is not going to say it in a room."

"That I had."

"And if anybody in this yard asks you what he said, you will tell them the truth and not the useful half of it, and if you cannot manage that then you will say nothing at all, because the useful half is what he has been living inside for seventeen years and it has eaten him, and it will eat you faster than it eats him."

Adrian did not say anything.

"And do not stand between him and that wall this afternoon."

---

"Why not."

"Because you will do it," said Mara Quill. "You will stand in front of that wall with your hands at your sides and you will turn it into a thing that is happening to a man, and I have watched you do that to four people in a fortnight and it is the only thing you are actually good at and it is the thing that has made this yard into what it is, and I am not going to let you do it to him."

"He is a man of sixty-one who has been holding a thing for seventeen years."

"He is a man of sixty-one who wrote the thing," she said. "Those are not the same man and if you cannot hold both of them at once then you should not go down that bank at all."

---

She went back up it without another word, and Adrian sat on the edge of the second table until about half past two, and at about a quarter past three Iven Tallow came down the bank carrying the leather case under his arm and did not stop at the stones and did not stop at the wall either.

He went and stood about nine feet off the near end of the first table at the top and looked down at the two men.

"You told her you were going to say it here."

"I told her I was not going to say it in a room," said Iven Tallow. "You were not in the yard at a quarter to nine and I would have said it to you and not to her and I did not want that, and it is because I have said things like this in rooms four times in my life and in all four of them there was nobody else in the room, and I have learned exactly nothing from any of the four, and I have learned a great deal from the fact that I learned nothing."

---

He came down. He put the case on the stones and sat down about four feet off a man of about sixty-four and did not say anything for long enough that the man said it.

"You told me the day before yesterday that you had nothing to say to me and that you had said it."

"I did say it."

"So I am going to sit here and let you say something else, because the day before yesterday you were a stranger and today you are a fact, and a fact deserves being said to twice."

"I am going to say something here that I have not said to anybody in seventeen years," said Iven Tallow, "and I would like you to be about four feet off when I do, and not nearer, and I would like the other man to be able to walk away at any point without it stopping, and if either of those two things cannot be arranged then tell me now."

The man of about sixty-four looked at the sun for a while.

"You have arranged it," he said. "Go on."

---

"The Common Measure was mine," said Iven Tallow.

Nobody in that yard said anything at all, and there were about nine of them at the top of eleven feet who had come down about four feet each, which is as far as anybody in this district had come.

"I wrote it. In about nine months, with about four thousand rooms in it, and the whole of the design of it — the whole of the reason anybody paid for it — was that not one of those rooms could end up owning a bus, a lamp, a ferry, a piece of road or a bell. Not a market. Not a ward. Not me." He turned the case over on the stones without opening it. "There is not one of those in this basin that a market can hold. That is not an accident. That is me, seventeen years ago, writing a rule and then standing in the room where it would be enforced."

---

"How does a thing that big close," Adrian said.

"It needs two things and I wrote both of them in myself, in my own hand, in about the ninth month, because I would not trust anybody else to write them."

"Which two."

"Two independent witnesses who had understood the terms and had not been given them by anybody. And a term on the thing, so that it could not run for ever, and so that the morning somebody wanted to change it they would have to stand up and say so in front of two people who knew what it cost."

"That's a good rule."

"It is an excellent rule and I have never been able to find anybody who could carry it," said Iven Tallow, "and I wrote it myself about ten months before I found out, which tells you what I was."

---

"The close came."

"The close came. It was the sixth week of the last year of the old counting and there were about nine hundred people in a hall and every one of them had read the terms and about four hundred of them were frightened of what they had read."

"And you could not find two."

"I could not find two people in this basin that I trusted more than I trusted myself," he said. "Not could not find two people. Could not find two people I would rather have stood in that hall than me. And I did not go looking for long, and I have been asked about that by four people since who were not in that hall, and I would like it said out loud in a yard, because the looking is the part that ought to be examined and the holding is only the part that was obvious."

"Then give it to a market," Adrian said.

He heard it come out of him in the voice he used for a thing that was a good idea, and he heard the shape of it about a second afterwards, and he did not take it back.

"That is not refusing me."

"No," said Iven Tallow. "I want to be exact about that, because I have watched men not understand it for about forty years. I have handed you the whole of it. What I am refusing is the sentence you have just said."

"Why?"

"Because you have said it at about eleven in the morning in a voice you use for things that are good ideas." He did not raise it. "And there is no market in this basin. There is a yard with about nineteen people in it, and a clerk with a page that has nothing ruled on it, and a boy who counts things that stop. You have said *then give it to a market* in front of both of them and neither of them has a market in them anywhere."

"I wrote *we* on a page that went up that lane."

"You wrote *we* and it came back with a figure on the back of it," said Iven Tallow, "and that is the best thing anybody in this basin has done to me since I came up the hill and I have not stopped thinking about it. And you have been in this yard eighty mornings and you keep saying *we* like a man who has not worked out that a district is the thing that cannot be found."

---

Adrian stood there with his mouth open and the four sentences came back at him from about nine feet away, and they were not hers, because she was at the top of eleven feet and had gone up the bank before he did, and what came back was the shape of what she had said.

*Do not stand between him and that wall.*

He was not standing between him and the wall. He was standing in front of a man who had just given away the only fact about himself that had cost him anything, and Adrian Vale could not look at a fact of that kind without putting a term on it, and he had been doing it since he was about twenty-one years old in rooms where nobody had ever stopped him.

"Sit down," said the man on the stones.

---

Neither of them had expected him.

"You have not asked him one question," said the man of about sixty-four. "You have had about nine answers this afternoon and you have offered him a third thing, and he has given you two things he has never said to anybody and you are standing there trying to buy him."

"I am not buying him."

"You are doing the thing where somebody tells you something true and you turn it into a price, and I have watched you do it to about four people in a fortnight," said the man of about sixty-four, "and you did it to a woman with a scale about six mornings ago and she read you the whole of it in a yard. Sit down."

Adrian sat down on the stones at the bottom of a low wall, on the side away from the sun, about nine feet from a man of sixty-one who had come down that bank at about a quarter past three to say the true thing about the Long Fracture in front of him.

Iven Tallow watched him do it.

"There," said Iven Tallow. "Now I will carry on, because you are the only person I have ever told this to who has had somebody in the room with him, and that is the reason I did it here and not in a room, and you were right to tell me not to do it in a room and I want you to hear yourself having been right about one thing this afternoon."

---

"So you took it into yourself."

"I took the obligation into myself. That is what a custodian is; it is not a title, it is a temporary hand on something the public record cannot safely leave lying about. And I left it open, on the grounds that I would hold it until there was somebody better than me."

"And there has not been somebody better than you."

"There has not been somebody better than me in seventeen years. I have looked. Twice a year I have gone round this basin and I have read every fair copy of every lot in it and I have spoken to about nine thousand people about a thing that most of them have never heard of, and I have not found one. And I did not know that for about six years and I have known it for eleven, and I would like both of those in the record as well, because a man who says he looked for twenty years is doing a different thing from a man who says he found nobody in eleven."

---

Adrian stood with his hands at his sides and did not interrupt, and Mara Quill's four sentences were going round in his head and he was about nine feet from both men and could have walked away and did not.

"So the Measure did not close," Adrian said.

"The Measure did not close. It sat open for about eleven weeks with me standing in the gap where the two witnesses should have been, and on the morning it was supposed to go over the rights went over, and there was nothing holding them, and there was one man in a hall in Alder Reach who had promised in front of a room that he would hold the whole of it and then did not, because he could not be in two places and he chose to be in one."

"That is the Long Fracture."

"That is the Long Fracture," said Iven Tallow. "Not a machine. Not a judgement. Not a judgement at all. A settlement that did not close, with one man in the hole in it, and a great deal of what has gone wrong in this basin in seventeen years has gone wrong down a crack that starts in that gap, and about nine people in this yard can tell you the crack and not one of them can tell you the gap. And there is one thing I did that the gap does not excuse. In the ninth year I put my name on the roll of a ward market when nobody had asked me to and nobody would have been able to stop me, and about four hundred people went without bread in the winter after that, and I have never been able to make that come out of this, and I have had about eight years to try."

---

"You are telling me this because you want me to do it your way."

"No," said Iven Tallow. "I am telling you because you are the only person in this basin who has spent eighty mornings doing the exact opposite of what I did, out loud, in public, at a price, in a yard, with a clerk writing it down and reading it back to you in front of everybody, and because I am sixty-one and I have run out of anybody else, and I would rather say that to your face than have you work it out in about four years and be disappointed about the shape of it."

Adrian sat down on the stones. He had not decided to. He found out about it afterwards.

"You kept it open," he said. "I have kept it shut. That is not the same thing and you know it is not."

"It is the same shape and it is a different sin," said Iven Tallow, "and I have had seventeen years to work out which of the two is worse, and I do not know, and I have been asked twice and I have given two different answers and both of them were true when I said them."

---

"Why did you disappear."

"Because I was the only thing left holding it and I could not hold it and be a person." He said it without any weight at all. "That is the whole of it and there is no second half of it anywhere. And I have been the only thing left holding it every morning since, and there is a difference between those two sentences which I have had a very great deal of time to look at, and I have never been able to make the difference into anything a person could use."

The man of about sixty-four had not moved. He had his hands on his knees and he was looking through the gap in the hedge at about two miles of nothing and the sun was behind the far side of it.

"I would like to ask you something," he said.

"You may."

"You have said a great deal and I have followed none of it, because I do not know what a Measure is and I have never in nine years had to." He turned his head about four inches. "Is it going to end."

---

Iven Tallow did not answer for a long time, and Adrian understood, some distance away and about nine years too late, that the man who came down that bank at about a quarter past three had known he was going to be asked exactly that, and had come down the bank anyway.

"That is the one thing I came to this yard to find out," Iven Tallow said, "and I cannot answer it, and I have not been able to answer it for seventeen years, and I have never said it in a yard before and I would have gone on not saying it if this yard had been empty."

The man of about sixty-four looked at Adrian.

"You have been in this yard about two months," he said. "You have been the only person in it that could be asked anything by anybody, and there has not been one thing come down that lane for you in all that time and there is a figure of forty coppers against your name on a piece of paper. I am not asking you to know. I am asking you to answer."

"I don't know," said Adrian.

---

The man of about sixty-four looked at him for a while and then back at the sun.

"Aye," he said. "That is all right." He was quiet for a moment. "I have had a figure in a book about that man for nine years. It says eleven seconds. Nobody has ever asked me whether it is right and I have never once wanted to know, and I would like to keep it."

---

The old man got up off the stones before Adrian did, and picked up the case, and stood a long moment with his hand on it.

"There is one way to end it," he said. "I am telling you and I am not asking you for anything. A closing that says out loud that there is an override and what the override is, and states the remedy that takes its place, and is accepted by two fresh independent witnesses who understood the terms and were not given them by anybody. Then it is not there. Not because somebody beat it. Because it was named, and a thing that has been named can be replaced."

"Two."

"Two," said Iven Tallow. "And you have not got two."

"I have a boy who counts things and a clerk who has been frightened for four days about not offering a column to a man who reads four figures off a wall."

"That is two people."

"That is two people who have never witnessed a term in their lives," said Iven Tallow. "And a witness who does not know what the term costs is not a witness. He is a person standing near a thing. That man has been standing near a thing for fifteen years and there is a line in a book that says so and nobody has ever asked him to describe it, and if you put his name against that clause he will be a decoration and you will have found two names and no witnesses and you will have done precisely what I did, with better manners and a shorter distance to run."

---

Then he stopped and looked at Adrian for a moment longer than was comfortable.

"One more thing," he said, "and then I will let you go up that bank, because you are going to be able to make this fit what you have been doing for eighty mornings, and I would like it said now, in front of the man on the stones, that it will not be true."

"Go on."

"What I did is not the beginning of what you are doing," said Iven Tallow. "What I did is what happens when a person is the only one. And in about four years you are going to be in a position to be the only one, and you will be about forty-two, and there will be nobody in the room but you, and you will not be able to ask the yard, and there will be nobody in the room to ask. And I would like that entered against me by somebody with a pencil in about four years, and I would like you to be the man who is not able to say it out loud, because that is the whole of what I have come down this bank for."

---

Adrian went up the bank at about ten past four and did not go to the boards.

He stood at the top of eleven feet with the low wall about nine feet to the left of him and the sun behind the far side of it. A man of fifty-six was saying four figures off that wall in the ordinary voice to about nine people and got all four of them, and the clerk's pencil went into a book, and a boy of seventeen wrote something on the back of his own sheet in a box he had ruled that week for a different reason.

"Five hundred," said the man of fifty-six, to nobody, and then went home without waiting to be asked anything by anybody, which is what he does, and which is the first thing anybody has noticed about it that was not a figure.

The two men at the bottom of that bank did not come up for about another hour. When they did, the one of them who was sixty-one went past the near end of the second table and put his hand flat on the book for the length of a breath without opening it, and the one who was about sixty-four went to the lane and stood at the top of it with his hands in his pockets looking at a hill, and the boy of seventeen, who had watched all of it from the boards, wrote one line on the back of his own sheet and did not read it out and did not offer it to anybody, and it said that a man who had been dead for seventeen years had told a man of thirty-eight the truth at the foot of a low wall and that there had been about nine people at the top of eleven feet who had heard all of it and had not come down, and that the nine of them were not witnesses of anything and had been in the room anyway.
