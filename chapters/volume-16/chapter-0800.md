# Chapter 800: The Eighty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Of Fifty-Six Said The Four Off That Wall For The Five Hundred And Twenty-Fifth Time And Then A Man Of Thirty-Eight Said Out Loud In A Yard In Front Of About Nineteen People The Sentence This Whole Run Of Mornings Has Been Walking Towards And Then Two People Refused To Write It Down

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and thirty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and ninety-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand and fourteen days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and twenty-eight days. Six hundred and eighty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and forty-eight days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run, and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page on any morning of this run, and the man who reads that wall out loud has never once been asked about it, and nobody in this yard has ever heard anybody propose anything else. Four hundred and three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Four hundred and eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and seventy-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and eighty-one days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is four hundred and seventeen days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and fifty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and forty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move on any morning of these ten. The number of documents this district does not own is four. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four. The number of conditions with no end on it is four, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and there is no instrument in this basin that can pay it, and the whole of what this district has to pay anybody with is a book.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank one hundred and fourteen mornings and moved this morning from one hundred and thirteen. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for sixty-four mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is eighty-four, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move on any morning of these ten. The lane count is twenty-three and did not move this morning. The lot book is on the boards with five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the name in it does not satisfy the rule made out loud in that yard sixteen mornings ago, and the heading over that column still says *whoever*, and nobody in this district knows who ruled it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and twenty-fifth of those mornings, and he has said them to about nine people at a time and to one woman with a key and to one woman with a beam scale and to a man of forty-one who comes up that lane twice a day and to nobody, and not one of them has ever asked him what any of it is for.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and thirty-ninth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and thirty-eight of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the twenty-ninth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and neither of them knew what was being decided about the district of Lot Seventeen at about ten o'clock, and neither of them would have wanted to.

---

There was no key in that yard on the eighty-fourth morning and about nineteen people in it and every one of them knew where everybody else was.

"It sits at about ten," said the woman who keeps a scale, from her own step, about forty feet off, without coming up the bank. "I have known since the sixty-fifth morning and I have not said it and I am not going to say it now either, and the reason I am not is that a yard that has been told a time is a yard that stops doing the things it does with the rest of the morning."

---

So nothing happened in that yard from about ten until about four, and it was not a slow morning and it was not a quiet one. It was about nineteen people doing the ordinary work of a district that had nothing to do, and the man who digs loam went down the bank and came back up with nothing, and the woman who keeps a scale weighed four pounds of nails for a man who was not in that yard, and the boy of seventeen did not write anything down at all for the whole of the middle of the day, which is the longest anybody in that yard can remember.

Adrian came down the eleven feet at about half past two and sat on the end of the second table, which he has not done since before the woman with a key came up that lane, and stayed there.

---

"You are on my table," said the man who sits at the end of it.

---

"I know."

---

"That is the whole of what I have got," said the man at the end of the second table. "I put you on it. You can stay. If I move you off it I am a person who moves a man of thirty-eight off a piece of wood at half past two on the morning a room sits nine miles up that hill, and I have been at this table for fifteen years and careful in this yard for about nine of them, and I am not going to be the other thing."

---

At about half past three the man of sixty-one said the thing he had come up that bank to say and had been not saying since the first afternoon he set that case down on those stones, which is twenty-nine afternoons running, and he said it to the stones, which is what he does.

"It does not matter what happens today," he said. "Not because I do not care about it. Because there is a clause nine miles up that hill that has no end on it, and the only person who can end it is me, and I have not ended it, and I am not going to end it today whatever a room says, and a room cannot end it either, because a room is not the holder. I have been the holder for seventeen years. I have been the man in the gap where two witnesses should have been for seventeen years, and in about four years somebody is going to come up that lane and offer me a way out of the gap and I am telling this yard now, in front of about nineteen people, that I have thought about it and I am not going to take it."

---

"Who is going to offer it," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"I do not know and I have not been told and that is the safest place I have been in about seventeen years." He did not get up off the stones. "And I would like it noticed that I have said this out loud in a yard this morning without being asked, which is a thing I have watched four of you do in the last ten mornings and have not been able to do myself until now, and that I worked out while you were standing there that the reason was not courage. It was that there is nobody left in this basin to ask me anything and I would rather be the one who says it than the one who is asked."

---

The man of about sixty-four got up off the stones' neighbour at about a quarter past four and picked his coat up off the grass and did not put it on.

"I go out at about half past four in the morning," he said, to about nineteen people. "It is nine miles up that hill. I have gone out at about half past four for nine years and I am going out at about half past four this morning and there is going to be carts on that road at about five and I am not going to stop and I am not going to be looked at and I would like the yard to know that I have been on that road every evening for nine years and that on a great many mornings of this run of mornings I went past a building where a room was deciding something about a district of about nineteen people, and I did not stop, and nobody has ever asked me why, and I would have said."

He went up the lane. The lane count is twenty-three and did not move, and does not move for anybody going up that hill, and there is nobody in that district who has ever proposed that it should.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them.

And then he put his hand on the stone, which he does, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet were still standing exactly where they had been at ten o'clock, and the clerk's pencil was not in her hand, and nobody in that yard had said a whole sentence since about half past three.

Adrian Vale got off the end of the second table.

---

"I have got a thing to say," he said, "and I am going to say it now, and I am going to say it in the ordinary voice, and I am going to say it to about nineteen people and not to the woman with a key because she is nine miles up that hill and will not hear it and I would not have wanted her to, and I am saying that out loud so that if she hears about it afterwards it is not a surprise to her."

---

Somebody said *go on* and about nine people did not say it.

---

"Everything this basin writes says that it is a record of a thing and not of a person." He was standing at the near end of the boards and he did not have a page in his hand and there was nothing on the boards in front of him. "It is the sentence every clerk in this basin says out loud and it is true of every one of them and it does not matter how careful the clerk is. The founder's clause is a record of a thing that was never a thing — it is a record about who may own a bus, a lamp, a ferry and a bell, and a market is not a bus and a market is not a lamp, and there is not one market in this basin that could own one of those things, and that is the design of it and the whole of the design of it — and there is a person's name inside it, and nobody in this basin will be able to tell you whose, and the man who wrote it has been in this yard for about twenty-nine afternoons saying that he will not say."

He stopped there and about four people in that yard looked at the stones.

"I have never read it," he said. "I have not been in this yard as long as the wall has, or the table, or the man at the end of it, or the boy with the sheet, and I have not read the clause and neither has the man who reads that wall and neither has a boy of seventeen with a sheet for every person in this yard. We are all of us people who have heard about a thing."

---

Then he said the other half of it, and he said it slowly, and he said it to the man at the wall as much as to anybody.

"And I know it. I do not know it from reading it. I know it because this yard has been doing the same thing at every end of itself for as long as I have been standing in it, and I have watched it happen.

"The clerk entered a sentence on the seventy-fourth morning about a column with one name in it, and the name in it does not satisfy the rule that governs it, and she cannot take it out. The boy said a figure out loud in this yard five mornings ago and the right one and the wrong one are now in two books and neither of them can be struck, and that is a rule this district made about itself and it is a good rule and it has cost that boy the only instrument he has. The woman on the step came up that bank five mornings ago and said the fifth of the five was a shape, because there is no figure in this district that a person can be weighed against. A woman on a working list nine miles up that hill walked two miles here and said it was not a shape, it was nine people who are already paid by something that has no figures in it. Both of them are describing the same absence from opposite ends and neither of them has been paid anything, and the count is still five. A man at the end of this table made a rule about who may be entered and the rule is perfect and it has never refused anybody because there has never been anybody for it to refuse. And a column with a heading over it says *whoever* and nobody in this district knows who ruled it and I have had every morning of this district with my hands on it."

---

The yard did not make a noise.

"A record that cannot name the person it burdens is not protecting anybody," said Adrian Vale. "It is only hiding."

---

Nobody in that yard said anything for long enough that the man of fifty-six, standing about eleven feet off the wall with his hand on the stone, took his hand off it and put it back on again.

---

"Mara Quill," he said. "Will you enter that."

---

"No," said Adrian, before she had answered.

---

"She has not said no yet," said the man of fifty-six, from about eleven feet off the wall.

"She is going to, and she is going to say no for exactly the reason I am going to say, and I would rather save her the sentence than hear her say it." He did not look at the clerk. "A sentence about records, entered in a record. It would be a record about a record. And it would have a heading over it, and the heading would be about a thing, because that is the only thing anybody in this basin knows how to rule a heading about, and in about four years somebody would come along and read that heading and see a man of thirty-eight's opinion about a book in a lot book, and the opinion would be about a thing and not about a person, and it would be the same defect as every other one in this basin, and it would be the last one, and it would be mine."

---

"Then it goes nowhere," said the man at the end of the second table. "You have said the only true thing anybody has said in that yard since I came up that lane and it is going to be a thing in the air over a bank."

---

"Yes," said Adrian.

---

"I have got about four years," said the man at the end of the second table, "and about nine people have now heard it, and every one of those nine can be asked about it afterwards, and in about four years somebody is going to ask each of them separately what a man of thirty-eight said at four o'clock on the eighty-fourth morning. That is a thing that happens. That is the only instrument there is."

---

"You will remember it," said Mavis Dorr.

---

"I will remember it in the way I remember things," said the man at the end of the second table, "which is exactly, in the order it was said, with nothing added to it. That is what I have. It is not nothing."

---

Orren Pike had his sheet out and had it against the boards and his thumb on the corner of it, and he had not written anything and everybody in that yard could see that he had not written anything.

---

"No," he said.

---

"You have been writing down everything anybody has said in that yard for four years," said Adrian.

---

"I have been writing down every morning for four years," said the boy of seventeen. "Every morning, in pencil, in the margin, and I have never offered one to anybody, and that is the only reason it has been mine. This morning you are asking me to put a sentence in the body of the sheet, where the columns are, where the row for a person is, where a person could be looked up by anybody who ever holds this. And the day that sheet is found, every sentence in the body of it is a record, and it is a record about a record, and I would have written the last true thing in this district into the one thing in it that cannot be used."

He put the sheet away.

"You do not get to spend mine either," he said, to a man of thirty-eight who had said the identical sentence to him about a figure five mornings ago and had been told he did not."

---

Nobody in that yard wrote it down.

At about a quarter past six the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall again on his way down the lane to nobody at all, and got all four of them, and the lane count is twenty-three, and the bid has been open four hundred and forty-eight days, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five.

Adrian Vale was still at the top of that bank. He has been in that yard one hundred and five mornings. He has not been given a bed, a wage or an office, has not asked for any of the three, and has not been in a room nine miles up that hill, and there is a length of fencing wire in the pocket of his coat about four miles from a gap of a mile and a half that he has not been to, and the man at the end of the second table has said out loud in about nineteen people's hearing that he is not going to ask him what it is for.
