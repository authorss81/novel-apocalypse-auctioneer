# Chapter 752: The Thirty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Woman Of About Fifty-Eight Was Asked What The Fourth Line Said And Had To Go And Look

The clerk had those boards to herself from a quarter to eight until about nine, and Adrian had worked out in about three weeks that this was the only hour in which anything in that district could be said to anybody, and had spent four of the last five mornings standing at the top of that bank like a man waiting for a bus.

The morning went in the way it goes in. The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move. Four hundred and ninety-one days is the age of that figure. The board carries six hundred and fifty, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and sixty-six days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty days, and six hundred and forty-one days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move. The number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is sixty-six and moved this morning from sixty-five. The count of things this district has made is thirteen and did not move. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for sixteen mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is thirty-seven, on the word of a man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand since the sixth of the fifth month is eighteen and did not move this morning.

Four hundred days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and fifty-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it. Three hundred and sixty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month, and that counts the mornings that have gone by and not the mornings a month has in it. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and thirty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and sixty-nine days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in this yard watched it.

Three hundred and nine marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are two hundred and ninety-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and seventy-seventh of those mornings, and nobody has asked him what one of them is for.


She came down the bank at about ten to six every working morning and read three lines in that lot book standing up about four feet off the fourth one, and she did not stop, and she did not look at the fourth one.

On the thirty-sixth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month Adrian was standing four feet off her when she got to it, and he asked her a question instead of moving, which was a piece of rudeness he had planned for two days and did not enjoy.

"Who wrote the fourth line."

She stopped. Which she has not done in fifteen years, by the count of the man of fifty-six, who has not been counting her, but who turns up.

"You've had that fence wire in your pocket fifty mornings," she said, "and now you've got a question."

"I'm working through them."

"Mm."

She read the first three lines again. She read them the way a person reads a thing they have read so often that the reading has stopped being about the words and become about the hands.

"It's in my hand," she said.

"I know it's in your hand."

"Then you know who wrote it."

"Mara wrote it," Adrian said. "Your hand or hers. One of you dictated and one of you wrote, and you've never once said which, and I have read four hundred documents in this basin written by people who would not say that."

The woman of fifty-eight did the thing she had never done in fifteen years, which was to put her hand flat on the page, the way you cover a line when you are going to say it out loud and want to be able to point at it while you do.

"It says: *no name is entered for the man below here.* Nine words. I said them. She wrote them. He dictated."

Adrian waited. There was a shape in that sentence and he could see its edges and he did not want to put his hand in it.

"And the man below here is the man the third line is about."

"He dictated it, and he was not the clerk," she said. "Fifteen years ago. He came and found me — I carried for the unlisted then, same as now, it's the only trade I've got and I don't get paid for it, don't start — and he said, he wanted a line on that book saying that his name was not going to be on it."

"He wanted protection from his own line."

"He wanted it *written*," the woman said. "Do you understand the difference, and I'll wait. He could have gone and asked a clerk to write it. He could have said nothing and gone and been nobody in particular. He came and found a woman who carries things for people who aren't on anything, and he paid me — he paid me, that's the part nobody has ever asked me about — he paid me the first day's wage for a carrier, which is nine coppers and which is more money than I have had in this district in six years, and he said, write it down, so that if anyone comes up that lane and reads this they will see that there was no name here before they see that there is a man here."

Adrian's mouth had gone dry.

"And she wrote it."

"She wrote it because she wrote everything," the woman said. "Nine words, one hand, one sitting, at about half past two in the afternoon. I did not want my hand anywhere near it, and she never asked me to, and she has recopied that fair copy once a year for fifteen years without ever once putting her own date under it, which is the single most decent thing anybody has ever done in this district and she has never been thanked for it either." She took her hand off the page. "It is in her hand under nine words of mine, and it has been sitting on that table for fifteen years saying *no name is entered* in a book where the next line down has been sending strangers up that bank since the week it was written."

"So that's the answer," she said. "You asked me who wrote the fourth line and you've got it, and now I want you to understand something about what you've been told, because you are a man who has spent nine years telling this district that a figure may not be corrected. Well. That is my figure. That is nine words I said out loud for nine coppers to a man who could not pay me either, and I have read them every working morning for fifteen years standing up, because if you sit down you start reading them properly and if you read them properly you have to have an opinion."

"Do you have an opinion?"

"I've got a *want*," she said. "There's a difference and I'm too tired to explain it to a man who counts."

---

The clerk was at the near end of the first table at about a quarter to eight and had entered the morning and had not looked up, and Adrian came and stood at the near end of it with the fencing wire still in his pocket and said, "What did the fourth line cost?"

"Mara," said the clerk.

"Mara, what did the fourth line cost?"

The clerk put her pencil down. That was the second time in fifty mornings, and this one Adrian could feel all the way down his back.

"You called me that yesterday," she said. "On the bank, in front of nineteen people, when you told them you were thirty-eight and not what we all thought. You called me *Mara* and then you didn't use it again, and I have been sitting here since a quarter to eight wondering whether that was a mistake or a door."

"It was a mistake."

"Good," she said. "Then say the other thing."

Adrian stood there.

"I don't know your name," he said.

"You wrote nine words of mine and my own address into a public book at half past seven this morning," she said. "You know my name. You have known my name since I was nineteen and I came to this table and put my hand on a page nobody else would touch. I have been *the clerk* in your mouth for about two years, which is longer than any of the other people in this yard can remember, and which I have not objected to, and which I now find I object to, and I would like it settled one way or the other today."

"Mara," Adrian said. "Your name is Mara Quill. You were nineteen the morning you opened that page and you have written nineteen in the corner of every entry in it for fifteen years, because that was your age on the first one and a figure may not be corrected in this district. You are thirty-four. You have been signing your own work with a figure fifteen years out of date for longer than most of the things in this basin have been standing, and you wrote the rule that stops you fixing it."

The clerk of fifteen years looked at him for a long moment.

"Say that again when you mean it," she said.

"I mean it."

"Then write it down," she said, "and not in the book. In your own. And get it right, because if you get it wrong about my age you will get something else wrong that costs me a job."

---

He wrote it down. And then he wrote four other things down, because he had come up that bank with a list he had made the night before and had been carrying down the hill for twenty minutes without opening it.

Nine years and eleven days since the stranger first came down that lane.

Four strangers in fifty mornings.

A page that comes for a number and does not know it has come for a number.

Nobody in this district can tell him where those pages are printed.

And the fifth thing, which was the one he had come down the bank to ask about, and which he had not asked, and which was: **the whole mechanism of this basin works on a piece of paper and every piece of paper in it comes from the same room, and I have been in that room, and nobody here knows that, and the only reason they don't know is that I have never said so.**

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and his hands empty, as he was every morning and had been for nine years. That is his three hundred and ninety-first night of that run and he has slept on three hundred and ninety of them, and nobody in this district has asked him in nine years what the run is or where it goes, and about four people within twenty feet of that wall have said since that he has now gone three hundred and ninety-one nights of a run without being given one thing and without being asked one thing, and that the count of nights is a count and not a figure behind which anything was bought or owed.


He looked at the list for a while.

Then he put it back in his pocket and went down the bank, which was a piece of cowardice he would have to pay for later and did not enjoy paying for at the time.

---

The boy was at the near end of the first table at about ten, doing what he had done at about ten on every one of fifty mornings, which was counting what people had said out loud and writing the number in the margin of a sheet of his own outside both columns.

He had been doing it for so long and so quietly that Adrian had stopped seeing him do it, which is the specific failure Adrian was living through: you stop seeing the instrument that has been measuring you.

Adrian had learned the boy's age wrong. He had assumed the yard was right about *about nineteen* and had not asked, which made him exactly the thing he accused everybody else of being.

"You're not nineteen," he said.

"No," the boy said.

"Seventeen?"

"Seventeen in March." He wrote something. "Mum had me at thirty-one." He did not look up. "Why, is that going in the book?"

"No," Adrian said. "That was for me. I've been guessing at people for nine years and I've just been told what that costs."

The boy put his pencil down, which Adrian had now seen twice in one morning and had begun to understand as the local equivalent of a raised voice.

"Who told you to guess?"

"Nobody," Adrian said. "Nobody had to. It's what a yard does when nobody asks anybody anything."

"Right," the boy said, and picked the pencil up again.

Adrian looked at the sheet.

It was two columns ruled on the left and one ruled on the right, and it was not in the clerk's hand and it was not in any hand Adrian had seen in this basin, and the left-hand columns were in the boy's careful upright letters and they were headed — he could see, because the boy had not thought to hide it, because nobody had ever come close enough to look —

WHEN / WHO SAID IT / HOW LONG IT TOOK / WHO WAS STANDING NEXT TO THEM

And Adrian's stomach went cold in a way he had not felt since a bridge in Uplands, because he understood in about a second and a half what that sheet was, and what nobody in nine years had thought to make.

It was not a record of what this district said.

It was a record of who was standing next to them when they said it.

The fourth column — who was standing next to them — was the one that made it worth something. Because a figure on a wall is a figure about nobody. A figure said out loud with four other men within hearing is a figure that a man can be held to by five other men. Somebody, somewhere, a long time ago, had understood that the difference between a record that can be used and a record that can only be looked at is the presence of an audience.

And this district, which has spent nine years insisting out loud that a public record is a record of a thing and not of a person, had a boy of seventeen in it who had quietly worked out how to make one into a record of a person without ever once being told to.

"What's the second thing in the right-hand column for," Adrian said.

The boy shut the sheet.

"Not you," he said.

"Mate—"

"Not you," the boy said, and he did not raise his voice, and there was nothing in his voice at all, which was worse. "You've got a list in your pocket. I watched you read it and I watched you put it back. You've been round this yard fifty mornings asking people about fifth lines and you're carrying a list of five things and you've not asked one of us a single question about any of them, and now you want the sheet."

"That's not fair."

"No," the boy agreed. "It's not. But you're the man who reads things and I'm the man who writes them down, and if I hand you that sheet you will read it in a room by yourself with the door shut and call it a public record, and it won't be one, it'll be yours."

"I would publish it."

"You'd publish it on a *day*," the boy said. "There'd be a day about it. There'd be a date and a reason and about nine people in a yard, and you'd stand at the end of it and everyone would know that you had it."

Adrian found he had nothing to say, and it was a novel sensation, and it took him most of a minute.

"Orren," the boy said. "It's Orren. If you're going to use it."

He went back to counting and did not look up again, and Adrian stood there for a while longer, and then went and sat down on the boards of that first table, which nobody has ever done, and put his head in his hands for about nine seconds, and then got up, because there was a cart coming up the bank at about three with its near wheel still dragging.
