# Chapter 760: The Forty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Man Wrote An Answer Down And Showed It To The Yard Before He Let It Go Up That Lane

Four hundred and ninety-nine days is the age of the figure on the sheet at that gatepost, and the figure is four hundred and eleven. The board carries six hundred and fifty-eight, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-four days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and eighty-eight days, and six hundred and forty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning. The number of mornings the second table has stood at the foot of that bank is seventy-four and it moved this morning from seventy-three.

Four hundred and eight days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and sixty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and sixty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and thirty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and forty-one days back. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and seventy-seven days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and seventeen marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move, and nobody counted one this month, because a page in a stranger's hand is not a month.


He wrote it at about seven in the morning at the top of the bank, sitting on the boards of that first table with his knees up and a length of fencing wire in his pocket that he had still not looked at.

It was one page. It was the back of a stranger's page again, because there was nothing else in the district, and by the time he had got four lines onto it he had stopped twice and started again twice, and the second time he started again was because he had written *the market of Lot Seventeen*, which is not a market, it is a book on a table.

He wrote:

> *Lot Seventeen, at the top of a bank, in a yard, about nine miles from Alder Reach and forty from the gatepost where four hundred and eleven is written. The count of things this district has made is fourteen. Two of them are a book with five lines on it and a reading of four figures off a wall by one man twice a day, and neither of the two has ever been paid to anybody.*
>
> *We send a name. The name is Adrian Vale, it went into our book on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month in the column for whoever reads a thing out loud, and he will say it out loud in that room if he is asked to.*
>
> *We cannot send the other two things and we are not going to pretend we can send them. We have no instrument that can pay a person who is not in a household. We have had none for fifteen years. That is the fifth of five things this district does not have, it is named on our book every morning and paid on none of them, and the count of the five is still five. The reason it is not paid is that paying it would take an instrument, and an instrument is a thing a district makes.*
>
> *The ninth line of your notice says payment is to come out of the funds of the market sending the bearer, at the first sitting, in full. We hold nine coppers. The clerk can tell you which person every one of them belongs to, which is precisely why we do not send them: there is no form in this district that can take nine coppers off a person and put them against a year, and the five lines in our book have been trying to be that form for fifteen years and are four conditions and a man.*
>
> *If a person is required, then a person can be sent. If a figure and a term are required, then a figure and a term cannot be sent from here, because there is nothing in this district that would make either of them true on a day, and we are not going to write one that would not be.*
>
> *The name is Adrian Vale. He will say it. We do not know whether that is worth anything in that room and we are not going to guess.*

He read it four times and then took it down the bank and showed it to four people before he let anybody else have it, which is a thing he has done for nine years and has never done in this district, and which the clerk noted.

"You've written *we* eleven times," she said.

"I've written *we* seven times and *our* twice because there are nineteen of you and I have been here fifty mornings, and I counted, because there is a boy in this yard who would have and I did not want to have to be told."

"You've written it eleven times because it's easier to be a district than a man, and you've done it because you've got a name in a book now and a name in a book is the *worst* possible preparation for writing eleven *we*s." She read the second paragraph twice. "That's good. That's the good bit. Don't let anybody in this yard talk you out of that bit."

"Which bit."

"The one about the instrument." She tapped the fourth line. "You could have written three words on that line. You wrote the whole of it. And you did not put a figure on it, and I want you to notice that you didn't, because you could have. You could have written a number and put your own money against it and been here by the end of the week with a nice clean promise, and I would have had to spend four mornings arguing with a woman of about thirty-six and a boy of seventeen and a man who puts tables up, and I'd have won in the end, because I usually do, and you'd have gone up that lane with a lie on it that was true in your pocket."

---

He went up the bank and stood on the boards at about eleven and read it out loud, all of it, in a yard of about nineteen people, at about the speed of somebody reading a thing they had written themselves.

Nobody interrupted him, which he had not expected, because there were three separate people in that yard who could have taken it apart in a sentence.

When he had finished, the man who puts tables up said: "You're leaving without a number."

"I'm leaving without a number."

"A number's the easy bit. Everybody thinks the number's the hard bit because they've all got money. The hard bit is the *thing that makes it true on a day*, and you don't have one, and I have watched about nine men in this district promise you a number over the last nine years and I have watched every one of them be true on the day for about eleven months."

"And then what."

"And then it's not true," Seth said, "and there's no instrument to say so with, and that's how you get a book with five lines on it and four hundred and eleven on a sheet at a gatepost."

Nobody argued about any of it.

The woman of fifty-eight, at the back of the yard, said one sentence, which was: "A woman I carry for has been in Alder Reach. Four months back, and she came back, and I did not ask her what for, because I do not ask her what for." And then she stopped.

"You did not ask her," Adrian said.

"I did not ask her."

"Then I will," he said, "and it will be a different question, because I am a man who reads things, and I can ask a question out loud and tell her afterwards what I did with the answer, and that is the only difference between us and it is not nothing."

The woman of fifty-eight looked at him for a while, and then said, "Ask me on a morning when there's nothing on," and Adrian said that he would, and Orren wrote something in his margin, which was not a number.

And she said one more thing, out loud, in that yard. "Nine coppers. He paid me nine coppers for nine words fifteen years ago and I have not been able to spend them, because I do not know which of the five things this district does not have they belong to, and there is no form, and I have carried them in a cloth in my bag for fifteen years." She set the bag down on the boards. "That is what the figure would have to be made out of, isn't it. Not the coppers. The *form*." 

---

Three hundred and seventeen marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are three hundred and three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move, and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and it went on that book working exactly as it was written this morning with nobody's hand on it. The near rail has stood on the near side of that table for twenty-four mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-four, on the word of a man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his own hands, on his three hundred and ninety-ninth night of that run, having slept on three hundred and ninety-eight of them, and nobody in that yard went down the bank and said one word to him.


He went to find the man of fifty-six, because on the fourth morning the man had said the most careful sentence Adrian had heard in nine years, and because there was one question attached to it that Adrian had walked away from on purpose.

"You said there's one of the four you can't account for."

"Aye."

"You said you've been carrying it about four years."

"Aye."

"What would it take to settle it."

"Either a year of somebody writing the four down every morning and comparing them, or a man of about sixty-four at that wall at night with a candle," the man said, "and I have not got either and I am not going to get either, because the moment one of those things starts, the four figures stop being four figures a man says out loud and become four figures on a page, and then this yard has got a document about its own wall, and you know what a document about a wall turns into in about four years."

He turned his head slightly.

"I've had it out loud for four years," he said, "and I have never had it out loud to anyone. That's not nothing. That's the whole reason I said it in a yard and not to you, and you should take it as the compliment it is and not ask me for the figure."

Adrian stood there and felt the entire shape of his own life go past him.

"All right," he said. "Don't write it down."

"That's the first time anybody's said that to me."

"I'm not being kind," Adrian said. "I'd have written it by the end of the week and it would have been a document about four figures." He sat down on the boards. "So there's nothing I can do about it."

"No," the man said. "There isn't. And that's the first useful thing anybody's offered me in nine years, and you offered it by accident, and I'd like it noted that I noticed."

He turned back to the wall.

"I want it asked in a yard one morning," he said. "Out loud, with the yard in it. Not by you — you've only been here fifty mornings and you'll be gone. By whoever's here. And then it's not my document. It's theirs."

"Nobody's going to ask that."

"That's my business," the man said. "I've got a run of mornings."

---

The clerk would not open a column.

It took twenty minutes and it happened at about one and the argument was the best argument Adrian had been in for nine years, and it was about a boy of seventeen and an old record, and it was not won by the boy winning it.

"There are four columns on that page," she said, "and I know what they are and I wrote them. Figure. Where it came from. Who said it. Whether it was altered. There is not a fifth one and I am not making one for a boy who has had it in his coat for four years."

"It's not his, it's the district's."

"It is *absolutely* his. It is the only thing in this basin I have ever been asked to enter that nobody can be held to. If it goes in that book then every person who stands in this yard is in it, and every person in it can be asked about every other person in it, and the moment that happens one of them is going to want something out of it." She put her hand on the boards. "I have watched that happen in four districts. Not violently. Politely. One of them asks one of them for a figure, and the figure is given, because there is a page, and then a figure has gone into a page in a way that nobody voted for, and after that the page is a different instrument, and it belongs to whoever is most willing to use it."

"That's what I said," Orren said to Adrian, without turning round. "About a room."

"You said it about a room," Adrian said. "That was four mornings ago and it was the best thing anybody said in this yard and I've been carrying it about ever since and not doing anything with it because I like having it in my pocket."

"Everybody does."

---

What they did instead was worse and better.

Adrian put it to the man who puts tables up, because he was the one person in that yard who had spent nine years doing a thing for nothing and had opinions about who got the credit for it. He put it to the woman who keeps a scale. He put it to the man of fifty-six, who said, "Ask me when there is nothing on. I have nothing on most mornings," which turned out to be the first joke anybody had made in that yard in nine years and got about four seconds of noise out of nineteen people.

And then Orren put it to the clerk himself, standing on the boards with his sheet under his arm, in fifty-one words, which he counted before he said them, which is in the margin of his own sheet, and which took her completely off guard.

"If you write the column, then you're the one who fills it in, and that's wrong. So don't write it. Write the *rule* and put a rule on the page, and then it's the yard's and not yours and you can't be asked for the contents because the contents aren't yours."

The clerk of this district, who invented the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud and filled it in herself four mornings ago with a hand that was not steady, stood at the near end of the boards for a while and did not say anything.

Then she wrote four lines on the bottom of the page, in her clear small hand, and turned it round.

> *There is no column of who was present.*
> *Any person in this yard may write, on any day of their choosing, on any paper they are carrying, who was standing near them when they said a thing out loud.*
> *No such paper is to be entered in this book by anybody, including the clerk.*
> *Entered by M. Quill. Not asked for. I would like it known that I was talked into this and that I think it is probably right, and that those two facts are going in the book next to each other because one of them without the other is a lie.*

That got read out at about four o'clock by the man of fifty-six, who has read four figures off that wall twice a day for four hundred and eighty-five mornings, and who asked a question about it first, and who was answered, and who then said: "That is the longest thing that has been said in that yard in nine years and there is not one word of it about a person, and I have noticed that, and I would like somebody in particular to notice that I noticed."

---

The answer went up that lane at about half past four in the hands of a man of about twenty-nine who drove a cart with a near wheel that still dragged, because Adrian wanted it to go with somebody the district knew and not with a courier, and because the cart driver had been coming up that hill for nine years and being handed nothing and would, in Adrian's judgement, enjoy it.

It is a page and not a lot. There is no object on it, and no use, and no toll, and no bearer, and no term, and no release, and no remedy — which is six of the seven parts, and the seventh is a person, and Adrian had checked twice, standing on the boards in the cold with the string still round it, because he is a man who has read four hundred lots and cannot help himself. The one part it does have is the one that everybody in this basin has spent fifteen volumes saying must never be printed on a page, and he has printed it, and he did it in a yard, and nineteen people watched him do it.

The number of people who have come down that lane with a page in their hand since the sixth of the fifth month is nineteen, and it did not move this morning.

The clerk entered that at about five. Then Orren said out loud, in front of nine people at the boards, that a man had gone *up* that lane that afternoon with a page in his own hand, and the clerk entered that too, and neither of those two entries is the same entry. There is now no rule anywhere in this district about somebody going the other way. About four people near that wall have said since that this is the first morning in nine years that anybody has gone up that lane, and that nobody in this district has ever done it and nobody knows whether it makes a thing better.

---

He was standing at the top of the lane when the man came back down.

He was about fifty, in a coat that had been good, and he was not the man who had brought the page on the third morning, and he had not come down that lane in nine years, and there was nothing in either hand.

"You're Vale," he said.

"Yes."

"The clerk's clerk says you're the one in the column. She said it as a joke and then she stopped, which is how you tell." He looked at the yard behind Adrian — at the boards, at the wall with four figures on it, at the four of them still standing there at about five in the afternoon. "There's a page in Alder Reach with Room Four on it and four hundred and ten on the corner, and there's one with four hundred and eleven on the corner that has a hand in it, and about nine of us have been trying for four mornings to work out who wrote it. We did it from a bundle. We never once thought of coming up here."

"I know," Adrian said.

"I don't say it to be thanked. I say it because you're going up that lane in about nine mornings to stand in a room, and if you go up there with the answer your clerk just wrote you'll be the ninth person to walk in with a piece of paper and go away again, and the first eight are the reason there's a room." He turned the whole thing round so it was facing up the hill rather than down it. "What I've got for you is not a term. It's four mornings of a man's handwriting and a corner with a number on it, and it's the only copy, and I'd like it read out in that yard by somebody with no interest in it, and then I'd like it sent back."

Adrian looked at the page for a while.

"You're giving me the only copy."

"I'm giving you the only copy of a note a man took in a corridor," the man said. "I'll have the room. You'll have the note. That's not generosity, it's that a thing one man has seen in a corridor is worth about nine coppers and a thing nine people have said out loud is worth whatever they can hold it to."

He started up the lane and then stopped, without turning round.

"The notice said anybody could go up. Nobody's ever gone up. That's what I'm asking about."

Then he was gone, and the count did not move, and by about six the books were closed and both tables were bare.

At the foot of that bank the second table has stood seventy-four mornings now and it stood seventy-three yesterday, and the near rail has been on it twenty-four mornings with nobody's thanks on it, and the count of things this district has made is fourteen, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and the fifth of the five is still five.

The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud has one name in it, and it is going to be read out in a yard tomorrow morning by a man of fifty-six who has never been asked a question in nine years and who has four figures in his head and one name, and neither of those five things is a market, and the room in Alder Reach is going to have to decide what it wants.

And that is what happened on the forty-fourth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, and a man of about thirty-two who puts tables up went down that bank after the books were closed and fitted nothing, because there is nothing left on that table to do, and he stayed there anyway, and nobody thanked him, and he was going to be thanked on some morning or he was not.
