# Chapter 770: The Fifty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Boy Of Seventeen Counted A Man Out Loud In A Yard And Found Out That Nobody Had Ever Counted Him And That The Figure Going Round Was Fifty And Was Never Entered Anywhere

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and nine days is the age of that figure. Neither of them has moved on any morning since a boy of seventeen said one of them out loud in this yard on the thirty-seventh morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month and did not read the number back to himself, which is a different thing entirely and was not noticed at the time, and a figure that has been said out loud in a yard is a thing that happened and a figure on a sheet is a thing that is, and this district has spent fifteen years refusing to be told the difference.

The board carries six hundred and sixty-eight, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and eighty-four days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and ninety-eight days, and six hundred and fifty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and eighteen days is how long the bid has been open. It was not run, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page, and there is nobody in this district who would be surprised to find the same sentence in that book tomorrow morning, and nobody in this district has ever proposed anything else.

Three hundred and seventy-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and nothing correct has been written beside it. Three hundred and seventy-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and forty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-one days back, it is open, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and eighty-seven days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twenty-seven marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are three hundred and thirteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move, and nobody counted one this month, because a page in a stranger's hand is not a month and this month has had four of them. The number of documents this district does not own is four and did not move. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and did not move. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank eighty-four mornings and moved this morning from eighty-three. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for thirty-four mornings. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and it has not moved on any morning since four people drew a map on the back of a stranger's page and left a hole in it about four inches across, and the hole is on a page in a clerk's own book and the reason for the hole is entered and it is the only reason in that book that is about a place.

The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is fifty-four, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered, and the man it counts is the man who puts tables up and is not the man who keeps a tally and is not the man who mends fencing, and they are three different men, and the figure is a count of mornings and not a figure behind which anything is bought or owed, which the man who keeps a tally said out loud in this yard on the first morning of that book and which the man who puts tables up cannot now un-say, because on the fifty-first morning about eleven people thanked him for a rail.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and ninety-fifth of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundred and ninth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and eight of them. He was asked one question, on the forty-sixth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, by a man who had a reason and not an interest, and he answered it, and the answer is in a clerk's own book on a page with nothing ruled on it, and there is no line for him in the lot book and there never has been and the count of the five things this district does not have is five and the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household and it is not paid.

---

The figure that has been going round that yard since the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month is fifty, and it is in about nineteen mouths, and it is in no book.

Nobody counted it. That is the whole of it, and it took a boy of seventeen rather less than a minute to establish at about a quarter to four this afternoon, standing on the boards of the first table at the top of that bank with a pencil in his hand, and it is the single most useful thing he has done since he was eleven years old and stealing pieces of paper out of rooms.

"Fifty's wrong," Orren said.

There were about nineteen people in that yard and the man of fifty-six was at the wall and Adrian was nine feet off the near end of the boards with his hands at his sides.

"Fifty is what a man said in this yard on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month and it has been repeated about eleven times since and nobody has ever checked it, including me, and I have checked everything in this yard for four years." He turned the sheet round. "I have counted them off the boards. Somebody is on those boards at about a quarter to eight every morning and about four people come up that bank before nine, and I have counted you on the mornings when it was not one of those, out of my own page, and it is seventy."

"And the yard says two months, and the yard says fifty mornings, and neither of those is a count. The yard has been getting me wrong since I was about nine and it has never once had to be sorry, because a boy of about nineteen is a good enough figure for people who are not writing anything down. It is not good enough for me and I have known that for about four years and I have never had a reason to say so out loud."

---

"Say the number again," the clerk said.

"Seventy."

"Nobody is going to argue with you about whether you counted. I want to know what you counted."

"The mornings he was on this bank," the boy said. "I have not got a column for it. I have got a column for what was said and a column for the figure and a column for the day it was said and a column for who was standing next to them, and there has never been anything on that page about a man being *present*, because I count things that stop and he is the one person in this yard who has not stopped once, and I did not have a box for it, and I have made one this afternoon, on the back, in pencil, in the time it took to say so, and I have not entered it anywhere and I am not going to."

He put the pencil down on the boards.

"I am not going to take fifty off it either," he said, "because fifty is not wrong. Fifty is what was said. Seventy is what there is. And if I say a figure in a yard and then a different figure tomorrow then I am a man with a page and not a boy with a sheet, and the whole of the argument I have had with all of you for eleven mornings is that those two are different and I have just watched a man thank a man for a number and I am not doing it to him or to anybody."

---

The man of fifty-six read the four figures off that wall at about four and got all four of them, and then he stood still for a moment with his hand flat on the stone, and then he said:

"Six hundred and sixty-eight, nine hundred and eighty-four, six hundred and ninety-eight, and six hundred and fifty-nine," he said, "and that is the four hundred and ninety-fifth of those mornings, and this morning there is a fifth number in this yard and it is not on a wall and it is not mine, and I have read four figures out loud twice a morning for four hundred and ninety-five mornings and I have never once read one figure instead of another, and this morning there are two of them and both of them are true and I do not know what to do with that, and that is the first time in my life I have not known what to do with it."

He looked at the boy.

"Say it again," he said. "I would like to be in it."

The boy said it again, in the ordinary voice, in that yard, in front of about nineteen people, and it was the first figure the man of fifty-six had ever read off anything that was not on a wall, and he got it right the first time, and nobody wrote it down, and about four people heard it and have since disagreed about it.

---

The clerk entered both figures and entered no reason for either, which is what she did on about four of the eleven mornings before this one and is what she has done every morning of her life, and then she entered the other thing, in her clear small hand, and read it out because that is what she does now.

> *Entered on the fifty-fourth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, at about four, by M. Quill. Two figures about the same person, and neither of them is struck.*
>
> *Fifty is a figure that was said out loud in this yard on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month and has been said since by about eleven people. It was not entered at the time. It is entered now, at the old value, and the old value stands.*
>
> *Seventy is a figure that a person said out loud in this yard at about a quarter to four this afternoon, before I entered it, on the word of a person counting mornings off his own page and not off a book, and no name goes in beside it because I do not have a name for the counting and the counting is not mine.*
>
> *Entered that a figure may not be corrected, struck, replaced, superseded or taken out of that book, and that I wrote that rule when I was about twenty-five and I have kept it for nine years over about nine figures and I have never once had to put two figures about one person in a morning, and that this morning I have, and that the two of them are not in conflict because one of them is a thing somebody said and one of them is a thing that happened, and I have never had to write that sentence before and I have thought about it for the length of a walk to the wall and back and it is correct.*

---

The woman of fifty-eight came up that lane at about ten to six.

She had her bag on her arm and she had been up that lane and down it and back again and she had not asked anybody to come with her and about four people had wanted to. She came to the near end of the boards and put her bag down and did not sit on anything.

"I have told her," she said. "I told her that a man she has never met took a page out of a corridor and wrote her name on it and carried it nine miles down a hill, and that a woman with a scale read it out in a yard in front of about nineteen people, and that I said it out loud in this yard ten mornings ago and did not tell her I had. And she was not angry. That is the part I am not going to be able to put down for a year."

"What did she say."

"She said, *that is what not asking is for*," the woman of fifty-eight said. "And then she said, *you have been carrying things for me for nine years and there was one in your bag the whole time and you did not know, and that is not a fault, and do not go asking anybody in that yard about it either, because they will write it down.*"

She picked the bag up again.

"And I am going to have to go and ask somebody in that yard about it, and I am not going to write my name on a page to do it, and I have thought about that for about an hour and I have got to the bottom of it, and I am going to say it in a yard and then somebody can come up that lane and ask after me, and that is what happened to her and I am not going to pretend I have worked out a better way than she has not."

---

"My name is Mavis Dorr," the woman of fifty-eight said, "and I am fifty-eight, or about it, and I carry for people who are not on anything, and I have been in this yard every working morning for nine years going up that bank at ten and down it at ten to six, and there is not one thing on any page in this district that says so, and there is a man at the end of that table who wrote himself a line when he was twenty-two and I did not, and I have spent nine years telling myself that was because he asked and I was never asked."

She looked at the clerk.

"I am not asking you to put it in the column. I have read the column and I know what the boy said about the heading and I would not put a shopkeeper next to him in it for anything." She turned round. "I am asking you to put it somewhere. Any page. Yours. The back of a book. I do not care. I have worked out this morning that a name a person says out loud in a yard is a thing nine people heard, and about nine people can be asked, and that is not protection, and that the only protection anybody in this basin has ever had is being the one nobody can look up, and I have been that for nine years by accident and I have watched it cost a woman I carry for four months of her life."

---

Mara Quill wrote it on the page with nothing ruled on it, under the line about a man at the foot of a wall, and entered that the entry was made at the word of the person it is about, out loud, in a yard, at about ten to six, and that there is no column for it and there is not going to be one, and that a clerk in this district has now got two lines on a page that has nothing on it and neither of them is in the lot book, and that the count of the five things this district does not have is five and it is still five and she has entered the number of the five and not the number of the lines on her own page because the two are not the same thing and she has been wrong about that once.

---

Tarin Kest was still in that yard at about a quarter to six and had been since eleven, and he had watched all of it from about nine feet away with his waybill board under his arm and had not said one word, and he said one now.

"Nine mornings," he said, first of all. "A man came down this lane on the forty-fourth morning and told you that you would go up it in about nine mornings to stand in a room. I was not here. I have been told since, by a woman with a scale, and I have sat up there for seven hours and waited for you to say it, and you have not said it, and this is the ninth morning."

Adrian did not answer.

"I am not going to ask you in front of them," Kest said. "I am going to say the arithmetic out loud once and then never again. Nine mornings from the forty-fourth is the fifty-third. It was the fifty-third yesterday. The lane is empty this morning, and there is no courier, and there is nothing on the wall about a sitting, and no page of yours has come back with a day on it, and the room in Alder Reach has been sitting there for nine mornings with a column with your name against it and nobody in it. That is all I know and I got it from a shopkeeper."

He put the board under his other arm.

"Now the other thing, and it is the only reason I stayed."

"Adrian Vale," he said. "You've been on that bank seventy mornings and nobody in this district has given you a bed or a wage or an office and I have watched it happen and I have watched you not ask for one, and I am the keeper of every road in this basin and I can put a man on a run in the time it takes to say so, and there is a mile and a half of the eleventh with nothing on either side of it, and there is a man who goes out of that yard at about half past four every night and is not on any of my sheets, and I have known that for two years and I have never asked him why, and the reason I have never asked him is that I asked a man at a turning once, and that man told me what would happen to the next bundle, and he was right."

He turned the board over.

"I am not offering you *that*," he said. "I am not offering you his night. I am offering you the other half of it, and the other half is mine, and it is an afternoon, and it goes from half past two to about four, and it is a mile and a half of nothing with a turn and a culvert on it and a book at each end."

"What run."

"The eleventh mile, in daylight, on foot, one man, my waybill, a book at each end, and about a hundred and thirty days of it in a year and the rest of it weather." He held the board up. "It is not a bed and it is not a wage and it is not an office, and I am not offering you any of those three, and I want you to hear the three of them out loud so that you cannot say afterwards that I offered you one. It is a man on a road saying what is on the road. It pays in food and it pays at the end of a week and it ends when he says it ends, which is the only end anybody in this basin has ever been able to offer anybody and I have been keeping that about nineteen years for somebody."

---

Adrian took the folded page out of the inside pocket, on the side away from the wire, and unfolded it, and read four lines in a hand that was not the hand he writes figures in, in a voice about as level as the wall, and then folded it up again and put it away, and about nine people heard it, and it is the only thing in this district that anybody has ever been asked to hold and is on no page in it.

The four lines were the boy's request, written out in Adrian's own hand on the forty-seventh morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, in exchange for a promise: *next time somebody comes down that lane with a page in their hand, stand in this yard and say nothing at all.* It was the only instruction anybody in that district had ever handed him and it came from a boy of seventeen, and the boy was standing about nine feet away with the sheet under his arm and had not looked at him once while it was being read.

He took the length of fencing wire out of the other pocket.

He looked at it for the length of a breath, the way a man looks at a thing he has decided about twice, and then he put it back in the same pocket, and there is a gap in a fence about four miles from that yard that is about the length of his two hands and he has not been to it and did not go to it this morning, and the boy did not write it on the sheet, and it was not entered, and nothing was struck.

---

The books were shut at six. The second table was bare and the stone was on it with the hollow an inch deep in the middle of it and nothing at either end, and the rail was on the near side with nothing on it, and the lot book was on the boards at the top of the bank with five lines on it and a margin, and a column with one name in it, and four lines in a clerk's hand at the bottom of a page that a boy argued for in a yard and she was talked into and has not regretted.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank eighty-four mornings and moved this morning from eighty-three. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for thirty-four mornings with nobody's thanks on it and a rule in that yard, made by a man of fifty-six in a yard, that nobody is to thank anybody. The lane count is twenty-one and did not move this morning, and it moved on the forty-ninth morning at about three when a man with a cart came down that hill and stopped four feet below the top of it for the first time in nine years, and a boy of seventeen got to the figure before a clerk of fifteen years did, and the man was right and said so out loud and went back up the lane.

"A rail is not a payment," the clerk said, at about ten to six, to nobody, with the book shut in front of her. "A rule is not a payment. A form is not a payment. A name in a book is not a payment. A figure in a yard is not a payment. A margin is not a payment. I have been asked to enter all six of those things in a fortnight and there is no seventh, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five, and the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and it is not paid, and it is not going to be paid on any of the mornings I have left."

She put her hand flat on the cover.

"And there is a page in Alder Reach with forty coppers on the back of it and a name on the front of it that is his, and a man of forty-three who has offered him a mile of road in daylight and a book at each end of it, and a man of about sixty-four who has been out on that same road every night for four hundred and nine nights and has been entered on a page with nothing ruled on it and did not ask to be, and a woman with a scale who read four lines out loud this afternoon and is on no page at all, and a woman of fifty-eight who gave a name in that yard at about ten to six and has gone up a lane by herself to tell the person it belonged to, and a man of about thirty-two at the end of a table with a figure he cannot pay and a rule in that yard that nobody is to thank him for it. None of those is a payment. I have been a clerk for fifteen years and I have never wanted a place less than tonight."

And a man of thirty-eight has been in that yard seventy mornings and has not been given a bed or a wage or an office and was not asked for any of the three and did not ask for any of the three, and there is a length of fencing wire in the pocket of his coat that he took out this morning and looked at for the length of a breath and put back, and the bid is open at four hundred and eighteen days and was not run, and the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven.
