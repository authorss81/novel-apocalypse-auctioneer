# Chapter 778: The Sixty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Boy Of Seventeen Worked Out Whose Row The Word Was In And Was Asked To Have It Struck Out And Said No

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven, and it is the fourth figure in about four hundred mornings that anybody in this district has had to look at twice. Five hundred and seventeen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-six, and the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-two days, and nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and six days, and six hundred and sixty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty-six days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and eighty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and fifty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-nine days back. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-five days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty-five marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and those two figures have moved together every morning this book has run. The count of counted months is six and stands. The number of documents this district does not own is four and stands. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four, and stands. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and did not move. The count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-two mornings and moved this morning from ninety-one. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-two mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-two, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move, and a boy ruled a box on the back of his own sheet this afternoon and it did not move, and a box on the back of a sheet is not a thing four people made. The lane count is twenty-one and has not been anything else since a cart stopped four feet below the top of that bank.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and third of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and seventeenth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and sixteen of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and a man of forty-three was not, and had gone up that lane at about four with a waybill board under his arm and a boy of seventeen about nine feet behind him carrying nothing at all.

---

He came down off the boards at about ten with the sheet open and did not ask anybody for anything, which is what he did on the forty-seventh morning when he asked, and which is what he did on the fiftieth morning when he asked the man of fifty-six for the thing the man had asked to have asked, and both of those had gone differently.

"I have worked out whose row it is," he said.

---

Adrian was about nine feet off the near end of the boards and he had a length of fencing wire in his hand and he had forgotten that he had it in his hand for long enough for the boy to look up and see it, which was the first time in eleven mornings.

"Say the row."

"I am not going to say the row, I am going to say how I got it and then the yard can have it."

"Go on."

"Six mornings." The boy turned the sheet round. "On the fifty-fifth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month I stood on these boards and said seventy-six and told the yard that my method had a hole in it, and the hole was six mornings I was in this yard and not on these boards. I have had all six in my head since and I have not written them anywhere because where do I write them."

"You write them on the back."

"That is the other thing and I will come to it." He put one finger down on the sheet, not on any particular row, and moved it down about nine inches. "I have got three columns that anybody can check. What was said. The figure. The day it was said. I count days; I have told the yard I count days and I have never once been asked to prove it and I can produce any day on that sheet going back about four years. So this morning I took the six mornings and I went down the third column and I looked for six mornings."

---

"And you found one."

"I found one," the boy said, "and about four hundred people could have found it, and nobody did, and that is not a compliment to me, that is a fact about how little anybody looks at a third column. One row on this whole sheet has one of my six mornings in the third column."

"And the first column of that row."

"Two words," said Orren Pike. "And they are the same two words as the third line of the lot book, and you have both of them written about forty feet from where I am standing, and the row is the man at the end of the second table, and he wrote it there through me about two years ago, and I put the pencil in my own hand and he told me what to write and I did it because a blank is worse."

---

The yard did not turn round this time.

They had turned round on the forty-seventh morning, all at once, the way a yard turns when a thing has been said about somebody standing in it, and there had been about nineteen people in that yard and about eleven of them had known what a boy saying *the man* meant.

About nine people in that yard on the sixty-second morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month were simply looking at a boy of seventeen standing on some boards with a sheet, and about four of them looked at the far end of the second table, and the man at the far end of the second table did not move at all.

---

"That is my row," he said, at about four minutes past ten.

"I know it is now."

"Yes. It has been my row for about two years and you have been carrying my name about in a coat pocket in the first column of a page in a lane, and I did that, and I would like it said in this yard that I did it and not that you did, because there is a version of that where you did it to me and it is not true and it would be the easiest thing in the world for everybody to believe."

"I did the pencil," the boy said.

"You did the pencil and I did the word. We are the same instrument twice and I told you that on the forty-seventh morning and you have not stopped not believing it."

---

"I want it struck out," the man said.

---

Nine people in that yard made a noise of one sort or another and about four of them said no out loud before anybody had finished, and the man who digs loam said it twice.

"That is not what you are going to want to hear," Adrian said, from about nine feet off the near end of the boards.

"I know exactly what you are going to want to hear," the man said, "and I have had two days to get ready for you saying it and I have decided that I would rather have the awkward version. Strike it out. Leave the first column empty on my row. I have been at the end of this table for fifteen years and I have spent most of them being a line on a book and I would like about four words of paper not to be one."

---

"I don't strike anything," said the boy.

"I know you do not. That is why I am asking you and not her." He did not look up at the boards. "Here is why, and then I am going back to the end of my table. If you strike it out, that row has an empty first column, and an empty first column in a boy's page is a question about a man with no name in it, and a question does not go away, and in about four years somebody will come and find the question instead of the row. If you leave it, it is a fact about a man, and it is a fact about me, and it is a fact I put there myself about two years ago on a morning when a clerk was not looking, because a blank is worse. I have been alive on the second of those for two years without knowing it and I would like to go on being alive on it."

---

"Then I will leave it," the boy said.

"Then say it in this yard."

"Why."

"Because a thing I have asked you to keep is a favour, and a favour can be refused," said the man at the end of the second table, "and if it is a favour then in about four years it comes down to me whether you felt like it. I want it to be an instruction. An instruction has to be obeyed and an instruction can be refused and either way it has got a shape, and I have been a vague favour in about nine rooms for as long as I have been standing at the end of that table and I am tired of it."

---

The boy read it out.

He did not read his own page, which Adrian had expected him to do, and which he had half hoped he would. He stood up on the boards of the first table with the sheet under his arm and said about thirty words in a voice that shook once.

"There is a row on my page with a word in the first column where a name goes," he said. "It is not a name and it is not about anybody else in this yard. It is about the man at the end of the second table, and he wrote it there himself about two years ago through me, and I did the pencil and he did the word, and today he asked me to strike it out and I said no, and he then asked me to leave it and to say so in this yard, and I have done both of those things and neither of them is a thing four people made, and there is no page in this district that any of it is on except that one, and it is not going in."

---

Mara Quill did not enter it.

She did not ask whether she could. She wrote nothing at all and shut nothing, because the book was shut in front of her on the boards and she had been sitting four feet from it and she had not opened it once, and when the boy finished she said one sentence and it was not about the boy.

"He asked you to say it and you said it," she said, "and the column for whoever read a thing out loud has one name in it and had none, and I did not put a second one in this morning and I did not put a third one in either, and the man at the end of the second table has now been in a yard four times in twelve mornings and there is still no line for him in that book, and he knew that when he asked you to say it, and he asked you to say it anyway, and I would like the yard to notice that he is the only person in this district who says things knowing what they will not be written down."

---

Then the boy of seventeen said the other thing, and he had been holding it since about eleven o'clock and had been waiting for the yard to get through the first part.

"There are three people in this yard who have read a thing out loud in the last three weeks," he said, "and one of them is on a page."

---

Nobody said anything, because everybody could do the sum.

"I read two words off the back of a piece of paper on the forty-ninth morning and a clerk wrote them down," the boy said, "and it was me, and it was about nine words long and I have had it in my own page ever since," "She read four lines out of a corridor on the fifty-third morning and a clerk would not write her name. And he has read four figures off that wall twice a morning for five hundred and three mornings to whoever happens to be standing there, and nobody has ever offered him the column, and my clerk said on the fifty-third morning that she was frightened of not offering it to him, and she did offer it on the sixtieth morning, and he said yes and then he set a condition, and the condition is a rule about who goes in it, and nobody in this district can write that rule, and about nine people have told me this month that nobody can write it."

---

"So there is a heading with a problem in it," said the woman who keeps a scale, from her own step.

"There is a heading with a problem in it," said Orren Pike, "or there are three people in this yard who have read a thing out loud and one of them is a page, and I do not know which of those two it is and I have known for about four days and I have not said it out loud because I put him in that column and if I stand up here and say *there is a problem with the column* then in about four years a man in a room is going to say *yes, the boy of seventeen said so*."

---

Then the man of fifty-six said it, from about eleven feet off the wall, and he said it in the ordinary voice.

"Orren."

The boy turned round.

"You read four things out loud in that yard in three weeks and you are not in that column."

---

The yard heard it go quiet in a way that it had not been quiet on any morning in eleven mornings, because every one of the last eleven had been loud, and this one had a hole in it.

"The reason you are not in it," said the man of fifty-six, "is that you asked. On the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month you stood on those boards and asked her to look for a place to put a name. There was one place. She put it there. You did not read the heading, and she did not read the heading, and she told you on the fifty-third morning that she had written it when she was nineteen and had not read it properly since, and you told her it was your fault, and it was, and you have not said one word about it since."

---

"I have said it was my fault."

"You said it was your fault in about forty words on the fifty-third morning and you have not said one word about it in nine days." The man of fifty-six turned round and looked at the wall and then at the boy. "And you have never once asked to be in it. In four years. You have counted everything anybody in this yard has said out loud and you have never put your own name anywhere, and there are three people in this yard who have read a thing out loud and two of them have been refused a page and one of them is seventeen."

---

Orren Pike did not say anything for about as long as it takes to walk down a bank and back up it.

"That's not the same," he said.

"No," said the man of fifty-six. "It is not the same, and it is going to be the same in about four years, and you know it is going to be the same because you built it, and the reason you built it is that you were fifteen and a blank is worse, and I did the same thing with a line in a book when I was about twenty-two, and we are the same instrument twice, and I have said that to you once and you did not hear it, so I am going to say it again and then I am going to read four figures off this wall and go home."

---

At about four o'clock the boy of seventeen went up that lane as far as the wall and stood about nine feet off it in the place where the turner stands, and there was nothing on it except one line in a clerk's hand from the sixtieth morning that had a man's whole condition in it, and he read it, and he stood there a while, and then he came back down.

He got the sheet out on the boards of the first table, at about twenty past four, and turned it over, and on the back of it, where he had ruled a box in pencil on the fifty-fourth morning for mornings a man was present, and where he had ruled a second box about six mornings ago for mornings he had been standing near something and could not see the boards, he ruled a third one.

He ruled it long. It took up the rest of the width.

Then he wrote in it, and Adrian read it upside down from about nine feet, and it was not a figure and it was not a name.

> *Mornings on which a thing was said out loud in this yard and was not entered anywhere. Not what was said. Not who said it. Not the figure. The mornings.*

He copied four mornings into it out of the box he ruled on the fifty-fourth morning and left a fifth space empty and ruled the space anyway, and there was nothing else in it and there was not going to be anything else in it this afternoon.

---

"That is not a column," Adrian said.

"It is not a column and it is not a page and it is not in that book and it cannot be entered in that book, and the rule I argued for on the forty-fourth morning forbids anybody entering it anywhere, and I wrote that rule and I argued for it and I would write it again."

"Then what is it for."

"It is for me," said the boy of seventeen. "Because a man stood at the end of a table about ten minutes ago and told me that in about four years there is going to be a reason given for keeping the next person out of a thing, and the reason will be reasonable, and a rule written after a refusal is a rule about that person. And there is no rule yet, and there is no column, and there is one of me, and I have got the start of a page that says *this is what happened and it was not entered*, and that is the only instrument in this district that anybody cannot use to keep anybody out, because it does not have anybody's name on it."

He put the pencil down on the boards.

"And I am not going to read that out in a yard," he said, "and I am not going to hand it to her, and if she asks me for it I will tell her about the rule and she will have to agree with me, and that is the first time in my life I have had a reason for not giving somebody a thing that is stronger than the reason I would have given her yesterday."
