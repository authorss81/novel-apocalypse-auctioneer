# Chapter 777: The Sixty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Keeper Of Every Road In This Basin Would Not Accept A Concession Until He Was Told What Followed From It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it is the first thing anybody in this yard reads before anything else. Five hundred and sixteen days is the age of that figure, and the two of them are entered by a clerk on two lines every morning in the order they were ruled.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-five. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety-one days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and five days. Six hundred and sixty-six days separate the second of January and this morning, and a body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-four days past a printing it did not make.

Four hundred and twenty-five days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and eighty days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and fifty-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-eight days back and no night has been named.

Three hundred and thirty-four marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and twenty marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and did not move. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and did not move either. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and has not moved all fortnight. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and is where it was on the first morning of this book. The count of the five things this district does not have is five and is not paid, and the fifth of those five lines is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and there is a blanket on a wall at a culvert in this basin that nobody has ever been able to say who left there.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety-one mornings and moved this morning from ninety. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty-one mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty-one, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered, and this morning it went in at a quarter past nine, which is nine hours earlier than it has ever gone in, and the man who keeps a tally entered that he had said it earlier and entered no reason. The count of things this district has made is fourteen, and did not move, and did not move this morning either although a road keeper wrote in a book at the bottom of a bank, which is a thing a person did on a road. The lane count is twenty-one, and did not move, and nothing has come down that hill with a page in his hand for twelve mornings.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and second of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and sixteenth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and fifteen of them, and a man of about forty-three who keeps every road in this basin and who has known about those nights for two years stood at the top of eleven feet at about ten and told about nine people in that yard that he was not coming down there until somebody asked him something, and nobody asked him anything for about five hours.

---

"You told me on the forty-eighth morning that the failure was not in the room," Adrian said, at about eleven, on the boards, "and for nine mornings I have been saying that you were wrong about the room and I was wrong about the cause, and I was wrong about the cause, and I am going to say it in a yard because you asked me for it in a yard."

Tarin Kest put the waybill board down flat on the boards of the first table.

"Do not," he said.

"It is the truth and you have been asking me for the truth for nine mornings."

"It is the truth and I did not come down that hill for it." He did not pick the board up. "A concession given away in a yard at eleven in the morning is a thing a person feels good about, and you have been feeling good about this yard for about eleven days and about four of the people in it have gone home worse off than they came, and I would like you to work out which of those four you are before you say anything else to me."

"Tell me what follows from it."

---

"It follows that a thing is lost on the eleventh mile because there is nothing in this basin that can find it," Adrian said. "Not because the road is bad and not because nobody was watching. Because a mile and a half with a turn and a culvert on it and nothing on either side is a place where a fact has no place to stand, and you have been walking about nine hundred yards of nothing for nine years and calling it a road and it is not a road, it is a gap, and I have been arguing with you about a room for nine mornings instead of agreeing with you about the gap."

"Aye."

"And it follows that the boy's page is not the answer," Adrian said, "because a rule he argued a woman into on the forty-fourth morning forbids entering it anywhere, and that rule is right, and I voted for it, and it is the reason the only instrument in this district that could have found anything on that mile was never allowed to be used."

---

"It follows that you have thought about this on the way down that hill and come down here with it ready," said Tarin Kest. "Go on. You have not finished."

"There is a waybill," Adrian said.

---

Kest did not move. He put the board down, picked it up again, and put it down the other way.

"There is," he said.

"You told a man of about twenty-nine's story down a road on the forty-eighth morning and then wrote him a carrier's entry yourself and he did not ask you to. A road, a direction, an hour, and a fact about a wheel. You said you had been the only person in this basin to have ever written one, and that you had learned it from a man at a turning who did it wrong once and got nine families told a thing that was not so."

"That is all true and I have not had it said back to me in nine years."

"That is the only instrument in this basin that can put a fact about a road into a book without putting a man's name at the top of it. And it is yours, and it is not a public record and it is not a district instrument and it does not have to be either, and nobody in this district has thought of it in nine years because all of us have been standing in the yard arguing about a wall and a book and a boy."

---

Kest picked the board up and held it under his arm and said nothing for long enough that the whole yard looked at each other.

"You are asking me to put a boy's page in my book," he said.

"No. I am asking whether my clerk's rule stops you. And it does not, and I have read it about nine times and it is about a paper a person is carrying. Your paper is not his paper and it is not anybody's paper in this district, and about nine lines of it are addressed to him and about four of them matter and you have understood the four."

"I have understood the four," Tarin Kest said, "and I would not put a boy's page in a road keeper's book if he carried it in his hand and offered it, and he did not offer, and I am telling you that now so that nobody in this yard spends the next four mornings persuading me."

"Then what."

"Then I will put my own entries in my own book and my own entries go where my book goes, and my book is read by about two hundred men and by about forty households, and it is not a public record and nobody in it is a resident of anywhere." He shifted the board. "And I will say one thing about it and then I will not say it again. A thing written in a road keeper's book about a mile of road with nothing on it is the only kind of writing that has ever been in the right place at the wrong time. I have been putting them in the right place for nineteen years and about nine hundred of them have arrived about a day late."

---

"Why have you never asked him," Adrian said.

"Who."

"The man on the stones."

Kest looked down the eleven feet of bank at the low wall and the two stones at the bottom of it, and there was nobody there yet because it was not half past three and it was not two o'clock either.

"No," he said.

"You said on the fiftieth morning that you had known for two years and had never asked, and you said the reason was that you asked a man at a turning once."

"I did say that and it is half the reason and I have been waiting nine mornings for somebody to notice that it is half." He turned the board over in his hands. "The other half is that a man who keeps two hundred men's roads is a man whose whole trade is knowing where not to be asked, and I found that out about forty years before I found out anything else, and I have been very comfortable in it for nineteen years, and a man of about thirty-eight has come down that hill and made this yard into a place where about nine people have become findable since the thirty-ninth morning and I have watched it happen and I have not once walked down that bank, and it has not been tact. It has been that I am the man in this yard who knows what finding somebody costs."

---

"You are also the man in this yard who can put a fact about a road in a book without putting a name on it," Adrian said, "and if you do not come down that bank this afternoon then this district has found the instrument and lost the man in the same fortnight, and I would like to know which of those two things you can live with."

"I did not say I would not come down."

"You said at about ten that you would not come down until somebody asked you something."

"Aye," said Tarin Kest. "Well. You have now been at me for thirteen mornings running and not one of them was that."

---

At about half past three he came down the eleven feet on foot, with the waybill board under his arm, and nobody at the top of that bank came down after him, and two of them were the man who digs loam and the clerk of the district, and both of them had been asked and neither of them had asked.

The man of about sixty-four had his coat folded on the stones and his hands on his knees and he had been there since about half past three and he had watched a man of forty-three come down a bank he had never been down in two years.

"You have been in that yard nine days," the man on the stones said, "and you have not been down here once."

"I have not."

"You know about me."

"I have known about you for two years," said Tarin Kest, "and I did not learn it from anybody in that yard, and it is the only thing I have ever done in this basin that I have not been paid for and it is the only thing I have never told anybody about, and I am going to ask you now and I would like to be told no."

---

"You have been in that yard nine days and you have talked to every person in it," the man on the stones said, "and there is nobody in that yard I would rather not be asked by and you are one of them and I am telling you that before you ask me, so that if you ask me then you know."

"All right."

"What is the run."

---

He had answered that question once before, in a yard, to a man who wanted something, and about nine people had been in that yard, and it had taken him about as long as it takes a clerk to read a line back twice, and had cost him the only thing he had, which was that nothing he said could be used.

This time he took longer.

"There is a turn and a culvert," he said, "and then about two miles of nothing and then a building, and I go out at about half past four and I come back at first light, and I have told you all that and it is on a page in a clerk's book that I did not ask to be on and everybody in that yard now knows it except me."

"That is not what I asked."

---

"No," said the man of about sixty-four. "It is not."

He put his hands on his knees again and looked through the gap in the hedge.

"There is a lamp in a window," he said. "Not a fire. A lamp, and it is lit at about half past four and it is the same every night, and I have never seen it out. There is a cart that comes out of a door about an hour later and it is loaded from inside and it takes about four people to load it and there are between four and nine people in that doorway and none of them has ever come out to me, not once in nine years, and I stand about two hundred yards off in a hedge because I am not going to be a man standing in a road at half past five in the morning."

"And the blanket," said Tarin Kest.

---

The old man turned his head about four inches.

"You knew about that."

"In the winter. On the eighth mile, at the culvert, about eleven weeks back. A carrier came through with a report that somebody had left a blanket folded on the wall at the culvert and that it was still there in the morning, and nobody came back for it, and there was no name on it because there is no name on a blanket." Kest's voice did not change at all. "I have that in my book. I have never connected it to anything. I have never once in eleven weeks connected it to you."

"You knew about a blanket on a wall and you did not think about the man on the stones."

"I did not think about you," said Tarin Kest, "because thinking about you would have meant asking, and asking would have meant being responsible, and there are about two hundred men in this basin who depend on me for knowing where not to be responsible. That is the whole of it and it is about nine years old and it has not cost me anything until this afternoon and it is now costing me the whole of a morning and I would like it noted that I noticed the exact moment it started costing."

---

"Did you take the blanket," he said.

"No."

"Did you leave it."

"No." The man of about sixty-four looked at his hands. "It was folded the way you fold a thing you intend somebody to have. It was folded and it was put where a man coming out at half past four in the morning would find it if he looked, and I have looked at that culvert every night for about eleven weeks and it has been there every night, and it has been taken away on some nights and put back on others by somebody who has never once been there when I am there."

---

Kest got the book out from under his arm and opened it on the flat of the waybill board, on his knees, at the bottom of a bank, and wrote in it for about two minutes with the light going.

"A road," he said, half to himself. "A direction. An hour. A lamp in a window at half past four, a cart at about half past five, between four and nine people in a doorway, and a blanket folded at a culvert on the eighth mile by somebody who has never been there at half past four."

He read it back to himself and did not read it to the yard.

"And the boy," he said. "Come down here."

---

The boy of seventeen came down the eleven feet, which he had done four times in eleven days and had never once done without asking.

"You are one end of a book," said Tarin Kest. "Not a writer. An end. I write it and you know it is there, and if I stop writing on that stretch for two weeks in a row then somebody tells you and you come and find out why, and you do not ask me why at the time, because at the time I have a reason and the reason is usually that somebody is standing where I should not be."

"That's a page," the boy said.

"It is not a page. It is a book with a man in it and about nine names in it who do different work. Your paper is ruled out of every book in this basin by a rule a clerk wrote on the forty-fourth morning and I read four lines of it in a yard the afternoon I got here and it is correct and I would not have written it and I would not undo it."

"Then why me."

"Because you counted me at the top of that bank on the forty-ninth morning before she could write it," said Tarin Kest, "and because I have watched you not enter a thing you were told to enter, and because a road keeper who has been alone with that mile for nine years has just found out there is a boy who counts things that stop."

---

The boy stood at the bottom of that bank in the last of the light for a while and did not say anything about the last part of that and that was the correct thing to do.

Then he said: "I want to write down that he has not been asked."

"You may write down that he has not been asked. You may not write down that he said anything else."

"He has said four things."

"He has said four things about a hedge and a window and a cart and a blanket, and every one of them is true, and if you write down that he said four things then in about four years a man in a room will ask him what he saw and he will not be able to tell you that he was not asked."

---

"I want to write down that he has not been asked," the boy said, "and that he said nothing else, and that is two lines and one of them is a blank and you have told me not to fill it, and that is the first blank anybody has ever let me keep in anything."

---

Then the man of about sixty-four said the thing, and he said it to both of them, and neither of them had asked him for it.

"I want to say one thing and then I am going to go up the lane," he said, "and I have thought about it for about two hours and two men on a bank and a boy standing there, and I want to say it while the light is on it."

"Say it."

"I did not say no." He stood up off the stones and picked his coat up and did not put it on. "That man at the end of the second table said no to something in a yard about nine mornings ago and he put it in a book and it is the only thing about him that anybody in this basin can use. I have got nothing. No line, no page, no margin, no column, nothing, and I have had nine years to arrange that and I arranged it very well."

"I know," said Tarin Kest quietly.

"So: no. And I am not going to stop you writing about me, because you will both go on writing about me this afternoon and tomorrow and for about four years, and there is no instrument in this district that will stop either of you and there is not one in the basin." He got his coat over his arm. "And because I am not going to be able to say *you found me, I did not want to be found* when it happens, because you did find me and I did not want to be found and those two things are both true and only one of them is going to be in the book."

He looked at the boy.

"So I am asking you, once, out loud, in front of the two of you: write down the reason. Not the four things. The reason. Why two men came down a bank at about half past three and asked a man on some stones a question he had already known the answer to."

---

Tarin Kest stood there with the book shut against his chest and did not answer for a while.

"There is not one," he said. "I have been honest with you in this yard every morning I have stood in it and I am going to be honest with you at the bottom of a bank as well. I came down because a man of thirty-eight stood at the top of that wall this morning and told me what follows from a concession, and because he told me I was right about the cause, and because I have been right about the cause for nine years and being right about it has cost me nothing and about four people, and I did not want to go on being the kind of man who is right about things at no charge to anybody but himself."

The man of about sixty-four thought about that until the light had gone off the stones and come back on them.

"That is a reason," he said. "Put that down and leave out the part where you did not want to."

"I cannot leave out the part where I did not want to. It is the reason."

"Then put down both," said the man of about sixty-four. "Both halves or neither. A half a reason is worse than none, because a man in a room will read a half one as a whole one and go looking for the other half in me."

He got his coat over his arm and stood there on the stones with the light going off them.

"I have been on this wall nine years and about two hours ago I told two men what is in a window I can see from a hedge," he said. "I did that on my own. Nobody asked me. I am saying so in front of about nine people standing up that bank, and every one of you can be asked about it, and there is not one thing in this basin that can be asked of me and refused."

---

At about four o'clock a man of fifty-six said four figures off a wall at the top of that bank in the ordinary voice and got all four of them, and the yard heard him, and then he did a thing he has not done in nine years, which was to stand still afterwards and say one more sentence to nobody in particular.

"You have all been in this yard eleven days," he said, "and you have finally got reasons. Not one of you has anywhere to put them. I have read four numbers off this wall twice a morning for nine years because a wall is the only place in this basin that will keep a figure for as long as anybody wants it without a person's name going on it, and that is all it is good for, and none of you lot has ever once asked me what it is good for."

He picked up his coat.

"Five hundred and two," he said, and went home, and about half the yard said one number each about what they thought he had meant, and four of them had it wrong.
