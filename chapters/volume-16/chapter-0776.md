# Chapter 776: The Sixtieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Clerk Of That District Did Not Enter Any Of It And Then Went Down The Bank And Asked A Question She Had Been Frightened Of For Four Days

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and it is not going to be explained this morning and nobody in this yard has asked. Five hundred and fifteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries six hundred and seventy-four. The train on that siding has stood nine hundred and ninety days. Nobody has entered anything for seven hundred and four days. Six hundred and sixty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and twenty-four days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run and nothing has been proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Three hundred and seventy-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and eighty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood, and it stands, and it was made to protect people in this yard and it has cost a boy of seventeen the use of his instrument on the only stretch of road in this basin where any of it would have mattered, and he did not withdraw it and it is not withdrawn this morning.

Four hundred and fifty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and fifty-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and ninety-three days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and thirty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and nineteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and it has not moved since a clerk of nineteen counted one off a wall in about ten minutes in front of about nineteen people. The number of documents this district does not own is four, and it did not move yesterday either, and a page is still coming up that lane. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and has not moved. The number of conditions with no end on it is four and has not moved and the fifth of those five lines is one of them, and the count of the five things this district does not have is five, and the fifth of them is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and it is not paid.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank ninety mornings and moved this morning from eighty-nine. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for forty mornings and nobody is to thank the man who fitted it and the man who fitted it has not been in that yard since the fifty-first morning, which nobody has established to be on purpose. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is sixty, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and did not move and does not move on a thing one person did, and a man of sixty-one has now been in that yard two afternoons. The lane count is twenty-one and has not been anything else for eleven mornings.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the five hundred and first of those mornings, and he said the fourth of them out loud this morning before anybody else in that yard had got to the top of the bank, which is about four minutes earlier than he has ever said it.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on his four hundred and fifteenth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and fourteen of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him again and had said good morning out loud, which the man of about sixty-four has not mentioned to anybody and has not been asked about.

---

They went up the lane at about half past eight because there was nowhere else in that yard that a clerk and a man could stand for twenty minutes without the whole of it being worked out inside a quarter of an hour.

"Say it again what you told me yesterday," Mara said. "The thing he said he wrote. In your own words, not his."

Adrian walked about nine hundred yards of it before he started.

"It was an attempt to stop anything in this basin being owned," he said. "Four thousand rooms. A bus, a lamp, a ferry, a piece of road, a bell. He wrote it so none of them could be held by a market or a ward or him. And then he could not find two people to stand in the room and say yes, so he put himself in the gap, and eleven weeks later the rights went over with nothing holding them, and that is what everybody calls the Long Fracture."

"And?"

"And he has been the only thing holding it since, and he told me that at the foot of a low wall in front of a man who was never asked anything in his life."

---

Mara Quill stopped walking.

"You are not going to enter any of it," she said.

"No."

"You entered two figures out of two other men's mouths. A man who cannot pay said forty coppers in this yard on the fifty-first morning and a boy of seventeen said seventy yesterday afternoon, and neither of them said one word to a man of sixty-one."

"Yes."

"That was in a yard at the top of a bank in front of about nineteen people." She started walking again. "He said the rest of it at the foot of a wall with nine people nine feet above him and one man four feet beside him who was not asked and could have walked away. If I enter that then this district has a record of a man's own account of the largest thing that has happened in this basin since the Fracture, and there are about nine people who heard it, and one of them is seventeen, and in about four years somebody in a room is going to ask that boy what a man of sixty-one said at the bottom of a bank, and there is not a line about that in anything I have ever kept."

"What do we do with it."

"We use it," said Mara Quill. "That is all we are permitted to do with it and I have thought about that for the night and a half I have had and it is the whole of what there is."

---

"That is the same sentence you have been saying since the fortieth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month."

"It is not," she said, "and the difference is the only thing I have got and I would like it said out loud in this lane before we get back down that hill. Since the fortieth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month I have been saying that a district cannot answer a page. What I have arrived at on that lane this morning is that a district can hold a thing it is not permitted to write down, and that is not the same thing at all, and it is the first time since the first morning of this book that this yard has produced anything that is not a figure."

Adrian let about nine yards go by.

"Say the other half."

"The other half is that a thing you did to somebody is not a thing about you." She said it fast and she said it exactly the way she says figures. "You have been treating it as a thing about you for seventeen years. It is the reason you cannot put the lot down and it is the reason you came down this hill, and I have watched you do it to four people in a fortnight, and the man who reads four figures off a wall would tell you the same thing about a number if anybody ever let him finish a sentence."

---

"And you did not stop me standing in front of him yesterday."

"No."

"Why not."

"Because you were on the stones." She did not look at him. "I said do not stand between him and that wall and you did not, and I have had a night and a half to work out whether that was obedience or whether you had nothing to offer him, and it was obedience, and I would like it noticed that it took you two whole days to do the one thing I asked for before lunch."

---

"What did it buy me," Adrian said, at the top of the lane, where there was nothing to see.

"It bought you a job."

"Two fresh independent witnesses who understood the terms and were not given them by anybody. That is a way to end a clause with no end on it. That is the thing he said there is one way to do and I have said it out loud in a yard on this hill this morning which is a yard of about two people and a hedge."

"It bought you a job," said Mara Quill, "and it did not buy you the two people, and you have been in that yard twenty-six mornings and the count of things this district has made is fourteen and it has not moved once and it did not move the day before yesterday when nine names were said out loud, and anybody up that hill can tell you the two people you have got and it is the boy and me, and I have never witnessed a term in my life and he has never witnessed anything at all, and the man of fifty-six has been reading four figures out loud twice a morning for five hundred and one mornings to nobody and nobody has ever offered him anything for it."

---

They went back down the bank at about ten and the woman who keeps a scale was standing on her own step with a weight on the door, waiting, which she does.

"You've been up that lane," the woman said. "I heard you go up it and I have been standing here since about half past nine working out whether I was going to say this and I have decided that I am, and I have decided it because of what I read out loud in this yard two mornings ago and what was done about it afterwards."

"What was done about it."

"Nothing." She came out from behind the counter, which she does not do while a customer is in, and there was no customer. "You read four lines seven mornings ago and you got all four and about nineteen people heard you, and the clerk would not put your name in the column for whoever read a thing out loud, and the boy stood on those boards and said the heading does not say whoever was asked, and she said she had known it since a quarter to eight, and then nothing happened. And you lot have all gone on since, and one of you has been to a great deal of trouble about a column since, and it has not been my column."

---

"What is your column," Adrian said.

"It isn't a column," said the woman who keeps a scale. "It is a decision. She decided it seven mornings ago and she has been carrying it about since like a plate of hot food, and you have all been busy, and I have been weighing about nineteen people's fruit in a shop with three pages on my own counter that I have never been asked about."

"I know."

"You know about the three pages. You have never once asked whether I wanted my name in a book." She put her hands flat on her own counter. "Now. You have been asking each other for about eleven days what instrument this district has for asking a person something, and there is a man at the foot of that bank who told you the answer yesterday and it is a yard. So use it. And the first thing you use it for is not nineteen kitchens and not nine coppers and not a clause nine miles up a hill. It is me."

---

"The man who reads the wall," Adrian said.

"The man who reads the wall." The woman picked her weight up off the door and set it down again, which is a thing shopkeepers do when they have decided to keep talking. "I have said out loud in that yard more than anybody in it this month and there is a column for whoever read a thing out loud, and it has one name in it, and that name is in it because a boy of seventeen stood on some boards and asked for a place to put a name, and I am not in it and neither is the boy and neither is the man who says four numbers twice a morning to whoever is standing there."

"She said on the fifty-third morning that she was frightened of not offering it to him."

"I heard her say it," said the woman who keeps a scale. "I heard her say it from about nine feet away and I have thought about nothing else since, because she is right and everybody in that yard was right, and the two of those together mean that there is a decision to be made and every one of you has been pretending there is not."

---

Mara Quill was standing about nine feet off the end of the first table and had been standing there for all of it.

"How long have you been there," Adrian said to her.

"Since about the *woman who keeps a scale*," she said.

---

The man of fifty-six was at the near end of the boards at about one with the boy of seventeen and a length of fencing wire he was using to explain something about the fourth column, and he had no idea any of them were coming and looked up when about nineteen people did not go on doing what they had been doing.

Mara Quill came the last four feet and did not touch the book, which is on the boards at the top of that bank and not in her hands this morning because she had deliberately left it there.

"I am going to ask you a question in this yard," she said, "and it is one I have been frightened of for four days, and I am going to say that out loud first because I have watched two people in this yard do that this month and it worked and I am copying both of them."

"I would expect nothing else."

"The column in that book is for the name of whoever read a thing out loud." She put her hands where he could see them, and Adrian understood that she had learned that off him and did not like having learned it off him. "You have read four figures off that wall out loud twice a morning for five hundred and one mornings. You have never been asked to fill it. I am asking you now. Will you say yes."

---

The man of fifty-six looked at the wall for a while.

"What happens to the next one," he said.

"I do not know," said Mara Quill.

"Then you'll not have me yet."

---

"Say what you mean."

"I mean that if the column is for whoever read a thing out loud then somebody has to decide who that is, and the day somebody decides it is a day somebody is in charge of it, and the day somebody is in charge of it is a day there is a reason." He turned round and looked at the yard. "In about four years there will be a reason given for keeping the next person out. It will be a good reason. It will be that the column is for public readings and not for a man talking to himself twice a morning, or that a person who has not been asked cannot be entered, or that the column is full. It will be reasonable and it will be made by a person with a pencil and it will be about four years from now and none of us will remember this morning."

"And you want a rule."

"I want a rule made before the reason," said the man of fifty-six. "Not the rule about the column. The rule about who. And I want it in a yard, out loud, in about nineteen people's hearing, and I want it made before anybody can say no to anybody, because the moment one person has been refused then there is a precedent and a rule written after a precedent is a rule about that person."

---

"That is a rule against the column," said Mara Quill.

"That is a rule made by the first man who goes in it, about the second," said the man of fifty-six, "and there is not one of those in this basin, and there is never going to be one that was not written by whoever went in first, and every person in this yard has spent a fortnight arriving at that by a separate road, and not one of you has written a line down."

She did not answer that for a while, and then she said: "You are not a witness."

"I am not a witness."

"You have never witnessed a term. You read four figures off a wall twice a morning to whoever is standing there and nobody has ever stood there on purpose."

"I have never witnessed a term," the man of fifty-six agreed, "and neither has the boy and neither has she, and that is not the answer to what I asked her. Will you write a rule, or will you put a name in a column with nothing in front of it."

---

"Yes," said Mara Quill.

"Then I will have you," said the man of fifty-six, "and I will not be in the column this morning, and I will not be in it until there is a rule, and you are going to have to say that in a yard as well, and I would like it read out at four o'clock so that about nine people who are not in this yard at one o'clock hear it later."

"You are asking me to enter a refusal."

"I am asking you to enter the whole of it," he said. "The refusal and the condition and the fact that I asked for the condition. In that order, in one entry, in the ordinary voice. That is what your book is for and it is the first time in five hundred and one mornings that anybody has asked it for something I could actually give."

---

She did it at about four, standing on the boards of the first table with the sun behind her and about nineteen people in the yard, and she read it out, and it was not a figure.

> *Entered on the sixtieth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, at about four, by M. Quill. A person in this district was asked in this yard at about one in the afternoon whether he would give his name to the column in the lot book for whoever read a thing out loud. He said that he would, and he set a condition before he said it, and I am entering the condition before the answer because that is the order it happened in.*
>
> *Entered that the condition is a rule about who may be entered, made out loud in this yard, in about nineteen people's hearing, before any person is refused, and not made afterwards, and that he gave as his reason that in about four years there will be a reason given for keeping the next person out, and that the reason will be reasonable, and that a rule written after a refusal is a rule about that person.*
>
> *Entered that I do not know whether that rule can be made, that nobody in this district has ever made one, that I am not entering a name in that column this morning, and that this is the third want I have entered in twelve mornings and that I have been told what happens to a clerk who enters wants against people and that there is nobody to enter this one against but me and the man who asked me for it, and he asked for it, and he asked for it in a yard and in front of about nineteen people, and I am entering that he asked for it.*

---

That night the boy of seventeen came up the lane as far as the wall and stood where the turner stands every morning and read the entry out loud, because the turner is not one of anybody's and reads whatever is on that wall, and there was one line of it on the wall that morning and it was in a clerk's hand and it had the whole of a man's condition in it, and the turner read it twice and went on up the hill without saying anything about it.

At about six, Adrian and Mara Quill stood at the top of eleven feet with the yard emptying behind them.

"Two," said Adrian.

"Two," said Mara Quill, "and neither of them has witnessed a term, and there is a rule nobody can write yet, and there is a clause nine miles up a hill with no end on it and a way of ending it that takes two of them, and I have been the clerk of this yard for fifteen years and I entered four lines this afternoon and not one of them was a figure."

"None of the five is paid."

"None of the five is paid," she said, "and it is not going to be paid this month, and the count of them is five and I entered it last, and I am going to bed."
