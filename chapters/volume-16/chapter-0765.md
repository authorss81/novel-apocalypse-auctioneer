# Chapter 765: The Forty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month A Page Came Back Down That Lane In The Hand Of A Man With A Cart And A Boy Of Seventeen Counted Him Before The Clerk Could And Wrote Down A Figure He Had Not Sent

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Five hundred and four days is the age of that figure, and it has not moved, and nobody in that yard has ever worked out what either of the two of them is for. The board carries six hundred and sixty-three, the train on that siding has stood nine hundred and seventy-nine days, nobody has entered anything for six hundred and ninety-three days, and six hundred and fifty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and thirteen days is how long the bid has been open and it was not run. Three hundred and sixty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Three hundred and seventy-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Four hundred and forty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is five hundred and forty-six days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is three hundred and eighty-two days past a printing it did not make.

Three hundred and twenty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are three hundred and eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. The count of counted months is six and it did not move, and it did not move because nobody counted one, and a month is not a thing that arrives on a cart. The number of documents this district does not own is four and it did not move, and there is a page in Alder Reach that this district does not own, and that is five of something if anybody ever decided that it counted, and nobody has. The number of places in this district where those three lines can be read is four and it did not move, and the number of conditions with no end on it is four and it did not move, and a fifth place would be a shop counter and a man at the end of a table said no to it on the forty-second morning and put his no in the book, and he was right.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank seventy-nine mornings and moved this morning from seventy-eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for twenty-nine mornings. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is forty-nine, on the word of the man who keeps a tally, said before it was entered. The count of things this district has made is fourteen and it did not move, and a figure came back down a lane this morning and it is not a fifteenth, and a figure is not a thing anybody made.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the four hundred and ninetieth of those mornings.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, on his four hundred and fourth night of that run, having slept on four hundred and three of them, and there is a page in a clerk's own book with one line on it about him and no line in the lot book, and he has not asked to see it and nobody has offered to show him.

---

Adrian was standing on the boards at about half past two with his hands at his sides and nothing in them, and Tarin Kest was standing about nine feet away with his waybill board under his arm, and about nine other people were in that yard, and not one of them had asked him why he was standing there.

The boy had told him on the forty-seventh morning: next time somebody comes down that lane with a page in their hand, stand in the yard and say nothing at all.

He had spent the morning finding out that he did not know whether a promise about a stranger covered a man he had been standing next to at the foot of a bank on the fortieth morning, and had decided that it did, and had not told anybody that he had decided, and had come up that hill at half past one and had been standing on those boards for an hour.

"You've not moved," Kest said.

"I've not moved."

"Your hands look wrong."

"They are the only part of me I have not got a use for this morning," Adrian said, "and that is a joke, and I would rather it wasn't."

---

The cart came up that bank at about three and it stopped.

It stopped about four feet below the top of the bank, which is not the top of it, and the near wheel was still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got down off it on the off side and came the last four feet on foot, and he had a sheet of paper in his hand.

He had never once done that in nine years.

Orren was on the boards. Adrian watched him get to it, and he was not a slow boy and he was not a frightened one, and what he did next took him about a second and a half and it was the fastest thing Adrian had seen him do since he came down that hill.

"Twenty-one," the boy said.

---

Mara Quill's pencil was in her hand about four inches off the page.

"I haven't —" she began.

"Twenty-one," Orren said. "A man has come down that lane with a page in his hand and he has stopped at the top of the bank and he is not one of us, and the count was twenty at a quarter past eleven yesterday and it is twenty-one now, and I am saying it before you write it because that is the rule and you wrote the rule and I am not going to be the reason it gets done late."

She looked at him for a while, and then she wrote it, and entered the reason, and entered that no name goes beside it, and entered that a stranger is not a resident of this district.

Then she put the pencil down and looked at the man on the bank, and Adrian watched her arrive at it before he did, and a good deal afterwards, which was long enough to be uncomfortable.

"You're the one with the cart," she said.

"Aye."

"Nineteen mornings of mine I've entered that a man came up that bank at about three and went straight up the lane, and I have never once entered that I could not tell you one thing about you," the clerk said, "and I have written *not asked* a hundred and forty times about a man on some stones and I am now about to write *twenty-one* about a man on a bank, and I would like the yard to understand that both of those are the same sentence."

---

The man of about twenty-nine stood four feet below the top of that bank with a page in his hand and looked at about eleven people, and Adrian understood that the man was doing arithmetic and that the arithmetic was about him and not about the yard.

Then the man said one sentence, and it was not the one Adrian had spent the morning expecting, and it was over before the cart wheel had finished turning and it was the only thing he said for the rest of the day.

"On the fortieth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month you asked me to stop and I said no, and then on a road yesterday at half past three you told a stranger about me without asking me, and I want to say the no is still the no, and I want the yard to know that a man who was counted this morning did not stop because he was asked to."

---

Nobody in that yard said anything, and the reason nobody said anything was that a boy of seventeen had said something to Adrian on the fortieth morning in twenty-one words, and Adrian had said *fair* before Adrian had understood it, and about nine people had been within thirty feet of that bank on the fortieth morning, and it had been sitting in the district for nine mornings doing nothing.

*A man refusing to be on a page is a man on a page. He just doesn't know which column.*

"He told you about me."

"He did," Adrian said.

"And you told a man I'd never seen."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because you asked me to," Adrian said. "On the fortieth morning you said that when the next one came down that lane with a page in their hand, I was to tell him you were here and you said nothing. And I did that, on a road, at half past three yesterday, without asking you, and I have been standing on these boards for an hour working out whether I would do it again and I would, and I would rather you knew that than have you find it out later from a man with a waybill."

The man of about twenty-nine looked at him for a long time.

"That's the first honest thing anybody in this yard has said to me in nine years," he said, "and it's no use at all, and I am going up the lane in a minute and I am taking this down with me, and I want to say one thing about the counting first, because I have been standing here working it out and I have got it."

He held the page up a little so that the yard could see that it was a page.

"That boy made me twenty-one," he said. "Not nineteen and not a man. He made me the same thing as the page, and he did it out loud in front of you lot before she could write it, and he did it because it's the rule, and the rule is the rule, and I have had that page in my hand coming down that hill and I have been a figure for about four hundred yards and I did not know it and he was right and I am not going to stand here and tell a yard of nineteen people that a boy of seventeen is wrong about arithmetic."

He put the page down on the boards, square, a foot to the near side of the lot book, and straightened it with the side of his hand.

"That's your answer back," he said. "And I carried it because nobody else would, and I would like that written down somewhere, and I have just worked out that nowhere in this district can be written down anywhere, and I am not surprised."

He got his hand on the cart wheel and then took it off again, which was the first thing he had done with that cart all morning that was not necessary.

"And I'll say the other thing, since I have stopped and I know what it has cost me and it is not the count." He looked round the yard. "I have not stopped at the top of that bank in nine years. Not once, not for a woman with a barrow, not for a funeral, not the morning the ice was on it. And I have stopped this morning, and the count has moved, and about nine of you are pleased, and the clerk has written my name in a figure, and I have never once in nine years been a thing anybody in this district could point at. So whatever any of you lot were about to say to me, don't, because I did not stop to be a person, I stopped because a man with a list asked me to and I did not think about it for about a minute and a half, and that is the whole of the reason and it is not a good one."

He looked at the boy.

"You'll want it for your column and you can have it, because a man who stops for a minute and a half has said nine words, and a word is a word."

---

He went up the lane. The cart went with him. Nobody went after him and about four people wanted to.

The returned page was folded in three, with a tarred string round it, and there was a lump of wax on the knot the size of a farthing, and the clerk looked at it for long enough that nobody in that yard came and stood near her, and then she asked Tarin Kest to turn it over without unfolding the second fold, which is the thing she had asked a man in a yard to do on the thirty-seventh morning and which she had not asked anybody to do since.

On the back of the printed side, in a colder and smaller hand than anything that had come down that lane in nine years, there were four lines.

> *Returned, and not read out. The pieces are not marked in the corner, which is permitted and is not a fault.*
> *Figure of pay: forty coppers for one year.*
> *Term: one year, ending on the day before the first sitting following, and renewable only by fresh consent of the persons the market represents.*
> *As sent.*

Four lines, and two of them were the notice's own ninth line turned round, and the fourth was two words.

"Read the fourth one out," Adrian said.

"Read it yourself."

"I'm not reading a page in this yard," Adrian said. "I sent it and I'm the last man who should have it in his mouth."

---

Orren read it. He was seventeen and he was the only person in that yard who was not a party to it, and the clerk looked at him for about two seconds and then did not stop him, and that was a second rule gone in a week.

"*As sent*," the boy said.

And then he waited, because a boy who counts things for a living knows that a word like that does not stop.

"Forty coppers," the woman of fifty-eight said, from the back of the yard.

"Forty coppers for a year," Adrian said. "And we hold nine."

"We hold nine," she said, "and I hold nine, and they are the same nine, and there is a person attached to every one of them and I could not tell you one of those people their own name without looking, and a clerk could, and she is looking."

---

Adrian took the letter out of his own coat, which he had carried up that hill in case somebody asked, and read his own sentence to himself, standing on the boards, in one breath, the way a man reads a page he has read in a clean hand.

*If a person is required, then a person can be sent. If a figure and a term are required, then a figure and a term cannot be sent from here, because there is nothing in this district that would make either of them true on a day.*

He read it twice.

"I wrote it," he said.

"You wrote it," said the clerk.

"I meant the whole of it. That is not a figure and that is not a term. There is no figure on that letter and there is no term on that letter and a man in a room nine miles from here has taken the second half of a sentence and put a figure against it and written *as sent* on the back, and he is not dishonest, and he is not stupid, and I would have read it the same way if I had been sitting in that room, because I wrote it as a district."

"You wrote it as a district," the woman of fifty-eight said, "and I told you on the fortieth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month that a district is easier to be than a man, and you told me a clerk had said the same thing to you, and then you went up that bank and read out loud a page with *we* in it seven times and *our* twice, and I have not said one word about that for four mornings, and I am saying it now, because two words in a small cold hand have just come back down a hill and they are *as sent*."

---

And then the man who digs loam came round the end of the second table and picked the lot book up off the boards and held it in one hand, which nobody in that yard had ever seen him do, and read the fifth line out loud, in a yard, in front of about nineteen people, for the first time in fifteen years.

> *A standing offer of labour, at the use of any person who requires a man with nobody, to be had at the second table at the foot of this bank at any hour, without end and without pay.*

He put it down.

"That is the fifth of the five things this district does not have," he said, "and it is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and it is the reason we cannot answer that page, and it has been on that book working exactly as it was written for fifteen years."

He looked at the returned page lying a foot away from it.

"And that," he said, "is a page that says a person can be paid forty coppers for a year at the first sitting, in full, and it says the words *as sent* on the back of a thing this district sent, and there is one figure in the whole of it and the figure is the same shape as the line I have been standing behind for nine years, and I would like somebody in this yard who is not me to say the next part out loud, because I have been next to it for nine years and I have not seen it, and I am not sure anybody else has either."

Nobody said anything for long enough that a dog that belongs to nobody went across the top of the bank and did not stop for anybody.

"The district's only unpaid man is the reason the district cannot send anybody," Adrian said.

"Aye," said the man at the end of the table. "That has been true in that book for fifteen years and nobody has ever said it in a yard, and there is a man on that page with your name on it and forty coppers on the back of it, and he is not me, and I want that in the air as well."

---

The count of the five things this district does not have is still five and it is not going to be five any more by the end of the week, and everybody in that yard knew that at about four o'clock and not one of them could have said how.

A figure had come back down the lane with two words on the back of it. A fifth line that had been working exactly as it was written for fifteen years had been read out loud in a yard for the first time and had turned out to be the same shape as the notice. And the only name in this district that was in any sense in front of that room was a name a man had given out loud on the thirty-ninth morning after the thirtieth of the eighth month, and it was in a column, and there was a figure against it, and a man at the far end of a table at the bottom of a bank had said out loud that it was not him, and he was right, and the man who put his name in the book was standing on the boards with his hands at his sides and had been standing there for an hour and a half saying nothing at all, which is the thing a boy of seventeen had asked him for, and had got.

There was no form in that district. There was no way to take nine coppers off nine people and put them against a year, and there was no way to make forty coppers true on a day, and there was no way to stop a line with no end on it except by ending it, and the one line in that yard with no end on it was the only thing in the district that could be described in the words *a man with nobody*, and the man who is that man said this afternoon that he was not the bearer, and there is a page in Alder Reach with a name on it that is the bearer, and the name belongs to a man who cannot pay it and is not to and will not.

Tarin Kest put his waybill board under his arm and said, at about four, "The eleventh mile," and Adrian said, "I know," and Kest said, "I haven't said it out loud yet," and Adrian said, "You have not," and neither of them said anything else, and that argument is not finished and is not to be finished on a morning when everybody else in the yard is thinking about a figure of forty.
