# Chapter 492: An Entry With A Figure And A Refusal Of That Figure

A clerk of nineteen years entered a figure and a refusal of that figure in one entry in the yard of Lot Seventeen, at the middle of the afternoon of the twentieth morning after the count, and read that entry out loud once in the ordinary voice to about nine people, and did not read it back from the top, and about four of those nine people said out loud that they could not tell which of the two halves of it was the finding, and the clerk said out loud that she does not know either, and the man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing in about four seconds and did not touch anything while he said it.

By ten there were about nineteen people in that yard and nine of them were still standing in it at half past four, and the light had gone round off the south end of that ground and come back along the face of that bank by then, and the boards of that second table had been in the sun since about one and had gone cold again at about four, and the four figures on that wall had not been said out loud a second time that day and a clerk entered that and entered the reason, which is that a figure a man reads off a wall every morning is not entered into a page twice in one day.

At about a quarter to eight, with that yard empty behind her, a clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered two hundred and thirty-two days as the age of that figure, and entered that the age of a figure is a figure about the figure and that the sheet has no day-count.

---

The man of about thirty-two who puts tables up came down that lane at about half past one and stood at the end of that second table and said one thing out loud, in the ordinary voice, to about nine people, and the boy of about nineteen counted what he said and got one hundred and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I said a sentence this morning with two figures in it and about four people have said since that it was the truest thing anybody has said in this yard this month, and I have been walking up and down that lane for eleven days to say it, and being true is the thing this district has least use for, and I can prove it, because I have been down here every working morning since the first of this month and I have not been thanked once and I am not going to be, and I would say it again.**"

Nobody answered him. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he would say it again, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The boy of about nineteen asked her, out loud and in front of about nine people, why the man of fifty-six had not said the four figures off that wall a second time that day when he says them twice every morning, and the clerk answered him out loud, and the boy counted what she said and got eighty-four and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Because a figure a man reads off a wall every morning is not entered into a page twice in one day, and he has not done it twice in one day in six months, and if I make an entry out of the second one then I am asking a man to say a thing a third time in a day so that a page can hold it, and I have entered that he did not, and I am not going to enter why.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a boy asked a question out loud in that yard and entered the question and the answer and the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Then the clerk of nineteen years entered the figure and the refusal of that figure, at about half past two, standing at the near end of that first table with her page in her hand and not on the boards, and about nine people heard all of it, and the boy counted what she said and got one hundred and twenty-five and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**The figure in this entry is two hundred and eighteen days. That is the number of mornings a man of fifty-six has got four figures off that wall in the ordinary voice, and he has not missed one, and about nine people in this district have said that that is a figure about a man. The refusal in this entry is mine and it is against about nine people and it is this. That is not a figure about a man. A man who has done a thing two hundred and eighteen mornings in a row is a thing a person did, and I am not going to put a count on him that could be used to say he ought to do it again.**"

About four of the nine said out loud, in about the same second, that they could not tell which of the two halves was the finding, and the boy counted what was said and got ninety-seven and read the number back to himself in a low voice, and a clerk entered that about four people said something at the same time and entered that she is not entering what they said as one thing and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The clerk then said one more thing out loud, to about nine people, and the boy counted it and got ninety-seven and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have been asked twice this week which half of that page is the finding and I am going to say out loud in this yard that I do not know, and that a page a stranger can walk up to and read with two halves in it and nothing on it saying which one is mine is a page that is going to sit there being useless to whoever comes next, and I would rather it sat there useless than have me pick one in a yard this afternoon because about nine people made a face.**"

Nobody answered her. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a clerk said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that she does not know which half is the finding and entered that she is not going to choose, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing, in about four seconds, at the end of that second table, with his left hand in its cloth and his right hand behind his back, and the boy counted it and got fifty-three and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**There is no mark on that page and there is not going to be one, and the two of you have been looking at it for about ten minutes as if it were going to arrive, and it is not going to arrive, because the person who wrote it does not know either.**"

Nobody said anything at all. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that a man said it without putting a hand on anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a scale then said one thing to about nine people, and the boy counted what she said and got sixty-nine and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have been wrong in this yard once this month and I said so out loud and it cost me six weeks of arguing and it was worth it, and I am telling about nine people this afternoon that a clerk being wrong in a page is cheaper than a clerk being right in a page by accident. Put both halves down. Leave it. Come back in ten days.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a woman said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that she is not entering ten days as a plan, because a plan is the one thing in this district with no figure under it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up that bank at about half past three and read the page standing up without being asked, in about six seconds, and said nothing at all about it, and a clerk entered that a man read a page standing up in the open and entered that he said nothing and entered no figure about what he thought of it, and entered that a man who reads that page has not been asked whether he thinks the first half is a figure or the second half is, and the record about the not asking says not asked. He was in that ditch until about ten and came up and got the four figures at the near end of that first table and said the word with them four times, and nobody asked him about the four.

The road keeper came up that lane at about twenty past one, got the four figures off that wall, and was not asked about the eleven miles. At about three a cart came up that lane with its near wheel dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine driving it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and did not stop. At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and about nine people within four feet of him were busy being certain about a page, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his hundred and eighty-second night of that run and that he has slept on a hundred and eighty-one of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

---

A hundred and forty-one days is how long that bid has been open, and it was not run on this afternoon, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Ninety-six days is how far behind the figure on that second line is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man. A hundred and one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. It is a hundred and seventy-one days since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is two hundred and seventy-four days back, it is open, nothing was pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is a hundred and ten days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard has watched any of it and nobody is going to.

Fifty marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are thirty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither of those figures moved this afternoon. The count of things this district has made is twelve, and a figure and a refusal of it in one entry is not a thirteenth. The reading of that lot did not advance this afternoon, and it stands at the fourth of the five things a document that sets a lot out has to say, and the fourth of those is a person. A clerk read one entry out loud in that yard this afternoon and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and no name went into it, and a clerk entered that she read an entry out loud in that yard and did not ask anybody for a name and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked. A way to pay a person who is not in a household is the fifth of the five things this district does not have and it has not been paid, the count of them is five, no sixth is in anything anybody has said in that yard, and six things stand in it that are not a payment and not one of the six is one.

The buckets went down that bank at about half past five, both of them empty, and came back up it full, and the woman of fifty-eight read those three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing at all, and nobody there said one word to her. After that the man of fifty-six said the four figures off that wall for the last time that day and got all four of them, and about four people standing near enough to see his face have said since that a man with a count of his own mornings in a clerk's hand stood about nine feet off a man saying he would say the truest thing in the yard again, and that neither of them looked at the other.

One entry in a clerk's book this afternoon carries a figure about a man and a clerk's refusal of that figure, and there is nothing on the page saying which of the two is the finding, and about four people said out loud that they could not tell, and the clerk who wrote it said out loud that she does not know, and a man who reads four figures off a wall every morning has now got a count of his own mornings in somebody else's hand and a refusal of it in the same line, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six and no name went into it, and a stranger can walk up to that page tomorrow and read both halves of it and be no better off than they are tonight.
