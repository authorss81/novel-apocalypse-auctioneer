# Chapter 489: The Man Who Reads Them Is Asked Nothing

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally asked a clerk of nineteen years, out loud, in the yard of Lot Seventeen on the eighteenth morning after the count, in front of about nineteen people, whether one of the four figures a man of fifty-six reads off that wall every morning could be put right by writing a newer figure beside it, and the clerk answered him out loud in the ordinary voice, and the answer was that it could be, and that four people in that yard had asked her the same question in two days, and that a thing four people have asked a clerk about in two days is a thing about four people and not about a column, and the woman of fifty-eight was at the top of that bank at the time and heard none of it, and the man of about forty-eight heard all of it and did not say anything else, and the reading of that lot did not advance.

It was the eighteenth morning after the count and a wind came and went twice before nine and the boards of that second table were cold and wet on the first of it and dry on the second, and by ten there were about nineteen people in that yard.

At about a quarter to eight a clerk of nineteen years entered that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered two hundred and thirty days as the age of that figure, and entered that the age of a figure is a figure about the figure and that the sheet has no day-count of its own.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them, a clerk entered them once, and at about four he said them again and got them again, and about nine people standing near enough to hear that have said since that he has said those four every morning since before the middle of the sixth month and that this is the two hundred and sixteenth of them.

"**The board carries three hundred and eighty-nine, the train on that siding has stood seven hundred and five days, nobody has entered anything for four hundred and nineteen, and three hundred and eighty days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

---

He asked it at about a quarter past eleven from the east end of that yard with the flat book shut under his left arm, and about nineteen people heard it, and the boy of about nineteen counted what he said and got eighty-five and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**I have kept a tally in this district for about thirty years and I have never once in that time put a figure beside an older figure on purpose, and I am not asking her to and I want nine people to know I am not asking her to. I am asking her whether a person is allowed to. That is a different question and I have not been able to answer it for about thirty years and I have never asked it out loud.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he did not ask her to do anything, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

She answered him at about ten past twelve with her page in her hand and not on the boards, and about nineteen people heard all of it, and the boy counted what she said and got one hundred and forty-eight and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**A person is allowed to and a clerk is allowed to and about four people in this yard have asked me that question in two days and not one of them asked me whether I wanted to do it. I have entered that three columns of that wall are a day out from the day they name, one of them at a time, and a clerk may enter a difference as a day, and a clerk may not enter a day out as a figure that somebody wrote on a different morning about a different thing. If somebody writes today's figure beside yesterday's on that board, then in eleven days a stranger can walk up to that page and see two figures and not know which one he is reading, and that is not a correction, that is a second column wearing the shape of a first one.**"

Nobody answered her. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a clerk said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that she is not entering that as a rule, and entered that a rule is a sentence a person can say twice and have both of them be the same sentence, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally then said one thing out loud, to about nine people, and the boy counted what he said and got sixty-two and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Then I have asked the wrong question twice in thirty years and I have found out about it on a morning, and I would have found it out anyway in about eleven days by watching two figures go past me on a wall, and I am not going to say thank you for it because you did not do it for me.**"

Nobody said anything. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that he did not thank her and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The boy of about nineteen then asked, out loud and in front of about nine people, whether the man of fifty-six was the person who would have to write the second figure if it was written, and the clerk said no out loud, and the boy counted what she said and got seventy-eight and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**No. And I want that said where nine people can hear it, because I have watched this yard for three weeks do everything it could to a man who reads four figures off a wall every morning, and the worst of it was asking him things, and nobody in this yard is going to ask him what to do about a figure on a board, and I have written that in my own page and not in mine.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a boy asked her a question out loud in that yard and entered the question and the answer and the reason, and entered that a man who reads four figures off a wall is not a person a column gets entered against, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing, in about four seconds, and the boy counted it and got thirty-two and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**Ask me the question that question is about and I will answer it today, and I have been in this yard five volumes waiting for somebody to work out what it is.**"

Nobody worked out what it is. A clerk of nineteen years entered that a man said a thing out loud in that yard and entered the thing, and entered that nobody asked him a second question, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

The man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds was at the end of that second table at about one and put his own mark in chalk on the edge of it in the ordinary way and said nothing, and there are thirty-four of them in a row going back to the ninth day of this volume, and a clerk entered that he was four feet from a man who was offered a question and did not take it up and entered the reason, and the record about the not asking says not asked. Out of that ditch at about ten came the man of about thirty-one who digs loam, and he got the four figures at the near end of that first table and said the word with them four times.

About twenty minutes past one the road keeper came up that lane, took the four figures off that wall without stopping, and was not asked about the eleven miles by anybody in that yard. The cart came up at about three with the near wheel still dragging, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm and did not stop. About half past three a man of about sixty-four was still at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that this is his hundred and eightieth night of that run, that he has slept on a hundred and seventy-nine of them, and that she is not going to set down what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

---

A hundred and thirty-nine days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Ninety-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line is a date and the date is a figure of a man. Ninety-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood since the tenth of the tenth month. A hundred and sixty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is two hundred and seventy-two days back, it is open, nothing has been pulled off it, and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is a hundred and eight days past a printing it did not make, and nobody in that yard has watched any of it.

Forty-eight marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of the twelfth month, and there are thirty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table, and neither of those figures moved today. The count of things this district has made is twelve, and a question asked out loud is not a thirteenth. The reading of that lot stands where it has stood since the second of the twelfth month, at the fourth of the five things a document that sets a lot out has to say, and the fourth of those five is a person, and it did not advance this morning. Nobody read an entry out loud in that yard today, and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six, and a clerk looked at it and did not put anything in it and did not say why. Nothing has been paid. The count of the five is still five and the fifth of the five things this district does not have is a way to pay a person who is not in a household, and a sixth has not been proposed by anybody in that yard on any day of this month, and six things stand there that are not a payment and none of the six is one.

The buckets went down that bank at about half past five, both of them empty, and came back up it full, and the woman of fifty-eight read the three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past and said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her, and about four people have said since that a woman who has come up that bank twice a day for a volume has never once been inside that yard and that nobody has ever worked out whether that is a choice. After that the man of fifty-six said the four figures off that wall for the last time that day and got all four of them, and nobody asked him anything at any point in the day and a clerk entered that she did not ask and entered the reason and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Three columns on that wall are a day out from the day they name and one of them is one of the four figures a man of fifty-six reads off it every morning, and on this morning a man who has kept a tally for about thirty years asked out loud in that yard whether a person is allowed to write a newer figure beside an older one, and a clerk of nineteen years said out loud that a person is allowed to, and that four people had asked her that in two days without once asking her whether she wanted to, and that the reason is not a column and the reason is four people, and nothing was written beside any of the three and the reading of that lot did not advance and the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was empty at about six and will be empty tomorrow.
