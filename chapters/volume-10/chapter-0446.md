# Chapter 446: Two Columns Of His Own

A boy of about nineteen got to the fourth thing at about eleven in the morning and it was this: the four figures on that wall are a day apart from each other, and the sheets in his own book are a page apart from each other, and for nine months those two things had been the same thing in his head and they had quietly stopped being the same thing.

It was the twelfth of the twelfth month and there was a hard clean sky and the wind came down that bank in gusts that lasted about as long as it takes a woman to fill a bucket. By ten there were about nineteen people in the yard of Lot Seventeen.

A clerk of nineteen years entered, at about a quarter to eight and before there was anybody else standing there, that the figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven and did not move, and entered the age of that figure as a figure about the figure, which is a hundred and ninety-four days, and entered that a figure about a figure is not a figure about a sheet.

The man of fifty-six read the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four, and said the word with each of them and got the word four times.

"**The board carries three hundred and fifty-three, the train on that siding has stood six hundred and sixty-nine days, nobody has entered anything for three hundred and eighty-three, and three hundred and forty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.**"

---

The boy wrote the four on his own sheet the way he has written them every morning since the middle of the sixth, and then he did the thing he has done every morning since the middle of the sixth, which is go back down the sheet and put the day on the left, and yesterday's four on the line above yesterday's day, and check.

They were all a day apart. Three hundred and forty-nine and then three hundred and fifty and then three hundred and fifty-one and then three hundred and fifty-two and then this morning's three hundred and fifty-three, and six hundred and sixty-five and six hundred and sixty-six and six hundred and sixty-seven and six hundred and sixty-eight and this morning's six hundred and sixty-nine, and every one of them up by one, and he had never once in nine months written down what up by one was.

He got to the third of the four and then he stopped, and he went back to the day before yesterday with his thumbnail and found the four figures he had written for the day before yesterday, and they were this morning's four figures as well, because he had written the same four twice and had put them on two sheets.

"I have got two sheets for yesterday," he said out loud, in that yard, to nobody in particular, in the ordinary voice. "I wrote the same four on both of them."

Nobody told him that. The clerk of nineteen years entered that a boy of about nineteen said a thing out loud, and entered the thing, and entered that the four figures he wrote for yesterday are the four figures that were on that wall yesterday morning and yesterday afternoon, and that two of his own sheets carry one day's four, and that a clerk is not entering a figure about his sheets and is not entering what he should have done about it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

"What did I do wrong," said the boy of about nineteen.

"You did not do anything wrong," said the clerk of nineteen years. "You wrote down what you were given, twice, and a page is a page and a day is a day and there is no figure in this district that can tell a stranger which of your two sheets is the one where you were given it, and that is not a fault of yours, it is a thing about what a page is."

---

He did not decide not to go over and ask the man of fifty-six. He noticed at about eleven that he had not decided not to, and he told the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up that, out loud, because that man had been the only one who had ever answered a question he asked in that yard.

The boy of about nineteen said out loud that he was going to go over and ask the man of fifty-six how many days the second of January was from this morning, and the man of about thirty-two who puts tables up answered him from the east wall in about four seconds, and the boy of about nineteen counted what he had said and got ninety-seven and read the number back to himself in a low voice, and about four people at the east wall heard the number.

"**You are about to ask a man of fifty-six how many days the second of January is from this morning, and he will tell you, and then you will have two numbers and you will not be able to say which of them is the one he gets off that wall every morning, and if you write his number on a sheet of your own then that sheet is a figure about his mornings, and a sheet of yours is not a column and I am not going to be the one who turns it into one.**"

The boy of about nineteen did not go over and ask him. Nobody told him not to and nobody told him to and the record about the not asking says not asked.

Then he went back to the end of that table and did the thing that is the whole of this chapter, and he did it without telling anybody he was going to do it, which is how the other four things happened.

He ruled a line down the middle of the sheet he was on.

He wrote the day in the left half, which is what he has always written, and he wrote the number of the sheet in the right half, which is a figure about his own book and about nothing else in the world. He began the right-hand column at one this morning, which is the first page of his own he has ever counted, and yesterday's two pages are not in it yet, and he has not decided what to do about that and did not say so out loud.

The clerk of nineteen years watched him do it and did not rule it and did not refuse it and did not ask him what it was for, and she entered one line about it and read it out loud before she wrote it.

"**A boy of about nineteen has ruled two columns on a sheet of his own, and a clerk is not ruling them and is not refusing them, and the left-hand one is a day and the right-hand one is a page, and this is not a fifth column of not-askings and it is not the column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud, and a clerk is not entering it as either of those two things, and is not entering it as anything else.**"

A clerk of nineteen years entered that a boy of about nineteen ruled two columns on a sheet of his own, and entered that the right-hand column is a figure about a page and not about a day, and entered that the difference between the two columns is a figure and that the difference is a figure about a schedule that nobody in this district chose, and entered that she is not entering what the difference will be tomorrow, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

The man of about thirty-four who mends fencing said one thing out loud from the far end of that table at about half past twelve, and he said it to the boards and not to the boy, and the boy of about nineteen counted what he said and got eighty-four and read the number back to himself in a low voice.

"**The figures on that wall go up by one a day and not by one a page, and the two of those are the same number most mornings and different numbers this month, and nobody in this district chose the difference and nobody in this district is going to get rid of it, and I have been in this yard two months watching about four of you write things down and this is the first one I have seen written down twice on purpose.**"

Nobody answered him. The clerk of nineteen years entered that a man had said a thing out loud there, and entered the thing, and entered that a figure on a board rises by one a day and not by one a page, and entered that a clerk may not enter that the two of those are the same figure, and did not enter it, and the record about the not asking says not asked.

---

At about one the man of about thirty-one who digs loam came up out of that bank and got the four figures at the near end of the boards and said the word with them four times, and that right arm of his did not go above the level of that shoulder at any point in the day, and he looked at the ruled line down the middle of the boy's sheet for about two seconds on his way past and said nothing at all about it. At about two the man of about thirty-seven who cuts reeds came up and stood at that table for about four seconds with the bundle under his arm and about nine people were within four feet of him and nobody said a word to him and the record about the not asking says not asked. At about half past two the road keeper came up that lane and got the four figures off that wall as he went past and was not asked about the eleven miles. At about three the man of about twenty-nine who drives a cart came up and went on up it, and the near wheel on that cart was still dragging, and he got it up about nine inches off the ground with one arm, and about four people watched him do it and one of them said out loud that it was the fifteenth time anybody had seen a man lift that wheel and not one of them ever asked him what the arm is for.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall with his coat folded on the stones beside him and nothing in his hands, and nobody offered him anything and nobody asked him anything, and the record about the not offering says nothing was asked and nothing was given. A clerk of nineteen years entered at about four that he is on his hundred and forty-fourth night of that run and that he has slept on a hundred and forty-three of them, and that she is not going to say what he is going to do with his hands tonight because nobody asked her.

---

A clerk of nineteen years entered the rest of that day at about half past four, in one long entry, and read it out loud to about nine people herself rather than waiting to be asked, and the rest of that ground did not hear it and was not told what was in it.

A hundred and three days is how long the bid has been open, and it was not run on this day, and nothing was proposed about closing it in a mouth or in a page. Fifty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is, and it has not been altered, and nothing correct has been written beside it, and the third line of that book is a date and a clerk entered a day a man said out loud on the eleventh of this month and entered it on her own page and not on that book. Sixty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood since the tenth of the tenth month. The first day of the eighth month is a hundred and thirty-three days past. The ninth of the nine printed nights is two hundred and thirty-six days back. A body four hundred miles off is seventy-two days past a printing it did not make, and nobody watched anything. The reading of that lot is begun and not finished and stands at the fourth of the five things a document that sets a lot out has to say, and the fourth is a person, and there is no column anywhere in this district for one. Twelve marks have been cut off that board since the mark for the first of this month, and twelve marks is a figure about a count of marks and is not a figure about a month, and there is no figure in this district for how long this month is. The column for the name of whoever read a thing out loud was ruled and empty at about six and nothing went into it, and the second column the boy ruled this morning is his own and is not that column. The fifth of the five things this district does not have is still not paid, and the fifth is a way to pay a person who is not in a household.

Nobody read that back from the top. The clerk of nineteen years entered that nobody read it back and entered the reason, which is that a thing read back by nobody binds nobody, and the record about the not reading says not read back.

At about half past five the two empty buckets came down that bank and went back up them full for the ninth time that day, and the woman of fifty-eight read the three lines in that lot book standing up on her way past, which takes her about two seconds longer than two lines takes her, and said nothing to anybody, and nobody there said one word to her. Then the man of fifty-six read the four figures off that wall for the last time in that yard and got all four of them.

The boy of about nineteen put his sheet in his inside pocket at about ten to six with a day in the left half of it and a page in the right half of it, and he did not know yet which of the two he would want in a fortnight, and the figure in the left half of that sheet is a day and the figure in the right half of it is a page, and they are two figures and not one, and a clerk has entered that once and has not been asked to enter it again.
