# Chapter 0989: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of Fifty-Six Said The Four Off That Wall At About A Quarter To Eight And Again At About Four And Got Them Both Times, And Put His Hand Flat On The Stone Afterwards, And About Nine People At The Top Of Eleven Feet Did Not Ask Him Anything About Any Of It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty-eight days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and three days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seventeen days. Eight hundred and seventy-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and six days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put one word to the man of fifty-six out loud. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and no line went on the back of that lot book for it because that page was not a place a reason could go before the two hundred and eighth morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and three mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-three mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-three mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-second morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fourteenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twenty-eighth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-seven of them. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-second time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and eighty-eight days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and eighteenth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He was at that wall before there was any light on it.

Between the two readings he goes and stands at the near end of the first table and looks at nothing in particular for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and then he goes back to the wall and does it again if it is going to be that kind of afternoon. Nobody has ever asked him why he reads it twice and nobody has ever asked him why the top one is last, and the reason he reads the top one last is a thing about his own eyes on a bad morning and not a thing about the stone.

He does not know and it has never once occurred to him to wonder, which is the difference between him and about four people at the top of eleven feet who have wondered about it and have said so to each other on the way up that bank.

There are four figures cut into that stone one above the other, and none of them is painted or chalked or filled in, and the top one is the worst of them because rain has got into the cut and taken the sharpness off its top left corner. That is the one that can go, and it goes not because he forgets it but because a man of fifty-six can get a stroke wrong in it on a morning when his chest is not what it was.

So he reads it last. He has read it last every morning for longer than most of the men at that bank have been standing at it.

He said the first three and then the fourth, one after the other with no pause in it you could put a finger into, and he got all four.

Then he put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one.

---

The place his hand goes is a shade paler than the stone either side of it, in the shape of a hand, and it is not much bigger than his hand, and he finds it in the dark.

Orren Pike had the boards up at about ten to eight and said the lane count and said no figure and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and he stood at the near end of the first table for a while after that and looked over toward that wall more times than he looked at anything else in that yard, and he has never once said at those boards what a man does at that wall, and nobody at those boards has ever asked him.

A pair of them came down that bank about nine feet behind where he was standing, and one of the two said something to the other about how early it was, and it was not said to him, and he did not turn round for it.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet were standing where the light had just come across the boards and they were watching his hand on that stone, and none of them said one word to him about it, or about the four, or about the fact that he had been doing both of those things at that wall before the boards went up on a morning when the wind was too hard off the top of the bank for anybody else to be out of their coat at that hour.

He stood at that wall until the light had come all the way over the boards and had made the ink on the last line written there shine, and then he took his hand off the stone and stood there with his arms down at his sides.

---

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table came round the outside and put his boot on the wedge.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and started at the empty end of his table and worked all morning without speaking. The turner's wheel went on turning. At the north end of that yard the man who digs loam had his own strip open and had been standing in the same place in it since about nine. The man of about thirty-four stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hand on the corner of his own table and said nothing out loud to anybody.

---

She came down at half past twelve and set the scale up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she was at it before the wind had come back round off the top of that bank, and every figure went onto the edge of her own board in chalk.

She said nothing out loud.

She had looked at that man at the wall more times than she had counted and she had never once walked over to where he was standing, and the reason for that, which she had never said out loud in that yard either, is that there is nothing to ask a person who is doing the only thing they do in front of you every morning, and that she had made her own arrangement with that at some point in a winter and had not gone back on it.

She weighed the next thing in the basket and put the figure on the edge of her board and looked out along the yard at the wall, where the four figures are, and about nine feet of that yard away a man was standing with his hand on the stone under the bottom one and had been standing there before she came down.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank. The turner had that number over his own wheel as well and it did not put a hand on his treadle. Nobody at those boards took it down.

By about half past three that low wall had been throwing a strip of sun about four feet wide along those stones for the best part of an hour, and the man at the foot of it had not moved out of it, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him. Good afternoon was said across the four feet between them and one word was given back, and that was the whole of the talking either of them did that afternoon.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall for the second time that morning and got all four of them.

Then he put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and left it there.

He said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and sixty-second time running.

Nobody asked him anything.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet were standing at the boards at that moment and had been watching that wall for an hour, and not one of them asked him anything about the four of them, or about the wall, or about his hand, or about the two times, or about anything else he had done that morning, and that was the whole of what they did about it.

---

Then the clerk read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in the ordinary voice, and she gave the account out loud before she put her pen to anything at all.

"The two hundred and seventy-third morning," she said. "Nothing said out loud in this yard this morning goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes on in my hand. That is the account and I have given it out loud before I wrote a word."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand, took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut the book and fifty-six lines after.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four, and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and did not go up that evening.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, knotted it, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank.

And the man of fifty-six was still at that wall with his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, after the boards had come down and after the light had gone off them, and the last of the others went up the bank about nine feet behind her and left him at the foot of the wall.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man of fifty-six said four figures off that wall twice this morning and got all four of them both times and put his hand flat on the stone afterwards, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything about any of it. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
