# Chapter 0980: The Two Hundred And Sixty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of Thirty-Eight Said Nothing Out Loud At Any Point Of It From About Half Past Nine Until About Four, And Nobody In That Yard Asked Him Anything Out Loud At Any Point Of It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and nineteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-four days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and eight days. Eight hundred and sixty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty-one days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-seven days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put anything to the man of thirty-eight out loud at any point of the morning, and nobody put anything to the man of fifty-six either. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

One reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and it was said on a morning when the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, so no line went on that page for it and it is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-four mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-four mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-four mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-third morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fifth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and nineteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eighteen of them, and this is the one hundred and twentieth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fifty-third time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-five lines and carried fifty-five lines when the clerk shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-nine days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and ninth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll is ninety-eight, spent, and may not be paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The light came across the eleven feet and the boards went up, and about nine people at the top of that bank came down it in the loose line they come down in, and at the foot of it the ground was already being worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table.

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it and said out loud that he was saying no figure this morning, and one of them asked him why, and he said it again in the same voice he says everything in.

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and the two men nearest him went on with what they were doing and did not look over at him.

Just after nine the second table was carried a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table came round the outside of it, set his own boot on the wedge, and sat down again.

---

He came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing.

That is what anybody at the top of that bank would tell you about how that morning started: a man came down the slope with his hands showing. He has no coat on in that weather. He carried nothing, because the pieces are already at the top of the bank, and he came down empty and started at the empty end of the table, which is the correct way round and has been for a long time.

He brought the pieces in off the ground where they had been left the day before and set them in a line along the near edge of that table. Not a heap. A line, with the ends squared off to the corner of the table, because a line can be counted by looking along it and a heap cannot.

When the line was full he went back up for more, and he came down the same way each time, so that anybody watching long enough could see the route he took and know the whole of it: out along the top of the table, round the end, up the slope, across, back down, and in at the same corner. He did that all morning without a tool and without speaking. About nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank past him at various times of the morning and about four of them counted his route without meaning to, the way you count a thing in a yard you have been in a long time.

There is a place at the near end of that table where the grain of the wood has been rubbed out of the top by the going of him, and it is the only part of that table anybody in that yard would call worn.

He has a place at that table that nobody gave him and nobody has taken off him. He has no bed, no wage and no office. He has not asked for any of the three, and he said a long time ago that he was not going to be thanked, and nobody at the top of that lane has ever offered him anything.

---

At about ten the man of about thirty-two put his own table up about four feet along from the second table and squared it with the back of his heel and went along the top of it with the cloth. The turner's wheel went on turning. At the far end of the second table the man who makes round things said nothing out loud at any point of this morning.

At the north end the man who digs loam had the strip open and did not come out of it. Nobody at those boards said good morning to him and he said nothing out loud to anybody.

The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve with her scale in a piece of cloth and set it up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk. She said nothing out loud. She did not look over at the near end of the first table and she was nine feet from it.

Nobody in that yard asked the man of thirty-eight anything out loud at any point from the boards going up to the light going off them. Not the man at the end of the second table, who was eight feet off him. Not the boy, who stood at that corner twice. Not the woman with the scale, who worked nine feet away for three quarters of an hour.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man of thirty-eight has worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table every morning of this stretch without anybody in that yard asking him one thing out loud, and that about four of them have not said what a man does with a morning that nobody takes any part in, and that about four of them have said he came down that slope at half past nine and went up it after the light went off the boards, and that about four of them have not said what he would have said if anybody had asked him.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and nothing at those boards took it down.

The man of thirty-eight heard it. He came down the slope with pieces in his arms at the time, and he went along the top of the table and set them down in the line, and about four of them were said after the man who keeps the tally and the fifth was not, and he did not look up.

The nails in that table have been gone over with something more than once. He did not put any of them back in himself, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him about that either. About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that he has been at that table a long time, and that about four of them have not said how he knows that the nails have been gone over.

---

A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall at about half past three and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near him, and he was not asked one question.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifty-third time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And at about four the man of thirty-eight stopped work at the near end of the first table. He stood with his hands in front of him and looked at the line of pieces along the top of that table without touching any of them, and nobody said one word to him, and he went up that slope about nine feet behind the others.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud, in the ordinary voice, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she read it before she put her pen to anything.

"The two hundred and sixty-fourth morning," she said. "The back of that book stands where it stood yesterday and the morning goes on it in my hand. That is the account and there is nothing else in it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and she shut the book and put her hand flat on the cover of it, and the page carried fifty-five lines before that and fifty-five lines after.

The light came off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and did not go up.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank last of all but two, with the chalk in her pocket and every figure she had weighed that morning still on the edge of her own board, and none of them said out loud.

---

That page stands at fifty-five lines and none of them is in the lot book. A man of thirty-eight worked at that table from about half past nine until about four and nobody in that yard asked him one thing out loud at any point of it, and a man of fifty-six read four figures off a wall twice and was asked nothing, and a woman weighed things at that bank and said one figure out loud and was asked nothing and thanked by nobody. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
