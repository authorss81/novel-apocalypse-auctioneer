# Chapter 0993: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Four O'clock The Man Of Fifty-Six Said The Four Off That Wall And Got All Four Of Them And Said In The Ordinary Voice That He Still Does Not Know What Those Four Of Them Are For, And Nobody Asked Him Anything About Any Of It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seven days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-one days. Eight hundred and eighty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and ten days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

Nobody put a question to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning and nobody said a reason out loud in this yard either, so the page at the back of that lot book did not move, and the clerk read that morning out loud and wrote the morning and took no line for it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and there was nowhere on that book to put it, so it went into the air and stayed there, and it is not counted anywhere.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seven mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-seven mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-seven mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-sixth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and eighteenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-second night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-one of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-third morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-sixth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-two days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-second afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

Nobody was watching that wall at about four o'clock.

He had got the four off it as usual and he had put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one as usual, and then he stood there waiting for the yard to be the yard it had been at that hour on all the mornings he could remember, and it was not.

There was a figure somewhere in the middle of that ground. He could not have said where it had come from and he did not try. What he had was the sense, which he has had before, that the middle of that yard was busy with a thing that had just gone across it, and that about four people at the top of eleven feet had their heads turned towards the middle of the yard, and that the man at the foot of the low wall had not moved and the man of sixty-one had not looked up off his own knees.

So he said his four words into about four feet of air in front of his own hand and the yard took them the way that yard takes a thing off the weather.

Nobody turned round. Nobody took a step. The wheel at the near end of that ground went on at the same speed it had been going at all morning, which is the loudest thing in that yard and is on all day whatever anybody does.

---

He is fifty-six. He has four figures cut into that stone one above the other, and none of them is painted or chalked or filled in, and the top one is the worst of them because rain has been getting into the cut for years and taking the sharpness off its top left corner, and he reads that one last because it is the one that goes.

That is a thing about his own eyes on a bad morning and not a thing about the stone, and he has never said so to anybody, and there is nobody in that yard he would say it to.

He said the first three and then the fourth with no pause in it that a finger would go into, and he got all four of them. He has not got one of them wrong in seven hundred and twenty-two mornings and he does not intend to start.

That morning he said them twice, once early with the light off that stone and once at about four o'clock with the light full on it, and in between, at about ten to twelve, he had gone and stood at the near end of the first table with his arms down at his sides and looked at nothing in particular for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

There is a walk between the two readings and he has walked it every afternoon of that stretch of mornings. It is about nine feet from the wall to the near end of the first table and back, and he walks it at the pace of a man who is not going anywhere and is not thinking about where he is, and he has got to the end of it and turned round and got back so many times that it has stopped being a walk anywhere in his own head.

What he thinks about on it, he has never said, and it is not about the four. What he thinks about on it is the eleven feet of light, and how much of the day is left in it, and whether he is going to be standing at that wall when it goes off it, which he always is.

Nobody at those boards has ever asked him why he does it twice, and nobody has ever asked him why the top one is last, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they would ask him one of those two things if there were any way in that yard of asking a person something without it turning into an event, and that about four of them have not said what would happen if they did.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved.

"Thirty," he said. "And no figure after it, because I am saying no figure."

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it. The man of thirty-two put his own table up and squared it with the back of his heel and went along the top of it with a cloth. The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked his route out along the top of his table and round the end and up the slope and back down and in at the same corner.

A wheel on a stand at the near end of that ground went on turning at the speed it goes at.

The woman who keeps a scale came down at half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket, and she said nothing out loud.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank.

He heard it. He was at the near end of the first table when it went and he heard all of it, the way he has heard that number every morning for as long as there has been a man keeping it in that yard.

And then, about a quarter of an hour later, something else went across the middle of that yard, and he did not know what it was, because it was not a figure anybody had read out, and it was not the number of the man who keeps a tally, and it was not a sentence he could make the shape of.

It might have been the man at the end of the second table saying something short to himself over a piece of his own work. It might have been nothing at all and four people turning their heads at the same moment for no reason that has ever been given to anybody.

He has never mentioned it to anybody and he is not going to, and there is nobody at that wall he could mention it to.

What he did do about it, at about half past two, was walk out to the near end of the first table and stand there with his hands at his sides and look at the middle of that yard, and the middle of that yard had nothing in it at all, and there was a woman at a scale with her back to him putting a figure on a board of her own, and a man at a table going along the top of it with a cloth.

Then he went back to that wall and stood at it with his hand on the stone under the bottom one and waited for the light to come down the eleven feet, which it did, about four feet at a time, and he watched all of it arrive.

---

What he did with it was turn round and go back to that wall and put his hand on the stone under the bottom one and wait, which is where he was standing when the light went off the boards anyway, so that it cost him nothing and it was not what he had come to the wall for.

He stood there for a good while with his hand on the stone and his back to the middle of that yard, and at about half past three the sun came over the low wall and laid itself down along the stones at the foot of it, and the man standing in that strip had been in it since about nine. A man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his arms hanging over his knees. Good afternoon went across the four feet and one word came back over it.

At four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that stone and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and said out loud, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and sixty-sixth time running.

Nobody asked him anything about it, or about that wall, or about his hand, or about the two times, or about anything else he had done that day.

---

And the clerk read that morning out loud once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account of it out loud before she put her pen to anything.

"The two hundred and seventy-seventh morning," she said. "Nobody has put a question to the man at that wall this morning and nobody has said a reason out loud in this yard this morning, so that page does not move and the morning goes on in my hand. I have said the account out loud before I have written any of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut that book and fifty-six lines after.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four. The goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and did not go up.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that slope with her back to that yard, and the man of thirty-eight went up about nine feet behind her with his hands showing, and the man of fifty-six was still at that wall with his hand on the stone under the bottom one after the boards had come down and after the light had gone off them.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man said four words into four feet of air this afternoon with his back to the middle of that yard, and that about four of them have not said what a man looks at when he is the only one looking at nothing.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man of fifty-six said the four off that wall twice this morning and got all four of them both times, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything about any of it, and nobody in that yard was looking at that wall at about four o'clock. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
