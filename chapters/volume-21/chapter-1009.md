# Chapter 1009: The Two Hundred And Ninety-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of About Forty-Eight Who Keeps A Tally Said His Number Into About Four Hundred Yards Of Air At About Half Past One And About Four People Repeated It Out Of Step With Each Other And At About A Quarter To Two She Said A Figure Of Her Own Into The Same Air

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and forty-eight days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty-three days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirty-seven days. Eight hundred and ninety-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and twelve days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and seventeen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and eighty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twenty-six days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and sixty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and fifty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one this morning and about four people repeated it out of step with each other, and at about a quarter to two the woman who keeps a scale said one figure of her own out loud, once, at her own scale, into the same air, and nobody in that yard said the two numbers were about the same thing and nobody said they were about different things. No reason was said out loud by anybody, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand without a line. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

It was on the one hundred and eighty-second morning that a reason was said out loud in that yard, and at that time the page at the back of that book was not a place a reason could go, so the reason was not written and it is not counted in this yard or in any record of this district, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-three mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy-three mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-three mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-second morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirty-fourth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and forty-eighth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and forty-seven of them, and this is the one hundred and forty-ninth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-second time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and eight days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirty-eighth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He says the count out loud before her hand moves, every morning, and has never once said it after.

That is the whole of what he has to give that yard at ten to eight and he gives it anyway, into the open air of a yard of about nineteen people, and then he tells them there is nothing else coming.

"Thirty," he said. "And that's all of it this morning."

---

There is a book wedged against the rail about two feet from his left elbow and it has been in that position for longer than he has been at those boards.

It has five lines on it. It has a column with one name in it and the heading over that column still says whoever. There is a space on a form that has been empty since before he started, and nobody has ever asked him about it, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him anything at all.

He could make a record of any morning he has ever sat on those boards. He has never made one.

---

At about half past nine the man of about thirty-two came along with his cloth and stopped at the boards and put one hand on the rail.

"That rail has moved in the night again," he said.

"It hasn't," said Orren Pike.

"You can see the daylight under it on this side where there wasn't any yesterday."

Orren Pike put his thumb on the rail where the man had said and looked, and there was no daylight under it on that side, and he said so, and the man of thirty-two went on along the top of his own table with the cloth and did not argue about it.

That is the amount of talking he gets at those boards. It is enough.

---

He counted nine spaces between the boards in front of him, and then he counted them again and got nine, and then he counted the pieces lying along the top of the man of thirty-eight's table, which he has counted on more mornings than he has counted anything else, because it is the one thing in that yard he can count with his hands where he is sitting.

The man of thirty-eight was working his route at about four feet off the near end of the first table and did not say one word out loud at any point of that day, and nobody in that yard asked him anything.

The count came out the same both times. He wrote nothing down.

---

She came down that bank at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table.

He watched her do it from the boards the way he watches her do it, which is closely, and without any expression at all, and with the whole of his attention.

He is seventeen. There are about nineteen people in that yard and he is the one who is at the place where the counting happens, and he has worked out over a good many years that the two facts have nothing to do with each other.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it.

It came to him at the boards and he did not take his elbows off the rail. It went up the bank and four voices picked it up out of step with one another and it came back down again, and he had heard that every morning of the run he has been at those boards, and it has never once been in a book.

---

At about a quarter to two the woman at that scale said one figure out loud, once, in the ordinary voice.

He heard it come across that yard and go up about four hundred yards of air the way everything goes up about four hundred yards of air in that place, and he waited for it to come back the way the other one comes back.

It did not come back.

He has no idea whether that means anything. That is the honest whole of what he has got on it, and he is the one person in that yard whose trade is numbers, and nobody in that yard said the two numbers were about the same thing and nobody said they were about different things.

He counted the spaces between the boards again instead and got nine.

---

Nobody answered her.

Nobody asked her what she weighs things for and nobody thanked her and nobody said anything at all, and the wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning through the whole of it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in.

The book was about two feet from his elbow with the back page of it open against the rail, and there was nothing on that page that morning that he had not already heard out loud in that yard before it was written there, and he could have put a figure on that page with a pencil out of his own pocket, and he did not, and he has never done it on any morning.

---

The sun came over the low wall a little after half past three and lay down along the stones at the foot of it.

The man standing in that strip of it said good afternoon to the man sitting about four feet off him, and got one word back, and the sun went on coming down those stones.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six had his four figures off that wall and got all four of them and put his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"I have not found out what these four are for and I have stopped expecting to," he said out loud to the stone.

About nineteen people heard that for the hundred and eighty-second time running and nobody asked him anything about it.

---

The clerk came along the boards to the near end of the second table at about four and gave the account of that morning out loud before she had the pen near any of it.

"The two hundred and ninety-third morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in that yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my hand and there is no line in it. That is the account of it and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the morning under the last line and took no line for it, and the page at the back of that book carried fifty-nine lines when she shut it and carried fifty-nine lines after.

---

The light went off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank stayed where they were.

He took his elbows off the rail and picked up his cloth and went along the top of his own table, which is the table of about nine feet he has been given for the purpose, and counted the pieces on the way.

Nine spaces. Nine pieces. Thirty pages brought down that lane, which is what he said out loud at ten to eight and which has not moved and is not moved by a decision, and a page going up that lane is not a page coming down it and a person going out of that yard is not a page and a person not asking is not a page either.

Nothing came down that lane. He wrote nothing down. Two numbers had gone through that yard that day and he had the whole of both of them in his head and neither of them was anywhere else.

---

Fifty-nine lines are on that page and not one of the fifty-nine is in the lot book, and it did not move on this morning. The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one and about four people repeated it out of step with each other, and at about a quarter to two she said a figure of her own out loud into the same air and nobody said the two numbers were about the same thing and nobody said they were about different things, and the boy at those boards, who is the one person in that yard whose trade is numbers, has no idea and wrote nothing down. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
