# Chapter 1003: The Two Hundred And Eighty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And She Put Her Own Board Against That Wall At About Four Feet Off The Near End Of The Second Table And It Stood There The Whole Morning And The Man Of Fifty-Six Read His Four Figures Off That Wall Twice And Nobody Read Her Board

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and forty-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and seventeen days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirty-one days. Eight hundred and ninety-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and eleven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and eighty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twenty days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and sixty marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

Her own board was put against that wall at about four feet off the near end of the second table this morning and stayed there the whole of it, and the man of fifty-six read four figures off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got all four of them both times, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the figures on her board have been on that board for nine years and that about four of them have not said who has stood nearest to it. Nobody read her board. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Once in that yard, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, a person said a reason out loud, and the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go before the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing was written for it on that morning, and it is counted nowhere.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and seventeen mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-seven mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-seven mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-sixth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-eighth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and forty-second night of that run, having slept on six hundred and forty-one of them, and this is the one hundred and forty-third morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-sixth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and two days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirty-second afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She had thought about where to put it for four days, which is four days longer than she has ever thought about where to put anything.

Nine years of figures are chalked along the top edge of that board in her own hand, in a gap she leaves at the end of each one, and the board has stood in this yard every one of those nine years at about four feet off the near end of the second table with its face to her hip where she can get at it with the chalk in her hand.

She had looked at that wall twice in four days and worked out the distance along it.

---

There was light on that wall at about a quarter to eight and the man of fifty-six was at it, because he is at it at about a quarter to eight, and she was coming down the slope at that hour and she saw him from about fifty feet off with his arm up and his mouth going.

She came down the rest of that slope and set her basket where she sets it, and she did not put her board where she puts it.

She carried it along the hard ground to the stretch of that wall that is behind the near end of the second table, and she turned it round so the figures on its edge were the right way up for anybody standing in that yard, and she set the foot of it against the stone, and she let go of it.

---

She went back for her basket.

That is the whole of what she did about it. She went back for the basket and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and put the cloth over her own scale and stood with her hands empty for a while, which is not a thing she does.

The board stood against that wall where anybody coming off that slope at any hour could see it if they were the kind of person who looks at a board.

---

What she wanted, and this is the plain shape of it, was for somebody to read it.

Not to ask her about it. She has no answer ready to that and would not want one asked. She wanted the thing that happens when a person reads a line of writing somebody else put down, which is that the writing stops being the private business of the person who wrote it and becomes a thing in the yard.

She had wanted it for about four days and had spent those four days finding out that she had no way of asking for it and no way of arranging for it.

---

About nineteen people came down that bank in the course of that morning.

She watched them from about four feet off the near end of the second table with her hands on her own scale, and she watched them the way you watch weather coming over a rise. She watched a man carry his own table up the hard ground and square a corner of it. She watched the man of thirty-eight go along a line of pieces and round the end of it and up the slope and back. She watched the turner's wheel go round at the near end of that ground without anything in the yard changing when it did.

Nobody read her board.

---

At about half past twelve the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table stood on the wedge under the leg that rocks until the men carrying it had gone by.

The board she had put against that wall did not go a foot further along anything. It stayed where she had put it, at about four feet off the near end of the second table as that table had stood at the start of that morning, and after the table had moved it was about three feet and a bit off the near end of it instead.

She noticed that. She noticed it and she did not move the board.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air in his ordinary voice, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other and it came off that bank and went away down the slope.

And a little before half past two she put a bar on her own pan and waited for it to come still and wrote the figure on the edge of her own board in chalk, in the gap she leaves at the end of each one, the way she has written them for nine years.

She did not say it out loud.

There was nobody in that yard to say it to that she wanted it to hear.

---

Nobody thanked her for the morning she had had with that board and nobody asked her what she weighs things for and nobody asked her why the board was against that wall or what was written on the edge of it, and the wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning through the whole of it.

Nobody at that yard had ever once looked at that board, and this morning it was standing against a wall with the light on it for the whole day, and nobody looked at it.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the figures on her own board have been on that board for nine years and that nobody in that yard has read one of them, and that about four of them have not said who has stood nearest to it.

Nobody up there has said that the answer to that is a woman who comes down that bank at half past twelve every morning and stands about four feet off the near end of the second table with her hand on the upright of her own scale, and has never once looked down at it herself while she was writing.

She knows what is written on that board better than anybody in this basin. That is what nine years does to a thing you wrote yourself.

---

The man of fifty-six came to that wall again at about four o'clock and had his four off it, all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone underneath the lowest one.

"Nobody has ever told me what these four are for and nobody at this bank is going to," he said, in the voice he uses for that stone.

He was about eight feet along that wall from where her board was standing. He had his back to it when he said it and he had his back to it when he put his hand down on the stone, and about nineteen people heard that sentence for the hundred and seventy-sixth time running, and not one of them asked him a thing.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet had two feet between the chalk on that board and the stone under that man's hand, and about four of them have not said what the distance is.

---

The clerk read that morning out loud at the near end of the second table and gave the account of it before she had the pen near any part of it.

"The two hundred and eighty-seventh morning," she said. "Nothing went out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so this morning goes in my hand and there is nothing else in it. That is the account and it went out loud before the writing of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line and took no line, and the page at the back carried fifty-seven lines when she shut that book and fifty-seven lines after.

---

The light came off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stayed up there.

She was the last person at that end of the table.

She took her chalk and put it in the pocket of her apron, and then she went along that wall and got her board up off the stone and turned it the other way round so the figures went back to facing her hip, and carried it back the four feet and stood it in its place.

She had put it against that wall at about a quarter to eight in the morning and she took it off at about half past four in the afternoon, and about nine people were on the top of that bank at that hour and about four of them have said since that they did not know she had done either half of that.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book, and it did not move on this morning. A woman put her own board against that wall at about a quarter to eight and took it off at about half past four and wrote one figure of her own on the edge of that board a little before half past two and did not say it out loud, and nobody read the board and nobody answered her and nobody thanked her and nobody asked her what she weighs things for. About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the figures on her board have been on that board for nine years and that about four of them have not said who has stood nearest to it. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
