# Chapter 0971: The Two Hundred And Fifty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Figure Went On The Edge Of A Board Of Her Own And Was Not Said Out Loud

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and ten days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and sixty-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-five days. Nobody has entered anything for eight hundred and ninety-nine days. Eight hundred and sixty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and seventy-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and forty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and eighty-eight days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and twenty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and fourteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

Nobody put anything to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning. No reason was said out loud and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard once before, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and it was said before the back of that lot book was any kind of a place a reason could go, and it was not counted anywhere then and is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-five mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-five mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and fifty-five, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said it before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-fourth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the six hundred and ninety-sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and tenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and nine of them, and this is the one hundred and eleventh morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and forty-fourth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines and it carried fifty-four lines when the light went off it. The record has stood one hundred and seventy days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundredth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The boards went up while the light was still on the eleven feet, and the wind came off the top of the bank flat and lifted the corner of the lot book off the rail it was wedged against. The boy got a thumb under it and pushed it back and did not look at what was written on the page it fell open to.

"Nobody reads it," he said, to nobody in particular, and went on with the boards.

He was seventeen and he had been at those boards long enough to know what was written on them and long enough not to have asked about it. What he did at them every morning was say the lane count out loud, because that was the one thing at those boards that got said out loud, and then either say a figure after it or say out loud that he was saying no figure. This morning he said no figure, and he said it in the same voice he uses for everything, and then he stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back and waited for the boards to stop being new to him.

A boy who says a figure with nothing after it is a boy who has said a figure. He had worked that out a long way back.

At the wall, the man of fifty-six said the four off the stone, one after the other and with no pause in it you could put a finger into, and he got all four. He put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and left it there. He had been coming to that wall long enough that there is a patch of it a shade paler than the stone either side of it, in the shape of a hand, and this morning he found it without looking for it, the way a man finds a door in his own house in the dark.

Two of them came down the bank about nine feet behind him and one said something to the other about the weather and neither of them said it to him. He did not turn round. About nine people at the top of eleven feet watched him do it and let him do it, and he stood there until the light had come right across the boards and made the ink on the last written line shine.

Orren Pike had come down behind those two with the boards still under his arm and he stopped about six feet off, because the boards were dry on the underside and could wait, and he watched the man's hand on the stone for a while before he carried them on. He has never said at those boards what that man does at that wall, and he has never been asked, and he knows more about it than anybody else in the yard.

He has never once read that page, and nobody at those boards has ever told him what is on it.

Just after nine, two men who did not speak to each other carried the second table a foot further along that ground and the near rail came with it and set down hard. The man at the end of that table came round the outside of it, put his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks, and sat down again without looking at anybody.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and took his place at about four feet off the near end of the first table, and there is a place at the near end of that table where the grain has been rubbed out of the top by the going of him, and it is the only part of that table anybody in that yard would call worn. He did not stop for anything. He did not turn round when the boards came down off the cart behind him, and he said nothing out loud at all that morning, and nobody in that yard put one word to him out loud at any point between the boards going up and the light going off them.

The man of about thirty-two carried his own table up the slope in both arms, one under each hand, and put it down about four feet along from the second table. He squared it with the back of his heel, once and hard, at the corner nearest the near rail, and the whole table moved about half an inch and stopped. Then he went along the top of it with the cloth, from the far end back to the near end, taking up what the night had left on it. He took the cloth back over the same stretch twice because there was more on it than there usually is. He has never spoken at that table.

At the far end of the second table the man who makes round things had his sleeves rolled and a bar in his hand and the small stove going at his elbow. He turned one of his round things over, looked at the place where it had been out of the fire, and put it back in.

---

She came down the bank at about half past twelve with the basket on her arm.

Nine years she has stood at that bank. She untied the knot in the cloth, folded it over the back of the leg, and weighted it with a stone she has carried in that pocket since before the yard had a second table on it. It is a stone off the bank and not a stone anybody gave her, and nobody has ever asked her where it came from either.

She stood at the scale a while before she put anything on it.

There is a thing she has never told anybody about, and it is not a secret, it is only that nobody has ever been in a position to hear it. She knows what her own figures weigh against each other. She can take a bar she has never weighed before and hold it in two hands for as long as it takes and know within a small margin what it will come to. Nine years of that. And every one of those figures goes onto the edge of a board of her own in chalk, and the board goes up that bank at the end of the day and comes down the next morning, and no page anywhere in this district has ever had one of them on it.

She put a bar on the scale. She waited for it to come still, and it took longer than most, because the wind was still coming over the top of the bank in that flat way and the pan wanted to move with it. Then she wrote the figure on the edge of her board, low down, in the gap left at the end of the last one.

She looked at what she had written for a while. She ran her thumb along the chalk — the chalk in that pocket is worn down to a nub and it has been for years — and she put it back in the pocket and went on standing at the scale.

She did not say it. Nobody stood close enough to have heard it if she had, and about four people at the top of eleven feet could have heard it from where they were and did not know there was anything to hear. She has never once read a figure off that board out loud to anybody, and she did not start this morning, and as far as anybody in that yard knows there is a figure on the board at all.

She weighed what else was in the basket and put those figures on the board too, and her hands were not steady for the first of them and were steady for the last.

There is a clean stretch of wood at the end of that board about the length of her hand, where the figures run out. Once, in a winter, she counted them along her own forearm with the tip of the chalk, and got to a number, and stopped. She has never written that number on the board and she has never said it to anybody, and it is not the sort of thing anybody in that yard would think to ask about.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally stood where he stands and put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it.

About four people said it after him out of step with each other, none of them at the same time as any other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank. The turner heard it over his own wheel and did not stop turning. Nobody wrote it down. The man who keeps the tally put his hand flat on his own coat afterwards and waited about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and then went and stood about nine feet further along, which is what he does, and he said no sentence before it and he said none after.

She was at her own scale with her hand flat on the upright when he did it. She did not turn round. She had the chalk in her hand, and she put it in her pocket, and she went on with the next thing in the basket.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the woman with the scale said nothing this morning at the point where that number goes into the air, and that about four of them have not said whether that was because she was not asked or because nobody was going to hear, and that about four of them have said the figure on her own board is where it was, and that about four of them have not said what anything is going to be done about it.

---

At about half past three the sun came onto the stones at the foot of that low wall.

The man of about sixty-four was where he is most afternoons. A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him. They said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon, and he said nothing else. Nobody went near him. He was not asked one question.

There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of that bank, and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-fourth time running. Nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk got up off her stool and read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people at the foot of that bank heard the whole of it.

"The two hundred and fifty-fifth morning," she said. "Nothing goes on the back of that book this morning, so the morning goes on it in my hand and nothing else does. Say it back to me if you did not hear it."

Nobody said it back to her. She sat down and wrote the morning under the last line on that page in her own hand, and took no line for it, and the pen went back into the holder, and the page carried fifty-four lines before she put her hand flat on it and fifty-four lines after.

---

At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard went with it, said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and did not go up. The boards came down the way they come down. The second table stood where it stands. The near rail was on the near side of it.

And then the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, knotted it, and put the basket on her arm, and she did not go up that bank with the rest of them.

She stood where she was until the last of the others had gone over the top. Then she took the chalk out of her pocket and looked at the figure on the edge of her own board — the one she had done last, the one she had held her hand over while the wind came over the top of the bank and the pan wanted to move — and she did not say it.

Then she went up that bank.

---

That page carries fifty-four lines and none of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. A woman has stood at that scale nine years and has been given nothing and has asked nothing. A man of thirty-eight has a place at that table that nobody gave him. Thirty pages have come down that lane and not one this morning. Nobody asked anybody anything about any of it.
