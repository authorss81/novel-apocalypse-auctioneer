# Chapter 0977: The Two Hundred And Sixty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Woman Of About Fifty-Two Came Down That Bank With A Frame And A Bag And Asked Nothing, And The Second Space On That Form Was Empty In Front Of Her For The Best Part Of An Hour And She Did Not Fill It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and sixteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-five. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-one days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and five days. Eight hundred and sixty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-eight days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-four days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-four marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody said good morning to the woman with the frame and nobody asked her anything at all. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go on that morning, so no line was written for it and it is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-one mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-one mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-one mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixtieth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and second of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and sixteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fifteen of them, and this is the one hundred and seventeenth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fiftieth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty, and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines when the clerk shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-six days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and sixth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll stands at ninety-eight and is spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She stood a while at the top of the bank before she came off it at all, the way a person stands when everybody else has gone down and there is still an audience of nobody on the top of a bank.

Then she came down with the frame in her right hand and the bag over her left shoulder, and she carried it in front of her with the bottom edge held clear of the ground, the way a person carries a thing they do not want scratched. The frame is wooden and about the size of a door off. There is a rebate cut round the inside of it, an inch and a bit deep, and nothing has ever been in that rebate, and the corners of the rebate are worn through where the wood has been lifted out and put back a number of times.

At about eleven she put the frame down in the sun at about four feet off the near end of the first table and stood it against the leg of it. Then she took the bag off her shoulder and set it on the ground beside her, and she took a form out of the bag and looked at it and put it back and took it out again.

Nobody said good morning to her. She said nothing to anybody. She asked nothing at all that morning, and nobody at that yard asked her anything either, not at the tables and not at the boards and not of the man of thirty-eight working nine feet along from where she was standing. About nine people at the top of eleven feet had come down that bank in the loose line they come down in, and about four of them had looked at the frame, and about four of them had not.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman carried a frame down that bank at about eleven with nothing in it and that about four of them have not said what a frame with nothing in it is for.

---

The form had two spaces on it. The first one had something in it. The second one was empty, and it was empty there in front of her with the light on it for the best part of an hour.

She did not put anything in it.

There is a thing about a form with two spaces where the first one is filled in: the whole shape of the paper tells you what belongs in the second one, and there is nothing to decide. That is the difficulty. Not that she had nothing to put there. That it was a shape with a place made for it and she was the only person standing near it.

Nobody at those boards asked her to fill it. Nobody at those boards said one word to her about it at any point of this morning. About four people at the top of eleven feet heard the sound of the pen in her hand twice, because she did pick the pen up both times, and put it down again both times.

About half past twelve she folded the form once and put it back in the bag and zipped the bag.

---

She picked the frame up off the leg of the table and stood it against the near rail instead, and looked through it.

It made the middle of that yard look further away than it was. The second table and the people at it went back past where they really were, and the man of thirty-eight at about four feet off the near end of the first table went back further than anybody, because he was the furthest thing from her of anything she could see through the rebate.

She stood there looking through it for long enough that somebody at the second table said something to somebody else about the light.

She did not say anything about what she was doing.

---

The rest of the morning went on behind her and she stood in the middle of it.

She had come down that bank with a thing to do and the thing had a shape, and the shape was on the paper in the bag, and everything else about the morning — the sun coming across the yard a foot at a time, the wheel going at the near end, the woman with the scale arriving with her basket at about half past twelve — went round the two of them like water going round a stone.

At about one she sat down on the low edge of the near rail with the bag beside her knee. Nobody noticed a woman sitting on the rail. She put her hand flat on the frame where it leaned and the rebate was under her palm, the worn-through corners of it, and she turned her hand over and looked at the places where the wood had been lifted out and put back a number of times.

At about two she got up and moved the frame from the rail to the leg of the first table again and looked at the yard through it from a different place, and from there the man of thirty-eight at about four feet off the near end of that table went past the far edge of the rebate and disappeared, and she moved to one side and got him back.

She had brought that frame a long way to carry it. Whoever had made it had made it for something that goes into the rebate, and the corners of the rebate are worn through from that thing being taken out and put back, and there is no such thing in this district now for that frame to be the frame of.

She did not put that thought anywhere. She had never once said a sentence about it out loud, in this yard or any other, and there is nobody she would say it to.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud. The woman with the frame did not come over to her and did not ask her anything.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and the turner heard it over his own wheel and said nothing. Nothing was written down anywhere in that yard for it.

The frame she carried down that bank has a rebate cut round the inside of it, and nothing has ever been in the rebate, and the corners of the rebate are worn through where the wood has been lifted out and put back a number of times. About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the corners of that frame are worn through, and that about four of them have not said what it is that goes in a frame like that.

---

At about half past three that yard had about four feet of sun on the stones and two men on it, a man of about sixty-four at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one four feet off him. They said good afternoon to each other across the four feet between them, and a man of sixty-one gave back the two words and nothing else at all. He was not asked one question, and nobody went near him.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fiftieth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in the ordinary voice and once only, and she read it before her pen went near the page.

"The two hundred and sixty-first morning," she said. "No line goes on the back of that book today and the morning goes on in my hand. I have said it out loud so that the yard has one account of it and not two."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-four lines before her hand went down and fifty-four lines after.

---

The light came off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard went with it, said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of eleven feet looked up the bank once first.

And the woman of about fifty-two picked the frame up off the near rail where she had stood it, and put the bag on her shoulder, and went up that bank with the frame in her hand and nothing in it, and the second space on that form went up with her in the bag and it was still empty.

---

Fifty-four lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. A frame with a rebate cut round the inside of it came down that bank at about eleven with nothing in the rebate and went up again at the end of the day with nothing in it, and a form with two spaces went down and came up and the second space is still empty. Nobody asked her to fill it. Nobody at the top of that lane has said one word.
