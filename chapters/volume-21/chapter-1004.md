# Chapter 1004: The Two Hundred And Eighty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Woman Of About Fifty-Two Stood In About Four Feet Of Sun With Nothing In Her Hands And Nobody Said One Word To Her And Nobody Said Good Morning To Her And The Second Space On That Form Was Empty The Whole Of The Morning

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and forty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and eighteen days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirty-two days. Eight hundred and ninety-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and twelve days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and eighty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twenty-one days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and sixty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

The woman of about fifty-two stood in about four feet of sun with nothing in her hands this morning and nobody said one word to her and nobody said good morning to her, and the second space on that form was empty the whole of the morning and nobody asked her to fill it, and no reason was said out loud by anybody in that yard, so the page at the back of that lot book did not move and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand without a line. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The one hundred and eighty-second morning is a morning on which somebody said a reason out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go on it, so that reason was not written down and it is counted nowhere now.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and eighteen mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-eight mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-eight mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-seventh morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-ninth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and forty-third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and forty-two of them, and this is the one hundred and forty-fourth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-seventh time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and three days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirty-third afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She had the form in the inside pocket of her coat, folded in four, where it had been since before the light came over that bank, and she took it out once at about nine and looked at it and put it back.

There are two spaces on that form. She has put something in the first one. The second one has been empty every time she has had the thing out in her hands, and she is not going to be the one who fills it, and nobody at that bank has ever asked her to.

---

She had come down that slope with nothing in her hands on purpose.

That is the thing she wanted to try. Every other time she has come down that bank she was carrying something, and the something gave her a reason to be standing where she was standing, and a person with a reason for standing in a place is a person that place has an opinion about.

So she came down it with the form in a pocket and her hands empty, and she stood in the four feet of sun that the low wall puts down in the middle of the morning, and she waited to find out what a yard does with a person who is in it and has not brought anything.

---

Nothing.

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them, which is thirty, and said out loud that nothing was coming after it. Nobody said good morning to her. Nobody said good morning to her at ten, or at eleven, or at noon, or when the second table was carried a foot further along the hard ground just after nine with a man standing on a wedge under the leg that rocks while the men carrying it went past.

She found, standing in the sun with her hands empty, that she was having to work at not being spoken to. That is what the whole morning was.

---

At about half past nine a man came down that slope with his hands showing and went to about four feet off the near end of the first table and started along a line of pieces laid out along the top of it, and round the end of it, and up the slope, and back down, and in at the same corner again.

He did that about four times an hour all day.

She stood and watched a great deal of that yard go past her in the same way, and none of it was done for her and none of it was withheld from her. It was the same as if she were a post at that end of the ground.

---

At about ten to ten she put her hand flat on the sun-warmed stone of the low wall, because there was nothing else to do with it, and she understood then why that man stands there.

About half an hour later a man of about thirty-two came and set his own table up on ground that would take it and squared a corner of it with the heel of his hand and ran a cloth along the top.

Nobody ran a cloth along the top of anything for her.

---

At half past twelve the woman who keeps a scale came down that bank with her basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and the two of them were in the same stretch of that yard for the rest of the day with about eleven feet between that wall and the top of the bank above them and no wall at all between where the two of them stood.

She said nothing to the woman with the scale. The woman with the scale said nothing to her. Both of them are women who have said a sentence out loud in that yard in the past stretch of mornings and been answered by nobody, and neither of them looked over at the other one, and that was the whole of what passed between them.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally said his number into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people said it after him out of step with one another, and it came back off the top of that bank.

The number came out about ninety feet from where she was standing and she had heard it every morning of that run, and this morning she heard where it went, which was nowhere, and she found she had been taking it for granted that it went somewhere.

---

At about half past three the sun came over the top of that low wall and lay down along the stones at the foot of it, and she saw the man standing in that strip step into it, and saw a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones with his coat folded beside him.

"Afternoon," the one on the stones said across the four feet.

"Afternoon," the man at the foot of the wall said back.

Two words across four feet after a whole morning, and she was about forty feet off them and got the whole of it, and she was not in it.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at the wall at the far end of that ground and got his four figures off it and put his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"I could not tell you the use of these four and I have never been able to," he said out loud to the stone, in the voice he uses for that stone, and about nineteen people heard him say it for the hundred and seventy-seventh time running, and nobody asked him anything.

She listened to him say it and understood that whatever that yard had done with her that morning it had done a great deal more of it to him, and she did not go and stand next to him, and she did not say anything, because he did not want anything said near him at that hour and she was not going to be the first.

---

The clerk read that morning out loud about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account of it out loud before she had her pen near any of it.

"The two hundred and eighty-eighth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so I write the morning in my hand and there is no line in it. I have said all of that before I wrote any of it."

She wrote the morning and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-seven lines before that book was shut and fifty-seven lines after.

Nobody in that yard looked at the woman standing in the four feet of sun while any of that was being said out loud.

---

At about half past four the light came off those boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in.

She said it too.

She said it out loud, into about four feet of sun, to nobody, in the ordinary voice, and nobody said it back to her, and the boards came down off the cart face down and the yard emptied itself up that slope.

---

She went up that bank with her hands still empty and the form still folded in four in the inside pocket of her coat.

The second space on that form was empty the whole of that morning and it was empty when she got it out again at the top of that bank, and nobody had asked her about it at any point of the day, and she had spent the whole of a morning finding out what it is to stand in a place where the whole of you is available and none of it is taken up.

She put the form away. She had wanted the sun and she had got it, and there is nobody she could tell that to.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book, and it did not move on this morning. A woman of about fifty-two stood in about four feet of sun with her hands empty from about nine until the light went off those boards, and nobody said good morning to her and nobody said one other word to her and the second space on that form was empty all day and nobody asked her about it. She said the goodnight with the rest of them and nobody said it back to her. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
