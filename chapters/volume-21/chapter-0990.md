# Chapter 0990: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Eleven, At About Four Feet Off The Near End Of The Second Table, The Man Of About Thirty-Four Said Out Loud, Once, In The Ordinary Voice, In About Nineteen People's Hearing, That He Was Going To Ask Her For The Figure In The Morning, And Nobody Answered Him And Nobody Asked Him Why And The Page Took No Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and four days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and eighteen days. Eight hundred and seventy-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-one days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and seven days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

One thing was said out loud this morning at about four feet off the near end of the second table and the page did not move for it, so the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody answered him and nobody asked him why and nobody asked him to say it again. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and it was said before the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, so nothing was written down for it then and that reason is not counted anywhere in this yard or in any record of it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and four mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-four mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-four mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-third morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fifteenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twenty-ninth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-eight of them, and this is the one hundred and thirtieth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-third time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and eighty-nine days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and nineteenth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He put his hand into his coat pocket at about a quarter to eleven and left it in there, which he had never done at that corner of that table.

He had squared the table at ten with the back of his heel, at the corner nearest the near rail, once and hard, the same amount as every morning, and he had taken the cloth along the top of it from the far end back to the near end, and then he had stood at the near end of it with his hand flat on that same corner, which is a thing a man does when he has finished a job and has not decided what to do with his hands.

The boards were up. The light had come the whole way across the eleven feet. At the wall the man of fifty-six had said the four and got all four of them and was still there.

---

At about eleven he said it.

He said it into that yard and not to anybody in it. He said it in the ordinary voice, at the speed he uses for a thing he means, and he said it once, standing where he stands every morning, about four feet off the near end of the second table, with his hand out of his pocket and down at his side.

"I am going to ask her for the figure in the morning," he said.

---

Nobody answered him.

Nobody asked him why. Nobody asked him to say it again, and nobody asked him to say it quieter, and nobody asked him anything at all, and the turner's wheel went on turning over the whole of it.

The man at the end of the second table went on with his own work without lifting his head. The man of thirty-eight went along the top of his table, round the end, up the slope, across, back down, and in at the same corner, without breaking the route. The man at the far end of the second table took a thing out of the fire and looked at the place where it had been in and put it back in.

About nineteen people at the foot of that bank heard the whole sentence.

Nobody asked her anything about it. Nobody turned and looked at her to see what she did. She was about four feet off him with her hand on the upright of her own scale and a bar in the other one, and about four people at the top of eleven feet had the pair of them in the corner of their eye, and nobody in that yard said a word to either of them.

---

He took his cloth and went along the top of that table from the near end to the far end, and then back along it again, because that is what a man does with a cloth.

What he had not expected — and he had expected a great deal, and had lain awake at some point in the middle of the night with a list — was how fast it would be over.

One sentence, and there was nothing left to do about it, and the rest of the morning was going to come whether he had said it or not, and there was not one single thing in that yard that had changed shape. He could not have said how long he had been standing at that corner of that table, and there was nobody in that yard he could have asked.

He squared the corner again with the back of his heel, without meaning to, and the table did not move this time because it was already square.

Then the morning did what the morning does, which is go on.

The wheel went on turning and nobody at that end of the yard put a hand on it. The man who digs loam went back into the same place in the strip at the north end. The man at the end of the second table took his boot off the wedge and sat down on the low edge of the near rail with his forearms on his knees. The man of thirty-eight went up the slope and down it and came back in at the same corner of his table and put the cloth along the top of it, and nobody at those boards asked him one word out loud at any point of it.

At about half past two Orren Pike came off the bank with his hands empty and stood at the near end of the first table and looked at the two of them at about four feet off the near end of the second table for as long as it takes to count a thing once, and then he went back up the slope and nobody stopped him and he did not say anything.

---

The woman who keeps a scale waited for the pan to come still.

The wind had come up over the top of the bank again about half past ten and the pan wanted to go with it, and she stood where she was and let it want to go, the way a person lets a thing want to go, and while she was standing there she thought, in the ordinary way, about the two things still in the basket and about the fact that the second one was the awkward one and would want doing last.

Then the pan came still and the figure was the figure and she wrote it on the edge of her own board in chalk, low down, in the gap at the end of the last one.

Orren Pike was at the boards with his elbows on them and he had heard the whole of what that man said, because he hears everything that goes into that yard, and he did not turn round, and he did not write it down, and he has never once written down anything anybody said in that yard, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the boy is the only man in that place who could have made a record of this morning and did not make one, and that about four of them have not said what that is.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off the bank, and nothing at those boards took any of it down.

At about half past three the stones at the foot of that low wall took the sun, and the man at the foot of it was standing where he had been standing all day, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his coat folded beside him on the stones. They said good afternoon and he said the one word back, and neither of them said anything else after that.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what those four of them are for. About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and sixty-third time running, and nobody asked him anything about any of it.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud. She did it at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice, and she gave the account of it out loud before she wrote a word of it down.

"The two hundred and seventy-fourth morning," she said. "Something was said out loud in this yard this morning and there is nothing on the back of that book for it, so the morning goes on in my hand and nothing else does. I have given the account out loud before I wrote it and that is all of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut the book and fifty-six lines after, and the pen went back into the holder and the book went onto the boards where it is wedged against the rail, and the boy put his thumb under the corner of it to settle it.

---

Nothing in that yard was different at half past four from what it had been at eleven, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man said one sentence out loud at about eleven this morning and got no answer to it and has not mentioned it again since, and that about four of them have not said what he expected to get.

The light went off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of the nineteen people in it. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stayed, and the boards came down off the cart face down.

The man of about thirty-four went up that slope with the rest of them and did not go near her scale on the way past it.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, knotted it, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank, and there was a figure of her own on the edge of her own board that she had said out loud once, on a morning this yard had not asked her for it, and a figure of her own that she had said out loud once on an earlier morning in that same stretch, and neither of the two of them is on any page in this district.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. At about eleven this morning a man of about thirty-four said one thing out loud, once, in the ordinary voice, in about nineteen people's hearing, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and nobody answered him, nobody asked him why, nobody asked him to say it again, nobody asked her anything about it, and the page took no line. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. A figure of her own went out into the open air of that yard on two mornings out of that stretch of them and on no page and in no book and down no lane, and nobody in that yard has done anything about either of them. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
