# Chapter 0996: The Two Hundred And Eightieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of About Twenty-Nine Who Drives The Cart Came Through The Gap In That Bank And Stopped In It, And Said Nothing At All

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and ten days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-four days. Eight hundred and eighty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and thirteen days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A man came through the gap in that bank this morning and stood in it, and nobody said a reason out loud in this yard and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand and took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and it was said on a morning when nothing on the back of that lot book could have taken it, so it went nowhere then, and it is not counted anywhere now.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and ten mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-ninth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-first of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-fifth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-four of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-sixth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-ninth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-five days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-fifth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He came through the gap at the top of that bank at the ordinary hour with the cart behind him, and he stopped in it.

Not a long stop. He put the wheel of that cart where the grass begins and stood with the reins slack in his hand and looked down the slope at that yard for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

He has gone through that gap on every morning of a run that is longer than the second table has stood on that ground, and he has gone through it the way a man goes through a door he knows the width of, without putting a hand up, without slowing, without looking at the two ends of it.

This morning he stopped in it, and he did not know why, and he did not find out why, and there was nobody standing near enough that gap for him to ask.

---

Down there a woman was at a pair of scales about four feet off the near end of the second table with a bar on the pan and her hand flat on the upright, and the boy was at the boards with his elbows on them, and a man of thirty-eight was at his own table, and a wheel was going at the near end of that ground, and the light was coming the whole way across the eleven feet.

He looked at all of it and none of it at once.

There is not much to look at in that yard and a man who comes over the top of that bank on a cart sees the whole of it at once and then loses most of it, because the bank takes the near end away from you and the boards take the far end, and what is left in the middle is a woman at a scale and a strip of open ground and a man going along a table with a cloth.

He had looked at that same hundred feet this morning and had seen it the way you see a thing you have seen every morning of a run of mornings, which is to say he had seen the shape of it and none of the people in it.

Then, this morning, for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, he saw the people.

The wheel went round and the boy said something he could not hear from up there and the woman wrote something on a board and did not look up, and a man at the near end of a table went along it with a cloth and did not look up either. A man at the far end of that table had a stove going with the fire showing in it, and he did not look up either.

Then he took the cart on through the gap and down the far side, which is the way it goes, and he did not say one word out loud in that yard, and he has not said one in any morning.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man stopped in that gap on a morning when a figure had been said four hundred yards away at the north end of that yard, and that about four of them have not said what a man looks at from nine feet up that slope.

He was four hundred yards off the north end and about forty feet above the middle of that yard, and what he had been looking at was not the north end.

---

The rest of that morning went the way it goes.

The boards were up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved on anything, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure. That man of fifty-six had been up at the wall before the sun was on it and had got all four of them off it.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table put his boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks and left it there. The man of thirty-two put his own table up on the hard ground, squared it with the back of his heel, and went along the top of it with a cloth, and a pair of them came up the slope carrying the boards off the cart and set them down face down and went back up.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and went to the empty end of his own table and started there, and he worked his route out along the top of it and round the end and up the slope and across and back down and in at the same corner, and he did not break it once for anything.

The wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning at the speed it goes at. The man who makes round things had the fire in the small stove at the far end of the second table and a thing in the tongs.

At the north end the man who digs loam was in a new place in that strip, having found one on the ground before this one, and he had been going at it with a spade for a good part of an hour by then.

---

She came down at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set the scale up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk.

She said nothing out loud that morning.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off that bank and went out flat over the top of it, and nothing at those boards took it down.

The man of about twenty-nine was up the slope by then and out of that yard and going along the top of that bank with the cart wheels ringing on the hard ground, and the number he keeps went out of that yard at about half past one and nothing at all went after it up that slope.

At about one he put the cart down at the top of that bank and came down the slope on foot with nothing in his hand, and stood at the boards for a while with his back to that yard looking at the top of the bank, and nobody at the foot of that bank said one word to him about it, and after a while he went back up and got on the cart and went on along the top.

He has done that four mornings out of a good many, and it is the only thing he does at that bank that is not getting a cart from one place to another place, and there is nobody in that yard he has ever asked about it.

At about two a man of about forty-two came down that bank with a coil of rope over his shoulder and stood at the boards for a while.

"How high are they, these boards?"

"About as high as your elbow," the boy said. "And about as wide as two of them laid flat side by side, and you do not want the height. You want the width, because you have got a load on that rope and you are thinking about where it goes."

"The width," the man said. "That is what I want."

"Then the width is what you get." The boy went back to the rail at the near end of those boards and did not look up while the other man stood there being told something he had known the answer to.

Then the man went back up. Nobody at that bank has ever asked the boy why he answers questions from men who come down there for something else.

---

The sun came over that low wall at about half past three and lay down along the stones at its foot. The man standing in that strip had been in it since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his arms hanging over his knees.

Good afternoon went across the four feet between them and one word came back over it, and then nothing at all.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that stone and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and said out loud, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and sixty-ninth time running.

Nobody asked him anything.

And the clerk read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the whole of the account out loud before she had her pen to any part of that page.

"The two hundred and eightieth morning," she said. "Nothing has been said out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes on it in my hand and there is nothing else in the account of it. I have given the account out loud before I have written a word of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut that book and fifty-six lines after, and the pen went back into the holder, and the boy put his thumb under the corner of that book to settle it.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and did not go up for a while.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that slope, and the boards came down off the cart face down, the way they are always put away.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man of about twenty-nine who drives that cart came through the gap in that bank this morning and stopped in it, and said nothing at all, and went on down the far side. Nobody in that yard said one word to him about stopping. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
