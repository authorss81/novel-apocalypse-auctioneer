# Chapter 0979: The Two Hundred And Sixty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Figure Went Out Of That Yard A Foot Away From Where A Man Came Through The Gap With A Cart And Did Not Stop

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-three days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seven days. Eight hundred and sixty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-six days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put anything to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard and the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go on that morning, and nothing was written down for it then and it is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-three mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-three mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-three mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-second morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and fourth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eighteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and seventeen of them, and this is the one hundred and nineteenth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fifty-second time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-five lines and carried fifty-five lines when the clerk shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-eight days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and eighth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll is ninety-eight and is spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

Further up the slope, where the bank is cut through, there is a gap in it, and the cart was standing on the high side of that gap waiting its turn.

The gap is the only way a cart gets through that bank without going round the end of it. The two ends of it are worn round at the edges from the wheels, and the ground under it is harder than the ground either side of it because everything that comes through here has been pressed into the same soft place for longer than the second table has stood where it stands. A man who drives a cart that way every morning knows all of that and has never once been told any of it.

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall, in the order they come on it, and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one. About nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him and nobody asked him anything.

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and then stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back.

The second table went a foot further along about nine and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table came round the outside of it and put his heel down on the wedge and sat down with his weight still on it.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table, and the light was already on that table when he got to it. The man of about thirty-two carried his own table up the slope in both hands and put it down about four feet along from the second table, squared it with the back of his heel, took the cloth along the top of it, and said nothing at any point of it.

At the north end the man who digs loam had the strip open and the spade in and out of the same place, and he said nothing out loud to anybody all morning and nobody said good morning to him.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud, and she looked up the bank twice while she was doing it.

Both times she was looking at the same thing, which was the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally, who was not standing where he stands.

He had walked up to the gap. He was there with his hand on the rail, and the cart was waiting on the high side of it about ten feet off him, and he was standing the way he stands, which is square and a little forward on the toes.

At about half past one he put his number out into about four hundred yards of air from there, in the voice he uses for it.

About four people at the top of that bank said it after him, out of step with each other and a half beat late, and the sound of it came down the slope and went along the row of tables and off the far end.

And while it was still going across that yard, the man of about twenty-nine came down through the gap with the cart.

---

He came through about a foot away from where the number had gone out.

He did not stop. He did not put his head out to see who was standing there. He said nothing out loud, and the wheels went over the hard ground in the gap the way they go every morning, and the cart went up the far side of that bank and was gone over the top.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet were on that bank when the two of them crossed, and about four of them saw the number go out of the air and about four of them did not, and not one of them said anything to anybody about either part of it.

Nobody in that yard said one word to the man in the cart or about him. He came through a gap in a bank with a figure going out of the air beside him and he did not stop for it.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a figure was said a foot away from where he went through that gap, and that about four of them have not said what a man is doing when a number goes out beside him and he does not stop for it, and that about four of them have said he comes through there every morning and has never once been asked which way he was going, and that about four of them have not said what anybody would have said to him.

She was at her own scale about four feet off the near end of the second table when it happened, with her hand on the upright. She saw the cart go and she said nothing about it and went on with the next thing in the basket.

She had seen that cart go through four hundred times. She had never once seen it go through with a number still in the air.

The man in the cart comes through that gap and up the far side and over the top, and that is the whole of that journey. He does not look at the tables and he does not look at the boards and he has never once looked at the woman who keeps a scale, because she is four feet off the near end of a table at the bottom of a bank and a man driving uphill with a loaded cart is looking at the ground.

That is what went through the gap this morning. A figure went out of the air about a foot from him, and he did not hear it over his own wheels, and he did not stop, and there is nothing in that to be angry about and nothing in it that anybody has said a word about.

---

At about half past three the sun was on the stones and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, and a man of sixty-one was about four feet off him. They said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon. He said nothing else, and nobody went near him.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifty-second time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once only, and it was read out before it was written, and about nineteen people heard every word of it.

"The two hundred and sixty-third morning," she said. "The back of that book stands where it stood yesterday. The morning goes on it in my hand and no line goes on it, and I am saying that out loud because somebody at those boards has to say it."

Then she wrote the morning in her own hand under the last line on that page, and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-five lines and it carried fifty-five lines after she had put her hand flat on it.

---

About half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and nobody at the top of that bank answered anybody back or said anything to anybody. The boards came down as they do. The near rail was on the near side of that table and the second table stood where it stands.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, put the basket on her arm, and went up that bank on the same side the cart had gone up on, and the man who keeps the tally was still standing at the gap when she passed him, and neither of them said anything to the other.

---

That page stands at fifty-five lines and not one of them is in the lot book. A figure went out of that yard this morning a foot away from where a man came through the gap with a cart and did not stop, and nothing came down that lane and nothing went on that page. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. A cart has gone through that gap four hundred times and no one has ever asked the man driving it which way he was going. Nobody asked anybody anything about any of it.
