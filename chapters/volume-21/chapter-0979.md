# Chapter 0979: The TWO HUNDRED AND SIXTY-THIRD Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Of About Twenty-Nine Who Drives The Cart Came Through The Gap And Did Not Stop And About Four People At The Top Of Eleven Feet Have Said Since That A Figure Was Said A Foot Away From Where He Went Through That Gap And The Page At The Back Carries Fifty-Five Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eighteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-three days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and seven days. Eight hundred and sixty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is.

Five hundred and eighty-seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-six days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and thirty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. Seven is what a clerk has counted. It did not move this morning. This district has not got four documents. Four places can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it.

No question was put to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning. No reason was said out loud this morning. Nobody said no. So that page did not move. The morning went on in her own hand. Five is what this district does not have. The five are unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-three mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and ninety-two. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-three mornings and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and sixty-three, on the word of the man who keeps it. It was said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-second morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. That is the seven hundred and fourth of those mornings. The lane count is thirty and it did not move. Nothing moved it. That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it. It has a column with one name. The heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-five lines. It carries the same figure as yesterday. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the seven hundred and fourth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eighteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and seventeen of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and eighth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and seventy-eight days, and the page at the back took no line this morning. The morning was read out loud before it was written down. Nothing came down that lane. None of it is in the lot book. None is on the page at the back. The second space there is empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight, said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him and one asked him what he was doing.

The light found the eleven feet and the boards went up, and about nine people at the top of that bank came down it, and further up the slope, where the bank is cut through, the gap was there with the cart standing on the high side of it waiting its turn.

---

He said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight, in the order they come on it, and he got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him and nobody asked him anything.

---

The lane count was said out loud at those boards at about ten to eight, which is thirty, and no figure was said after it, and the boy said out loud that he was saying no figure, and then stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back.

---

The second table went a foot further along about nine and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table came round the outside of it and put his heel down on the wedge and sat down with his weight still on it.

---

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table, and the light was already on that table when he got to it. The man of about thirty-two carried his own table up the slope in both hands and put it down about four feet along from the second table, squared it with the back of his heel, took the cloth along the top of it, and said nothing at any point of it.

---

At the north end the man who digs loam had the strip open and the spade in and out of the same place, and he said nothing out loud to anybody all morning and nobody said good morning to him. The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed four things and chalked four figures on the edge of her own board, and said nothing out loud, and she looked up the bank twice while she was doing it.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally was not standing where he stands. He had walked up to the gap with his hand on the rail and the cart waiting on the high side of it, and he put his number out into about four hundred yards of air from there, in the voice he uses for it.

---

About four people at the top of that bank said it after him, out of step with each other and a half beat late. And while it was still going across that yard the man of about twenty-nine came down through the gap with the cart, about a foot away from where the number had gone out, and he did not stop, and he said nothing out loud, and the cart went up the far side of that bank and was gone over the top.

---

Nobody in that yard said one word to the man in the cart or about him. He came through a gap in a bank with a figure going out of the air beside him and he did not stop, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a figure was said a foot away from where he went through that gap, and that about four of them have not said what a man is doing when a number goes out beside him and he does not stop for it.

---

She was at her own scale about four feet off the near end of the second table when that happened, with her hand on the upright, and she saw the cart go and she said nothing about it and went on with the next thing in the basket. The gap is the one place in that bank where a cart can get through without going round, and everybody who works in that yard knows what is going to come through it and when. She had seen it go through four hundred times. She had never once seen it go through with a number still in the air.

The gap in that bank is the only way a cart gets through that bank without going round the end of it, and the two ends of it are worn round the edges from the wheels, and the ground under it is harder than the ground either side of it. A man who drives a cart every morning knows that and does not have to be told it.

---

The sun was on the stones at about half past three. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall, and a man of sixty-one was about four feet off him. They said good afternoon. A man of sixty-one said good afternoon. He said nothing else, and nobody went near him.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifty-second time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once only, and it was read out before it was written, and about nineteen people heard every word of it.

---

"The two hundred and sixty-third morning," she said. "The back of that book stands where it stood yesterday. The morning goes on it in my hand and no line goes on it, and I am saying that out loud because somebody at those boards has to say it."

---

Then she wrote the morning in her own hand under the last line on that page, and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-five lines and it carried fifty-five lines after she had put her hand flat on it.

---

About half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and nobody at the top of that bank answered anybody back or said anything to anybody. The boards came down as they do. The near rail was on the near side of that table. The second table stood where it stands. And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank on the same side the cart had gone up on, and the man who keeps the tally was still standing at the gap when she passed him, and neither of them said anything to the other.

That page carries fifty-five lines. None of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. Seven is what a clerk has counted. Fourteen is what this district has made. Five is what this district does not have. The fifth of the five is unpaid. A line there is not a payment. A gatepost sheet carries four hundred and eleven. By how much, nobody has done. The toll is ninety-eight. It is spent. It may not be raised or paid twice. The low wall's ground is public lot. Since the one hundred and seventeenth afternoon. It is not a payment of that. The bid ran four hundred and fifty days. It was not run. It is not a lot. It will not be. The heading over that name says whoever. The name does not satisfy the rule. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The other space is on different paper. It is empty as well. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-eight days. Nobody asked what it is days of. Nobody asked the man of fifty-six one thing. Nobody asked the man of thirty-eight one thing. No reason was said out loud this morning. No line went on that page. A woman has weighed things there nine years. She has not been given anything. She has not asked for anything. He has no bed, no wage, no office. He has asked for none of the three. The lane count is thirty. It did not move. That page is at fifty-five lines. Nobody asked anybody anything about any of it. A figure went out a foot from where a man passed. It did not go out of that yard with him.
