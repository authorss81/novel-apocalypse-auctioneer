# Chapter 0987: The Two Hundred And Seventy-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man At The End Of The Second Table Said One Thing Out Loud, Once, About His Own Table, And Nobody Answered Him And The Page Took No Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty-six days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-five. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and one days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and fifteen days. Eight hundred and seventy-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty-eight days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and four days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-four marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

One thing was said out loud this morning by the man at the end of the second table about his own table, and it was not a reason, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put one word to him after he had said it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go on that morning, so it was not written down, and that reason is not counted anywhere and has never been counted since.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and one mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-one mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-one mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventieth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twelfth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twenty-sixth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-five of them. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixtieth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when the clerk sat down this morning. The record has stood one hundred and eighty-six days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and sixteenth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He had fifteen years at that end of that table and about eleven feet of daylight to work in, and he had used every morning of them the same way.

Boot on the wedge. Weight on the boot. Then he worked his own way down the length of the table and back, and then he sat on the low edge of the near rail with his forearms on his knees until about half past twelve, and then he did the second half of the day from the far end instead of the near one.

Nobody had ever asked him any of it.

He has done the second half of the day from the far end since a spring about fifteen years ago, and the reason for it is that the sun comes across those eleven feet from the far side, and a man who works a table from the wrong end of it spends the middle of every afternoon with his own shadow lying across his work.

He has never said that out loud in that yard and there is nobody in it he would say it to, and it is not a thing anybody has ever thought to ask him, because he has never once been the sort of man a person thinks of asking.

He is about thirty-seven. Fifteen years at that end of that table is longer than the man of thirty-eight has stood at his, and it is longer than the near rail has been on the near side of it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that nobody in that yard knows either of those two things and that about four of them have not said whether it would make any difference to him.

---

The boards went up at that bank with the light still lying across the eleven feet. The boy said the lane count out loud at them, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure.

The man of fifty-six had already been at the wall and had already said the four and had got all four of them.

Just after nine the second table was carried a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of it came round the outside, put his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks, and stood there while the two men carrying it got clear of him.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and started at the empty end of the first table. At the near end of that ground the turner's wheel went on turning, and nobody at that end of the yard had ever once heard him say anything about it.

---

At about eleven he said it.

He did not stand up and he did not put his hand on anything to do it. He had one boot on the wedge and his weight on that boot and he turned his head about nine inches and said it into that yard in the ordinary voice, at the speed a man uses for a thing he has decided to say.

"That wedge under that leg is mine, and I have put my boot on it every morning for fifteen years, and I am saying that out loud this morning, and then I am going back to the end of the table."

Then he took his boot off the wedge and set his heel down flat on the ground and went to the far end of the table and started his half of the day from there.

---

Nobody answered him.

Nobody thanked him. Nobody asked him what that leg is doing, and nobody asked him to say it again, and the man at the far end of that table did not look up over the top of what he was holding, and the man of about thirty-four did not turn round at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and the turner's wheel went on turning over the whole of it.

About nineteen people heard it and the whole of it went into the ground the way his boot had been going into that wedge for fifteen years.

Nobody at those boards wrote one word of it down.

Orren Pike was at those boards with his elbows on them and he had heard the whole of that sentence, because he hears everything that goes into that yard, and he did not turn round and he did not write it down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the boy could have made a record of a man owning a piece of wood this morning in about the time it takes to turn round, and that about four of them have not said why he did not. The clerk was sitting with the book open on her knees at that moment and she wrote nothing then and she wrote nothing later, because there was nothing in that yard that morning that went on the back of that lot book.

---

She heard it from her own scale.

She had the chalk in her hand and she heard a man at the end of that table say one plain sentence about a piece of wood under a table leg, and she stood there with her hand on the upright and let him finish it.

What went through her was not the sentence. What went through her was that he had said it, and that a person who has done a thing for fifteen years can decide one morning to say the doing of it out loud, and that the deciding takes no longer than the saying, and that she had got to that point herself twice.

She put the chalk away and weighed the next thing in the basket.

---

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man at the end of that table said one thing out loud at about eleven this morning, and that about four of them have not said what it is to hear a person own a piece of wood, and that about four of them have said nothing went on the page at the back of that book for it, and that about four of them have not said what the page is for.

---

At about half past three the sun was lying along that strip of stones and the man at the foot of that low wall was still standing on it. A man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his back against nothing at all. Good afternoon was said between them, and one word came back over the four feet, and after that neither of them said anything for the rest of the afternoon.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall, got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for. About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and sixtieth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

Then the clerk read that morning out loud from about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice, and she gave the account of it before she wrote a word of it down.

"The two hundred and seventy-first morning," she said. "There is a thing said out loud at that table this morning and it is not one of the things this page takes, so the morning goes on in my hand and nothing else does. I have said that out loud before I wrote it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut the book and fifty-six lines after, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched her do it and said nothing.

---

About half past four the light went off the boards, and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stayed on the bank.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank.

And the man at the end of the second table sat on the low edge of the near rail with his forearms on his knees, and nobody came and stood near him, and nobody asked him anything about the wedge, and it was still driven into the hard ground under that leg where it has been for longer than anybody has said out loud.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man at the end of that table said one thing out loud at about eleven this morning about his own table and the page took no line for it, and nobody answered him and nobody thanked him. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
