# Chapter 0988: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Man Of Thirty-Eight Went Up That Lane In The Afternoon With Nothing In His Hand And Came Back Down It With Nothing In His Hand, And The Lane Count Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and two days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and sixteen days. Eight hundred and seventy-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty-nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and five days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-five marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put one word to the man of thirty-eight out loud at any point of it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the back of that lot book did not become a place a reason could go until the two hundred and eighth morning, so no line went on that page for that reason, and it is not counted anywhere and is not going to be counted.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and two mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-two mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-two mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-first morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirteenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twenty-seventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-six of them. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-first time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and eighty-seven days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and seventeenth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The morning was the whole of the same thing again: boards, light across the eleven feet a foot at a time, the second table carried a foot further along, the boot on the wedge, the heel on the corner of a table, the wheel going.

He came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and started at the empty end of the first table, and he worked the way he works, out along the top of the table, round the end, up the slope, across, back down, and in at the same corner, and he did not break that route for anything all morning.

At about four feet off the near end of that table the grain has been rubbed out of the top by the going of him. He has a place there that nobody gave him. There is no bed in this district for him and no wage and no office, and he has not asked for the one or for the other or for the other, and he said a long time ago that he was not going to be thanked, and nobody at the top of that lane has ever offered him anything.

---

The lane goes up behind the top of that bank and out of sight of that yard in about nine yards of it.

It is not a made road. It is where the ground stops being the yard and starts being the side of that bank, and there is nothing at the top of it that anybody at the foot of that bank has ever described, and the two men who carried that table a foot further along this morning have each walked up about nine yards of it with a leg of the table on their shoulder and turned round again.

Orren Pike was at the boards with his elbows on them all through the time that man was gone, and he wrote nothing down, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the boy sat at those boards for the whole of that afternoon with a clear view of the top of the bank and did not once look up it.

Nobody at the foot of that bank has ever been up it.

There is no gate at the top of that lane that anybody at the foot of that bank has ever watched go open, and nobody at the foot of that bank has ever walked up it to find out, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that it is the only thing within about four miles of that yard that nobody there knows the end of, and that about four of them have not said whether they would rather go on not knowing.

He had been up it once before, in a stretch of mornings a long way back, and he had come back down it, and the whole of what he had made of the going was that the light up there is different from the light down here and there is more of it, and he had put that nowhere. Put like that it is not quite the shape of the thing: people have gone up it and come down it, and it was always men going somewhere else at a different hour with something in their hands, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have never seen a person go up it in the middle of a working day, and that about four of them have not said what they think that means.

He had looked at that lane for a stretch of mornings. It is the only thing in that yard he looks at that is not a table.

---

At about two he stopped work.

He put the last piece he had in his hand down on the line along the near edge of that table, squared off to the corner, because a line can be counted by looking along it and a heap cannot, and then he stood at the end of it and looked up the bank.

Then he went up the slope about nine feet behind the rest of them, and turned off at the point where that lane leaves the top of the bank, and went up it.

He went up it with nothing in his hand. Both hands, open, at his sides, the whole way.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet saw him go. Nobody said anything about it. The turner went on turning. The man at the end of the second table went on with his own half of the day from the far end. The woman who keeps a scale had her hand on the upright of her own scale and watched him go up that lane from about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she had a figure on the edge of her own board that she had not said and did not say.

He was gone long enough that about four people at the foot of that bank looked up at the top of the bank and then looked at each other and then went on with what they were doing.

Nobody went after him. Nobody said he ought to. Nobody asked him where he was going, before he went or while he was gone, and that yard has never in any morning asked him that, and it did not ask him that today.

---

He came back down that lane in the same part of the afternoon, and he came back down it with nothing in his hand.

Nobody at the top of that lane has ever offered him anything, and he came down the slope about nine feet behind the others with his hands showing again and went in at the same corner of the first table and put his hand on the top of it and stood there.

Then he took the cloth and went along the top of that table from the near end to the far end, and then back, and the piece he had left on the line was still square with the corner where he had put it.

Nothing came down that lane today. The count of what has come down that lane in the hands of the people who brought it has not moved and was not moved by a person going up it and not moved by a person not asking for anything at the top of it.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and nobody at those boards took it down.

At about half past three the sun came onto the stones at the foot of that low wall, where the man at the foot of it had been standing since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him. They said good afternoon to each other across the four feet, and he said afternoon, and he said nothing else after that.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and he put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for. About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and sixty-first time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in the ordinary voice, and she read it before she had put a pen to anything at all.

"The two hundred and seventy-second morning," she said. "Nobody has said a reason out loud in this yard today, so the morning goes on the back of that book in my hand and nothing else goes on it. I have said it out loud before I have written it and that is the whole of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut the book and fifty-six lines after, and she put the pen back in the holder and the book back on the boards.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in, and they stayed where they were standing until the boards had gone down that bank face down.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, knotted it, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank, and about four feet behind her a man of thirty-eight came off the top of that table and went up the slope with his hands showing.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man went up that lane in the middle of a working day with nothing in his hand and came back down it with nothing in his hand, and that about four of them have not said what a person does with a walk like that, and that about four of them have said he came back down it in the same part of the afternoon he went up it, and that about four of them have not said why.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man of thirty-eight went up that lane this afternoon with nothing in his hand and came back down it with nothing in his hand, and nobody in that yard asked him one thing out loud about it at any point, and the lane count is thirty and did not move, because a person going up that lane is not a page and a person not asking is not a page either. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. Nobody at the top of that lane has ever offered him anything and he still has no bed and no wage and no office and has asked for none of the three. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
