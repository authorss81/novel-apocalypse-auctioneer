# Chapter 0978: The Two Hundred And Sixty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Man Of About Thirty-Four Said One Reason Out Loud, Once, About His Own Knee, And The Page At The Back Took One Line Carrying The Morning And Not The Reason

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and seventeen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-two days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and six days. Eight hundred and sixty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-five days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-five marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

One reason was said out loud this morning by a man about himself, so one line went on the page at the back of that lot book, and the line carries the morning and not the reason. Nobody answered him and nobody thanked him. The lane count is thirty and it did not move. Nothing came down that lane. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-seven days.

There was a morning, the one hundred and eighty-second morning, on which a person said a reason out loud in that yard, and no line was written for it then because the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go, and it is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-two mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-two mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-two mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-first morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and third of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and seventeenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and sixteen of them, and this is the one hundred and eighteenth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fifty-first time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-five lines and carried fifty-four lines when the clerk sat down this morning. Three of the first thirty-three were never read.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and seventh afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is spent, and three of the first thirty-three lines on that page were never read.

---

By the time the light had come the whole way across the eleven feet the boards were up, and about nine people at the top of that bank were on their way down it.

At the near end of the first table a man of about thirty-four stood with his hand flat on the top of it, waiting for the yard to fill. He squared that table every morning. He carried it up the slope himself, one hand under each end, and he put it down about four feet along from the second table, and he squared it with the back of his heel at the corner nearest the near rail, and then he took the cloth along the top of it twice.

The heel goes in on the same corner every morning, hard, the same amount, and that is the whole of his method and it has never varied. He squared it that way first in a winter when the corner nearest the rail was the corner that kept not sitting, and then the near rail moved and the leg still moved, and he kept squaring it.

It is the leg on his side. He has known that for a while. The knee on that side goes into the job and comes out of it a degree different every morning, and it has been going into that job every morning for long enough that most of the men at that table have never once known why the table squares the way it does.

He had the sentence ready. That was the thing. He had had it ready for a stretch of mornings, in the shape you give a thing when you have thought about it too many times, and what stopped it every morning was that he could not find the moment it belonged to.

The man of fifty-six read the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and left it there while the light came across the eleven feet and reached the place his hand was, and nobody asked him anything.

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and then went and stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back and waited for the light to come across the boards.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along the ground and the near rail came with it on the near side. The man at the end of that table came round the outside of it and put his heel down on the wedge under the leg that rocks and sat down with his weight still on it.

---

At about eleven he said it.

"I have been squaring up the leg of that table every morning for longer than most of the men at it have stood at it, and my knee on that side is the reason for that, and that is all of it, and I am going back to the end of it."

Then he went back to the end of it, and he picked up the cloth, and he took the cloth along the top of the table from the far end to the near end.

Nobody answered him.

Nobody thanked him. Nobody asked him to say it again and nobody asked him what the reason was for, and nobody at those boards asked him one word about any of it, and the turner's wheel went on turning over the whole of it.

The man of thirty-eight worked on at about four feet off the near end of that table for the rest of the morning and did not look over at him, and the man at the end of the second table put his head down over his own work, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet went on with what they had been doing.

He put the cloth over his shoulder when he had finished. He stood at the end of the table with his hands hanging, and Orren Pike, who had come along the near side of the first table with his arms full of nothing, went past him and did not slow down. It is not a thing you can take back and it is not a thing anybody did anything with, and by the middle of the morning he had stopped standing there as though he had.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud to anybody that morning.

She heard him. She had the chalk in her hand at the time, and she heard a man at the other end of that yard say one thing out loud, and she put her chalk away and weighed the next one.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and nobody wrote that one down either.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man said one thing out loud at that yard this morning about himself, and that about four of them have not said what it is to hear a person say a plain thing about their own body where about nineteen people can hear it, and that about four of them have said nobody answered him, and that about four of them have not said what would have happened if somebody had answered him.

---

There is a place about four inches from the foot of that page where the last line does not sit level with the ones above it, because there is a worn place in the paper there. The clerk has never ruled it out. Nobody in that yard has ever asked her about the worn place and she has never mentioned it.

About four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifty-first time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice, and nobody at those boards asked her to read it again, and she wrote it after.

"The two hundred and sixty-second morning," she said. "One line goes on the back of that book this morning and the line carries the morning and nothing else goes on it. I have given you the account out loud before I wrote it, and that is the whole of the account."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line on that page in her own hand, and the line went over the top of the worn place and did not sit level with the ones above it, and the pen went back into the holder, and nobody at those boards said one word about the page while she did it.

---

At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank stayed on that bank while the boards came down.

The boards came down, and the second table stood where it stands, and the near rail was on its near side. And the man of about thirty-four stayed at the end of the first table after the boards had come down and put his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks and stayed there with his weight on it.

The woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and went up that bank with the basket on her arm.

---

That page stands at fifty-five lines, one more than it stood at yesterday, and not one of the fifty-five is in the lot book. The line that went on today carries the morning and carries nothing else, and it went over the top of the worn place about four inches from the foot of the page and did not sit level with the lines above it. Nobody asked the man of thirty-four why he said it, and nobody asked him to say it again, and nobody thanked him. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
