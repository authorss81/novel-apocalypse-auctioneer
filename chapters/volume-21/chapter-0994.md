# Chapter 0994: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Half Past One She Said One Figure Of Her Own Out Loud, Once, At Her Own Scale, And Nobody Answered Her And Nobody Thanked Her And Nobody Asked Her What She Weighs Things For

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and eight days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-two days. Eight hundred and eighty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and eleven days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A figure of her own went out loud at that scale this morning and no reason was said out loud in this yard, so that page did not move, and the clerk read that morning out loud and wrote the morning in her own hand and took no line for it. About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the page did not move this morning, and that about four of them have not said why it did not move. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Somebody said a reason out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the page at the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go on that morning, so no line was written for it, and that reason is not counted anywhere now.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and eight mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-eight mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-eight mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-seventh morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and nineteenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-two of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-fourth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-seventh time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-three days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-third afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She had the chalk out before the figure was off the pan.

That was not a thing she does. What she does is wait for the pan to come still, and write the figure on the edge of her own board in the ordinary way, and put the chalk back in her pocket, and put the next thing on, and let the whole of the weighing be the quietest thing at the foot of that bank.

At about half past one the pan came still and she took the chalk out and waited for the light to come far enough along that board to make the chalk shine, and then she looked out along that yard instead of at her own board, and she said it.

One figure. In the ordinary voice. Once.

He is the one man in that bank who puts a figure out to the whole of about four hundred yards of air, and she was not doing it the way he does. She gave it to about as far as the corner of that yard goes and no further, which is about four feet past the near end of her own scale, and there was a man standing at about that distance who had his back to her.

---

Nobody answered her.

The wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning over the whole of it. The man at the far end of the second table put his thumb on the rim of the thing he was holding, turned it a quarter round, and set it down on the stone without looking over. The man of thirty-eight went along the top of his own table and round the end and did not break his route and did not look up.

The boy had his elbows on the boards with his back to her and did not turn round.

Nobody asked her where the number came from. Nobody asked her what she weighs things for. Nobody said the word again to her and asked for it. Nobody put it to her to see whether she had got it right the first time, and nobody thanked her, and nothing at those boards took it down.

She went on with the basket.

Her hands were not steady on the next thing and she let them be unsteady, because a still hand that is lying is worse than a hand that is not. The one after that came to rest and the figure was the figure and she wrote it on the edge of her own board the way she has written every one of them for nine years, low down, in the gap at the end of the last.

---

She had been getting ready for it since half past twelve and she had not known that was what she was doing.

What she had actually done was untie the cloth and fold it and weight it and put the first thing on the pan and wait, four times over, and the chalk had been in her hand three of those four times instead of in her pocket, and she had put it away without marking anything, and on the fourth the pan came still and the chalk came out of her pocket before she had decided about it.

There is a bare stretch of wood at the end of that board, about the length of her hand, where no chalk has ever gone. She has never written anything on it. She has never run a thumb along it to find where the last one is, and she has never once wondered whether the board is nearly finished with, and both of those are one decision and she made it years ago and it has nothing to do with what she said out loud this afternoon.

What she wanted, and she had wanted it for two mornings, was not for anybody to answer her. She wanted to know what that yard did with a figure when it was loose in the air. She wanted to stand at a pair of scales with one already out of her mouth and find out whether it turned into a thing that happened to other people, the way a reason does, or whether it went the way her figures have gone for nine years, which is nowhere at all.

It went nowhere at all.

That was the whole of the answer and she had it standing at that scale with the chalk in her hand, and there was nobody she could give it to and nothing she could put it on.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the page at the back of that lot book did not move this morning, and that about four of them have not said why it did not move.

She was not one of the four and she did not ask anybody. She had not said a reason out loud in that yard this morning and she had said a figure, and she had been on that bank long enough to know that those were two things, and that nobody in that yard had ever done anything about either of them.

The second thing in the basket was the awkward one and it was the shape that never sits, and she had put it to the back of her list that morning on purpose, and she had then brought it forward twice while she was waiting for the wind to go off the top of that bank.

It went down onto the pan and wanted a hand under the upright while she waited and she gave it the hand, and the figure came out the figure, and she wrote it on the edge of that board in chalk low down in the gap at the end of the last one, and she put the chalk away.

She had said one figure out loud that afternoon. She was going to go on weighing for another two hours and she was going to write four or five more of them, and at the end of that day there was going to be one figure in the open air of that yard with about nineteen people in it and a good many figures on a board of her own with nobody at all.

She thought about it once more at about two, with the last thing in the basket going down onto the pan, and then she stopped thinking about it and went on weighing, because the wind had come up over the top of that bank about half past one and the pan wanted to go with it and it took a person with both hands on the upright to wait that out.

---

The boards had gone up at about ten to eight and the boy had said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure.

That wall at the far end had a man at it before the light, and he had got all four of them off it before the boards were up.

The second table was carried a foot further along just after nine and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table took the wedge side of it without being asked. The man of thirty-two set his own table up on the hard ground, squared it at the near corner, and went along the top of it with a cloth, and the wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table, out along the top of it and round the end and up the slope and across and back down and in at the same corner, and he said nothing out loud at any point of it.

The man of about thirty-four was at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hand flat on the corner of his own table, and he said nothing to anybody either, and he did not go over to the place where her scale went up.

She came down that bank at half past twelve with the basket on her arm.

---

At about half past one she said the figure out loud, once, at her own scale, in about nineteen people's hearing.

Nobody answered her and nobody thanked her and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and the wheel went on turning over the whole of it, and about four hundred yards of that morning took a figure off a woman at a pair of scales and gave her nothing back for it.

At about half past three the sun came over that low wall and lay down along the stones at its foot, and the man standing in it was where he had been standing since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him. They said good afternoon across the four feet and one word came back.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said out loud, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and sixty-seventh time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And the clerk read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account out loud before she had her pen to anything.

"The two hundred and seventy-eighth morning," she said. "Something went out loud at that scale this morning and nothing went on the back of that book for it, so the morning goes on it in my hand and there is nothing else in the account of it. I have said that out loud before I have written any of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut that book and fifty-six lines after.

---

The light went off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen.

And the woman who keeps a scale took the chalk out of her pocket and looked at the two she had done last and put it back without marking anything, which she had done four times in one morning about two years ago and had not done at all since.

Then she folded the cloth, knotted it over her own scale, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank about nine feet behind the last of the others.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. One figure of her own went out loud at that scale this morning at about half past one in the ordinary voice, and nobody answered her, nobody thanked her, nobody asked her where the number came from and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and that page did not move. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
