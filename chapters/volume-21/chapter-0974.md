# Chapter 0974: The TWO HUNDRED AND FIFTY-EIGHTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Of About Thirty-Two Put His Table Up And Squared It With The Back Of His Heel And Went Along The Top Of It With The Cloth And The Man Who Digs Loam Was At The North End And Said Nothing Out Loud To Anybody All Morning And The Page At The Back Carries Fifty-Four Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and two days. Eight hundred and sixty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is.

Five hundred and eighty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-one days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and thirty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and seventeen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. Seven is what a clerk has counted. It has not moved. Four of those documents are not owned here. Four places can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it.

Nothing was put to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning. No reason was said out loud this morning. Nobody said no. So that page did not move. The morning went on in her own hand. Five things this district does not have. It is five and it is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-eight mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and eighty-seven. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-eight mornings and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and fifty-eight, on the word of the man who keeps it. It was said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-seventh morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. That is the six hundred and ninety-ninth of those mornings. The lane count is thirty. It did not move. It is not moved by a decision. That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it. It has a column with one name. The heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines. It did not move this morning. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and ninety-ninth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twelve of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and third afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and seventy-three days, and the page at the back took no line this morning. The morning was read out loud once, and only then written. Nothing came down that lane. None of it is in the lot book. None is on the page at the back. The second space there is empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight, which is thirty, said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure this morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard him say both of those things.

---

---

The boards went up when the light found the eleven feet. At the north end of that yard the strip the man who digs loam has been opening was already open, and he was in it before most of the others were down the bank.

---

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall in the ordinary voice and got all four of them and laid his hand flat on the stone afterwards, and nobody at the top of that bank asked him anything about it.

---

The boy said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and then went and stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back.

---

The man who digs loam had the north end to himself all morning. He opened the strip a foot deeper than he had opened it the day before and set the spade against the side of it and worked down into it a foot at a time, and he stopped twice to push the sweat off his face with the back of his wrist. He said nothing out loud to anybody at any point of this morning. Nobody at those boards asked him what the strip was for and nobody at those boards said good morning to him either.

---

At about nine that second table went a foot further along the ground, and the near rail came with it on the near side. The man at the end of that table got up, went round the end of it, set his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks, and sat down again.

---

The man of about thirty-two came up the slope at about a quarter to ten carrying his own table in both arms, one under each hand, and put it down about four feet along from the second table. He stood over it and looked at the top of it from one end to the other. Then he squared it with the back of his heel, once, hard, at the corner nearest the near rail, and the whole table moved about half an inch and stopped. Then he went along the top of it with the cloth, from the far end to the near end, taking up what the dust of the night had left on it, and he took the cloth back over the same stretch twice because there was more on it than there usually is. He did not say anything to anybody while he did it.

---

The man of thirty-eight was already at about four feet off the near end of the first table when that was done, and he kept on at his own work while the table was carried past him, and he did not look up at any of it.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and weighed four things and chalked four figures on the edge of her own board, and said nothing out loud to anybody all morning. Nothing was said to her about yesterday either. Nobody came over to her scale. Nobody asked her what she weighs things for. The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one and four people said it after him out of step with each other, and she was at her own scale when he did it and she said nothing then either.

---

She had said one out loud yesterday, in the ordinary voice, and about nine people had heard it, and this morning that yard got on with its morning. She could not tell from where she was standing whether any of it had happened. That was the whole of what had changed, and it was not a thing you could see from the end of a table.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman said a figure out loud at that bank yesterday and nothing has come of it this morning, and that about four of them have not said what a yard is supposed to do with a figure that a person has said out loud, and that about four of them have said nobody asked her anything about it this morning, and that about four of them have not said whether anybody heard it and did not say anything on purpose.

The woman who keeps a scale has a stone in her pocket for weighting the cloth and she has had one for nine years, and it is a stone off the bank and not a stone anybody gave her. She has never once been asked where the stone came from either, and she has never once said, and it is the same pocket the chalk is in.

---

Nobody went near the man at the foot of that low wall this afternoon, and he was not asked one question. A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him at about half past three. They said good afternoon. A man of sixty-one said good afternoon, and he said nothing else.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them and put his hand flat on the stone, and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-seventh time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud, once, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and about nineteen people heard it, and the turner's wheel was still going while she did it.

---

"The two hundred and fifty-eighth morning," she said. "No line goes on the back of that book and the morning goes on it in my hand, and I am telling you the same thing I told you yesterday and the day before that."

---

Then she wrote the morning under the last line on that page in her own hand and did not take a line for it, and put her pen back in the holder, and the page carried fifty-four lines before her hand went down on it and fifty-four lines after.

---

At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up. The boards came down and the second table stood where it stands and the near rail was on the near side of it. And the woman who keeps a scale put the cloth over her scale and tied it and carried the basket up that bank, and on the way up she had the chalk in her hand and she did not stop to use it, and at the top of the bank the north end of the yard was still open and the man who digs loam was still in it.

---

That page carries fifty-four lines. None of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. Seven is what a clerk has counted. Fourteen is what this district has made. Five is what this district does not have. The fifth of the five is unpaid. A line there is not a payment. A gatepost sheet carries four hundred and eleven. By how much, nobody has done. The toll is ninety-eight. It is spent. It may not be raised or paid twice. The low wall's ground is public lot. Since the one hundred and seventeenth afternoon. It is not a payment of that. The bid ran four hundred and fifty days. It was not run. It is not a lot. It will not be. The heading over that name says whoever. The name does not satisfy the rule. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The other space is on different paper. It is empty as well. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-three days. Nobody asked what it is days of. Nobody asked the man of fifty-six one thing. Nobody asked the man of thirty-eight one thing. No reason was said out loud this morning. No line went on that page. A woman has weighed things there nine years. She has not been given anything. She has not asked for anything. He has no bed, no wage, no office. He has asked for none of the three. The lane count is thirty. It did not move. That page is at fifty-four lines. Nobody asked anybody anything about any of it.
