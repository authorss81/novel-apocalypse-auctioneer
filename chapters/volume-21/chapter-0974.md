# Chapter 0974: The Two Hundred And Fifty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Yard Carried On Exactly As It Had The Morning Before, Which Took A Person Most Of The Morning To Find Out

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and two days. Eight hundred and sixty-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-one days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and seventeen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody said one word to the man of fifty-six out loud, and nobody said one word to the woman who keeps a scale about yesterday either. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was said before that page was a place a reason could go, and nothing was written down for it then because there was nowhere on that page for it to go, and it is not counted anywhere now, and the mornings in that yard on which a reason has been said out loud have never been counted out loud either.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-eight mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-eight mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and fifty-eight mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-seventh morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the six hundred and ninety-ninth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twelve of them, and this is the one hundred and fourteenth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and forty-seventh time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines when the light went off it. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-three days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and third afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll is ninety-eight and has been spent. Of the first thirty-three lines on that page, three were never read.

---

He came up the slope at about a quarter to ten with his own table in both arms, one under each hand, and the boards were still up.

He had walked it up that slope every morning for a long stretch, and he could have carried it in one hand by now. What he would not do was turn it round on the way, because a table carried with the leg that rocks at the wrong angle all the way up arrives at the top of a bank having been carried wrong, and the difference between the two is half an inch at the corner and you find it or you do not.

He put it down about four feet along from the second table. Then he stood over it and looked at the top of it from one end to the other without touching it, the way a man looks at a thing he has already decided about and is only being fair to.

Then he squared it with the back of his heel, once, hard, at the corner nearest the near rail, and the whole table moved about half an inch and stopped.

Then he went along the top of it with the cloth, from the far end back to the near end.

The first pass never looks like enough. That is not a feeling, it is a thing about dust on a board: the first pass takes the dust that is lying loose and leaves the grit that has been worked into the grain, and the grit is the part that catches the light and looks like dirt, and it comes off on the second pass. So he took the cloth back over the same stretch, once, with more weight on it than the first time, and put the cloth over his shoulder.

He did not say anything to anybody while he did any of it, and he has never spoken at that table, and nobody has ever taken the silence at that table as being about anything.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet had come down that bank while he was doing it and had gone on past. The turner came along about half an hour later, looked at the top of that table from about nine feet off it, and did not stop turning his wheel, and that is the whole of what anybody said about the cloth.

---

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six had said the four off that wall and got all four of them and laid his hand flat on the stone afterwards, and nobody at the top of that bank had asked him anything about it.

At about ten to eight the boy had said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and had said out loud that he was saying no figure, and then had gone and stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back and waited.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along the ground and the near rail came with it. The man at the end of that table got up, went round the end of it, set his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks, and sat down again. The man of thirty-eight was already at about four feet off the near end of the first table when that was done, and he kept on with his own work while the table was carried past him, and he did not look up at any of it.

---

At the north end of that yard the man who digs loam had the strip open before most of the others were down the bank, and he had the north end to himself all morning.

He opened the strip a foot deeper than he had opened it the day before and set the spade against the side of it and worked down into it a foot at a time. He stopped twice to push the sweat off his face with the back of his wrist. He turned the clod over at the bottom of the cut before he took it out, every time, and he has never once thrown a clod out of that strip, and there is a heap at the north end that has been built up one clod at a time over a period longer than the second table has stood where it stands.

He said nothing out loud to anybody at any point of this morning.

Nobody at those boards said good morning to him. Nobody at those boards has ever asked him what the strip is for, and both of those things have held for the whole of this stretch, and it has never once occurred to anybody down there that the two of them might be connected.

---

She came down that bank at about half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk, and said nothing out loud to anybody all morning.

She had a whole morning to work out whether anything was different, and she could not find any part of it that was.

Two of the men at the second table had come about ten feet further along than they usually came and had stood there a while and had gone back. One of them had said good morning, which he says anyway. She had caught herself twice looking at the near end of the boards to see whether there was anything written there that had not been written there yesterday, and there was not, and there is not a day of the year on which there is not nothing written there that was not written there the day before.

She took the chalk out once and put it back without marking anything. She has decided nothing about it. What she has decided is that she is not going to find out whether anybody heard by waiting to be asked, because nobody in that yard has ever asked anybody anything they could have got by standing still.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and the turner heard it over his own wheel and said nothing. She was at her own scale when he did it and she said nothing then either, and afterwards she could not have told you which of the two numbers of the last two mornings she had heard first, and she did not think about it very hard.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman said a figure out loud at that bank yesterday and nothing has come of it this morning, and that about four of them have not said what a yard is supposed to do with a figure that a person has said out loud, and that about four of them have said nobody asked her anything about it this morning, and that about four of them have not said whether anybody heard it and did not say anything on purpose.

The woman who keeps a scale has a stone in her pocket for weighting the cloth and she has had one for nine years, and it is a stone off the bank and not a stone anybody gave her, and it is the same pocket the chalk is in. She has never once been asked where the stone came from either.

---

At about half past three nobody went near the man at the foot of that low wall, and he was not asked one question. A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone, and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-seventh time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud, once, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and about nineteen people heard it, and the turner's wheel was still going while she did it.

"The two hundred and fifty-eighth morning," she said. "No line goes on the back of that book and the morning goes on it in my hand, and I am telling you the same thing I told you yesterday and the day before that."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line on that page in her own hand and did not take a line for it, and put her pen back in the holder, and the page carried fifty-four lines before her hand went down on it and fifty-four lines after.

---

At about half past four the light went off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not say it and did not go up. The boards came down and the second table stood where it stands and the near rail was on the near side of it.

And the woman who keeps a scale put the cloth over her scale and tied it and carried the basket up that bank. On the way up she had the chalk in her hand and she did not stop to use it.

At the top of the bank the north end of the yard was still open, and the man who digs loam was still in it.

---

Fifty-four lines are on the back of that lot book and none of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. A woman said one figure out loud yesterday and this morning that yard got on with its morning, and two of the men at the second table came ten feet further along and stood there and went back. A man has taken the cloth over the same table twice a morning for longer than most of the men at it have stood at it. The count down that lane did not move and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
