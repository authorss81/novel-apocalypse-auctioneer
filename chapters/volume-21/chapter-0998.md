# Chapter 0998: The Two Hundred And Eighty-Second Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Eleven The Turner Said Out Loud, Once, That A Number Said Across That Yard And A Number Written On The Edge Of A Board Are Two Different Things Going Into The Same Place, And He Did Not Join Them, And Nobody Answered Him And The Page Took No Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-seven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-six. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twelve days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-six days. Eight hundred and eighty-seven days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and one days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and six days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-six days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-nine days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and fifteen days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-five marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-one marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

One thing was said out loud at those boards this morning and it was not a reason and nobody answered it, so that page did not move, and the clerk read that morning out loud and wrote the morning in her own hand and took no line for it. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

It was on the one hundred and eighty-second morning that a reason was said out loud in that yard, and the page at the back of that lot book could not have taken it then, so nothing was written, and it is not counted anywhere and nobody has counted it since.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twelve mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-two mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-two mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-first morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-third of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-seventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-six of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-eighth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-first time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-seven days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-seventh afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He had it worked out in his hands, not in his head, and that was the only place he had ever worked anything out.

The wheel is a big flat thing on a stand that takes a man's height, and you turn it with a treadle under the near end of it, and the sound it makes is the plainest sound in that yard. He has been at it for longer than the near rail has been on the near side of that table and he has never once been asked a thing about it by anybody at that bank.

What comes off it in an afternoon is round. That is the whole of what that wheel is for, and a man who spends his days at it stops being able to hear the shape of the thing he is making and starts hearing only that it is going.

---

It came up out of him about eleven, while the bar he had on was going round slow because he had put his heel down on the treadle instead of the toe of his boot.

He said it once, in the ordinary voice, over his own wheel, in about nineteen people's hearing, and he did not stop turning while he said it and he did not raise his voice to get past it either.

"A number said across this yard and a number written on the edge of a board are two different things going into the same place," he said.

Then he put his heel back down and the wheel came up to the speed it goes at and he said nothing else at all for the rest of that day.

---

Nobody answered him.

The boy was at those boards with the lot book wedged against the rail and did not look over. The man of thirty-eight went along the top of his own table and round the end and up the slope and did not break his route. The man at the far end of the second table had a thing in the fire and did not look up over it.

The man of about thirty-four was at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hand flat on the corner and did not turn his head, and she was about four feet off him at a pair of scales with her hand on the upright and a bar on the pan, and neither of the two of them gave him anything back for it.

Nobody asked him to say it again. Nobody asked him what he meant by the same place. Nobody asked him anything else at all about it.

Nobody thanked him.

He was not the sort of man in that yard that anybody thinks about asking anything, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have each had the sentence he said at that wheel in their head and have not said to each other what to do with it.

---

What he had said had been in him for a stretch of mornings and it had come up out of the part of him that knows what that wheel is for.

He did not go back over it. That is the one thing he has about a sentence of his own that most people in a yard like that do not have, and he has it because the work does not stop while a man is thinking, so a thought has to go out in the time there is between one thing coming round and the next.

At about eleven he had a bar on that wheel that needed about another hour, and he gave it that hour. He took his heel off the treadle and got his boot on it again and let the noise come back up to where it had been, and he did not say a second thing out loud that day, and the bar came off the wheel at about a quarter to twelve and he looked at it and it was not round and he put it back on.

That was his whole afternoon. There is nobody at that wheel to hand the next thing to and nobody at that bank had ever asked him for anything, and he has never in his life asked anybody for a second turn of a thing, and the bar was still not round when the light went off the boards.

---

There is one more thing about that wheel that he has never said out loud and is not going to.

Every figure that has ever gone into that yard has gone over it. He has been standing at the near end of that ground for longer than a good many people at that bank have been coming down that slope, and the air of that yard comes to him the way water comes to a low place, and a figure said at the north end and a figure said at a scale and the number of the man who keeps a tally every morning at half past one have all of them come to him, and every one of them has gone over him and gone on to the bank and stopped there.

He has never turned round. He has never once put a hand on that wheel to stop it going.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off that bank.

The man at the wheel had the whole of that number over his own work and it made no difference to him whatever.

He thought about that, standing on the treadle with his weight on it, and the thought was not about the man who keeps the tally. It was that he had put a number and a line into one mouth that morning for the first time in as long as he had been at that wheel, and that a number had been going across that yard every morning at half past one for longer than he had stood at it, and that he had never once thought about what the air did to it, and that this morning he had thought about it for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall and then had said the result out loud to about nineteen people who had not asked him for it.

Then the bar came round and he stopped thinking and went back to the work.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure.

The man of fifty-six had been up at that wall before there was any light on it and had got all four of them off it.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along the hard ground and the near rail came with it. The man at the end of that table came round the outside of it, got his boot up on the wedge under the leg that rocks, and stood there with his weight on that boot until the two men carrying it had got clear.

The man of thirty-two set his own table up on the hard ground, squared it at the near corner with the back of his heel, and went along the top of it with a cloth.

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down the slope with his hands showing and went to the empty end of his own table and worked out along the top of it and round the end and up the slope and across and back down and in at the same corner, and he said nothing out loud at any point of that day.

She came down at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud to anybody.

At the north end the man who digs loam was in a new place in that strip with the spade going in and coming out clean.

---

By about half past three that sun was over the top of that low wall and lying down along the stones at the foot of it. The man standing in that strip had been in it since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his coat folded beside him.

Good afternoon was said across the four feet between them and one word came back over it, and then neither of them said anything else.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that stone and got all four of them and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and said out loud, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and seventy-first time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And the clerk read that morning out loud once at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account of it out loud before she had her pen to any of it.

"The two hundred and eighty-second morning," she said. "One thing was said out loud at those boards this morning and no line goes on the back of that book for it, so the morning goes on in my hand and nothing else does. That is the account of it, and it went out loud before a mark of it went on that page."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-seven lines before she shut that book and fifty-seven lines after.

---

By about half past four the light had gone off those boards, and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said that afternoon by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stood where they were.

The turner came off that treadle at about half past four and stood at the near end of his own ground with his hands down at his sides and let the wheel stand still, which is the first thing he has done to it every afternoon for longer than there has been a second table in that yard.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man put a figure and a line into one mouth at that wheel this morning and did not join them, and nobody answered him, and the page took no line for it. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
