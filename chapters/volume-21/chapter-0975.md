# Chapter 0975: The Two Hundred And Fifty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And It Was The Seven Hundredth Morning He Had Read Four Figures Off That Wall And Got All Four Both Times, And Nobody Asked Him Anything

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fourteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and three days. Eight hundred and sixty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-two days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and eighteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put one word to the man of fifty-six out loud, and nobody asked the woman who keeps a scale anything about the figure she said yesterday. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

Once, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, a reason was said out loud in that yard, and that was a morning on which the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, so it was not written down and it is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-nine mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-nine mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and fifty-nine mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-eighth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. This is the seven hundredth morning on which the man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall, twice a morning. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fourteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirteen of them, and this is the one hundred and fifteenth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and forty-eighth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines when the light went off it. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-four days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fourth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

Ninety-eight is spent and may not be paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He was at the wall before the boards went up. He had been at that wall since before the middle of the winter, and the wall did not know it and did not care.

The four are cut into the stone one above the other. They are not painted, and they are not chalk, and they have not been filled in, and the top one is the worst of the four because rain has got into it and taken the sharpness off the top left corner, so it has to be read by the shape of what is left and not by the shape of what was cut.

That is the one that can go. Not because he forgets it — he has not forgotten it in seven hundred mornings — but because it is the one a man of fifty-six, on a morning when his chest is not what it was, can get wrong by a stroke. He reads it last. He reads it as the last of four, which means it arrives after three that have already told him what kind of morning his eyes are having.

He got it.

He said them in the order they come on it and he did not hurry the last one to meet the first, and he got all four of them, and then he put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and stood there while the light came across the eleven feet and reached the place his hand was.

He has been coming to that wall long enough that there is a patch of it a shade paler than the stone either side of it, in the shape of a hand. It is not much bigger than his hand. He finds it without looking for it.

Nobody asked him anything. Two of them came down the bank about nine feet behind him and one of them said something about the weather to the other one and neither of them said it to him, and he did not turn round, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched him do it and let him do it. He stood there until the light had come right across the boards and made the ink on the last written line shine.

Not once in that stretch has he looked at what is written at those boards, and not one person up there has ever told him.

---

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and stood at the near end of the first table until somebody at the second table told him to move along, and he moved.

Before nine the second table was carried a foot further along the hard ground by two men who said nothing, and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table put his heel down on the wedge and stood there a while before he sat.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and took his place at about four feet off the near end of the first table, and he said nothing out loud at any point of that morning. The man of about thirty-two put his own table up about four feet along from the second table, squared it with the back of his heel, and took the cloth along the top of it, and the turner's wheel went on turning and said nothing.

At the north end the man who digs loam had the strip open and the spade going in and out of the same place all morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man can stand in a hole at the north end of that yard and take a whole morning over it without anybody at those boards knowing what he is for.

---

She came down that bank at about half past twelve with her scale in a piece of cloth and set it up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud to anybody.

She had noticed, for the first time in a long while, that there is one person in that yard she has never once thought about asking anything, and that it is the man at the wall. Everybody in that yard has something they could be asked about — a table, a strip, a figure, a line on a page, a thing a person has done with their own hands — and he has four numbers in a stone, and that is all, and they are the same four every morning and he has never once been asked where they came from or what they are the numbers of.

She weighed what was next in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board. She looked at the one she had done last longer than she looked at any of the others, and then she put the chalk in her pocket and weighed the next thing without hurrying it.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and the turner heard it over his own wheel and said nothing. She was at her own scale with her hand on the upright and she said nothing either.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall twice a morning for a long stretch of mornings and got all four of them both times, and that about four of them have not said what a thing is like when it is the one thing you are certain of, and that about four of them have said nobody asked him a single thing about any of it, and that about four of them have not said whether anybody has ever wanted to know. She had heard him do it twice that morning without turning round. It is not a loud thing. It is four numbers said by a man who has had as long as she has had to get them right, and if you are standing at a scale with your hands full of iron you take it the way you take the turner's wheel.

---

About half past three the stones had the sun on them and the man at the foot of that low wall had not moved. A man of sixty-one was on the stones four feet off him, sitting down now, with his forearms on his knees. They said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near him.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-eighth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once only and in the ordinary voice, and she read it before she wrote anything at all.

"The two hundred and fifty-ninth morning," she said. "The back of that book stands where it stood yesterday and the morning goes on it in my hand, and I have said that out loud because I say it out loud."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched her do it, and the page carried fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines after she had put her hand flat on it.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four. The goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stayed where they were standing.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank. And the man of fifty-six was still at his wall with his hand flat on the stone when the boards came down and the light had already gone off them.

He was still there a good while after the last of the others had gone over the top of that bank, reading the four to himself under his breath, in order, with the third one given to the worn corner of the top figure.

He got all four of them again.

---

Fifty-four lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. This was the seven hundredth morning on which four figures have been read off that wall and got right, twice, and no line went on that page for it and no one has asked him a single thing about any of the four. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The count down that lane has not moved. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
