# Chapter 0975: The TWO HUNDRED AND FIFTY-NINTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Of Fifty-Six Read The Four Off That Wall Twice And Got All Four Both Times And Nobody Asked Him Anything And The Page At The Back Carries Fifty-Four Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fourteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and three days. Eight hundred and sixty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is.

Five hundred and eighty-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-two days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and thirty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and eighteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. Seven is what a clerk has counted. It stands where it stood. Four documents are missing from this district. Four places can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it.

Nobody asked the man of fifty-six one thing out loud this morning. No reason was said out loud this morning. Nobody said no. So that page did not move. The morning went on in her own hand. The count of things this district does not have is five. It is unpaid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-nine mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and eighty-eight. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-nine mornings and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and fifty-nine, on the word of the man who keeps it. It was said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-eighth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. That is the seven hundredth of those mornings. The lane count is thirty and it stands where it stood. It is not moved by a decision. That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it. It has a column with one name. The heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines. It carries the same figure. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the seven hundredth of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fourteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirteen of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fourth afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and seventy-four days, and the page at the back took no line this morning. That morning was read out loud before anything was written on it. Nothing came down that lane. None of it is in the lot book. None is on the page at the back. The second space there is empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight, said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure this morning, and the yard carried on and nobody at the top of that bank said anything back.

---

---

The boards went up when the light found the eleven feet, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet came down that bank, and the man of fifty-six was already standing at his wall when they were halfway down it. He said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight. He said them in the order they come on it and he did not hurry the last one to meet the first, and he got all four of them. Then he put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and stood there while the light came across the eleven feet and reached the place his hand was. He had been coming to that wall long enough that there is a patch of it a shade paler than the stone either side of it, in the shape of a hand, and he put his hand on that patch without looking for it.

---

Nobody asked him anything. Two of them came down the bank about nine feet behind him and one of them said something about the weather to the other one and neither of them said it to him, and he did not turn round, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched him do it and let him do it, and he stood there until the light had come right across the boards and made the ink on the last written line shine. He has never once read that page and nobody at those boards has ever told him what is on it.

---

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and stood at the near end of the first table until somebody at the second table told him to move along and he moved.

---

Before nine the second table was carried a foot further along the hard ground by two men who said nothing, and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table put his heel down on the wedge and stood there a while before he sat.

---

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing, and took his place at about four feet off the near end of the first table, and said nothing out loud at any point of that morning. The man of about thirty-two put his own table up about four feet along from the second table, squared it with the back of his heel, and took the cloth along the top of it, and the turner's wheel went on turning and said nothing.

---

At the north end the man who digs loam had the strip open and the spade going in and out of the same place all morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man can stand in a hole at the north end of that yard and take a whole morning over it without anybody at those boards knowing what he is doing there.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came down that bank at about half past twelve with her scale in a piece of cloth and set it up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed two things and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud to anybody.

---

Two hundred and eighty-nine mornings, and the board carries what it carries, and the second table was a foot further along this morning than it was yesterday, and every one of those mornings had gone down in a book somewhere with a figure at the end of it that was the same figure as the day before with one added to it. She had heard the boy say the lane count out loud at those boards and had not looked up.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and the turner heard it over his own wheel and said nothing. She was at her own scale with her hand on the upright and she said nothing either.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall twice a morning for a long stretch of mornings and got all four of them both times, and that about four of them have not said what a thing is like when it is the one thing you are certain of, and that about four of them have said nobody asked him a single thing about any of it, and that about four of them have not said whether anybody has ever wanted to know. She had heard him do it twice that morning without turning round. It is not a loud thing. It is four numbers said by a man who has had as long as she has had to get them right, and if you are standing at a scale with your hands full of iron you take it the way you take the turner's wheel.

Most of what is on the edge of that board was put there before the light went off them. She looked at the one she had done last longer than she looked at any of the others, and then she put the chalk in her pocket and weighed the next thing in the basket without hurrying it.

---

About half past three the stones had the sun on them and the man at the foot of that low wall had not moved. A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him. They said good afternoon. A man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and nobody went near him.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-eighth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once only and in the ordinary voice, and she read it before she wrote anything at all.

---

"The two hundred and fifty-ninth morning," she said. "The back of that book stands where it stood yesterday and the morning goes on it in my hand, and I have said that out loud because I say it out loud."

---

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched her do it, and the page carried fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines after she had put her hand flat on it.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four. The goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in. And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, put the basket on her arm, and went up that bank, and the man of fifty-six was still at his wall with his hand flat on the stone when the boards came down and the light had already gone off them.

---

That page carries fifty-four lines. None of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. Seven is what a clerk has counted. Fourteen is what this district has made. Five is what this district does not have. The fifth of the five is unpaid. A line there is not a payment. A gatepost sheet carries four hundred and eleven. By how much, nobody has done. The toll is ninety-eight. It is spent. It may not be raised or paid twice. The low wall's ground is public lot. Since the one hundred and seventeenth afternoon. It is not a payment of that. The bid ran four hundred and fifty days. It was not run. It is not a lot. It will not be. The heading over that name says whoever. The name does not satisfy the rule. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The other space is on different paper. It is empty as well. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-four days. Nobody asked what it is days of. Nobody asked the man of fifty-six one thing. Nobody asked the man of thirty-eight one thing. No reason was said out loud this morning. No line went on that page. A woman has weighed things there nine years. She has not been given anything. She has not asked for anything. He has no bed, no wage, no office. He has asked for none of the three. The lane count is thirty. It did not move. That page is at fifty-four lines. Nobody asked anybody anything about any of it.
