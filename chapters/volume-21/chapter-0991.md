# Chapter 0991: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Four Feet Off The Near End Of The Second Table The Man Of About Thirty-Four Did Not Go Over To Her Scale And Nobody Said Why, And The Page At The Back Of That Lot Book Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and five days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and nineteen days. Eight hundred and eighty days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and eight days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud in this yard this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand and took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and no line went on the back of that lot book for it on that morning because that page was not yet a place a reason could go, and that reason is not counted anywhere and has not been counted since.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and five mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-five mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-five mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-fourth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and sixteenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirtieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-nine of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-first morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-fourth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twentieth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He had it by heart, and he had it by heart because there was nowhere else in that yard to put it down, which was not the same as having written it anywhere.

There was one thing on it. It had been one thing on it since the evening before, when he had gone up that bank and stood at the top of his own steps with his hands on nothing and found that he had put it out into about nineteen people's hearing instead of putting it to a person, and had then lain in the dark for a stretch working out what the morning was going to be like.

He had a list because a man with nothing to do at the top of his own steps will make a list. He had never made one about anything else. There is no paper in this yard that he writes on and he did not go down and get any. He made it in his head at about the time the light comes and he has never once needed to look at it to know what is on it.

He came down the slope a little before the boards were up and squared the second table at the corner nearest the near rail with the back of his heel, once and hard, and took the cloth along the top of it from the far end back to the near end, and then stood at the near end of it with his hand flat on that corner and his other hand down at his side.

Between that corner and the place where her scale goes up there are about four feet of ground. They have been about four feet of ground for longer than he has been standing at that corner.

---

Orren Pike had the boards up at about ten to eight and said the count out loud at them before any hand had moved on anything.

"Thirty," he said. "And no figure after it, because I am saying no figure."

He has said those two sentences at that bank in that voice for longer than the second table has stood on that ground, and nobody at the foot of that bank has ever asked him what a figure is or what he would do with one if he ever had one to do it with. He is seventeen. He hears everything that goes into that yard and he has never once put one of them on a page, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the boy is the only person at those boards who could have made a record of that morning and did not make one.

At that wall the man of fifty-six had been before the light and had got all four of them and had come away from it to let the sun get across the eleven feet.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along the hard ground and the near rail came with it and set down hard enough to shake the legs, and the man at the end of that table stood outside it with his boot up on the wedge under the leg that rocks and left it there. The man of about thirty-two put his own table up, squared it at the near corner with the back of his heel, and went along the top of it with a cloth of his own.

The turner's wheel went on turning at the near end of that ground.

---

He did not go over to her scale.

Nobody said why.

He had the morning in front of him and a thing on the list in him, and the two of them sat in the same place all morning like two men at one table with nothing at all between them.

At about a quarter to eleven he took his hand off the corner and wiped the top of that table where the cloth had not been, which it did not need wiping. At eleven he was standing exactly where he had been standing at about a quarter to eleven. He had a whole hour in front of him and he did the same four things in it that he had done in the hour before, and then the hour was over.

The light came the whole way across the eleven feet, reached the boards, and went along them, and about nine people at the top of that bank were standing on it, and not one of them at that bank looked at him for anything at all.

What he found, and he found it at about half past eleven with his hand on the corner and the cloth hanging from the other one, was that there was nothing in the morning to get ready for. He had come down that slope with a morning to get through, and the morning had turned out to be a table and a cloth and four feet of ground he was not going to cross, and he had not known at seven o'clock how completely those three things would fill the day.

The one thing he had not allowed for, and he stood there and let himself see it, was that nobody was going to come and ask him about it. Not today, not at noon, not at the goodnight. He had built the morning around the idea that a person might come, and the whole of the day the idea had done was take up the room where the morning was.

There is a thing a man can be proud of that has nothing to do with getting anything. He was doing that thing, at that corner, with that cloth, and about nineteen people could see him doing it and not one of them would ever say so, and he had never in his life wanted a person to say so and had never once been in a place where it would have been safe for them to try.

He was not disappointed. He had not gone down that slope expecting anything, because there was no shape for expecting. He was aware, though, standing there at half past eleven with his hand on that corner, of a kind of tiredness in the middle of the day that had nothing to do with his arms, and of not being able to put a name on it, and of letting it stand there without a name on it, which was the hardest part of the three.

---

She came down at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set the scale up about four feet off the near end of that second table, and untied the cloth and folded it over the back of the leg and weighted it with the stone she has carried in that pocket.

She did not say one figure out loud.

There were four things in the basket that morning and the second one was the awkward one and she did it last, the way she has done it for years, and the figures went onto the edge of her own board in chalk in the ordinary way, and she put the chalk away after each one of them.

He went along the top of that table from the near end to the far end and back again. There is no dust on a table a cloth has just been along, and he went along it anyway.

About nine feet off, the man of thirty-eight came out at the empty end of his own table, went round the end, went up the slope, came across, came back down, and went in at the same corner, and the pieces along the near edge of that table stayed in a line the whole of the afternoon.

A pair of them came up the bank about nine feet behind her and stopped to look at the light, and one of them said that there was wind coming over the top of that bank and would be across the boards by four, and the other one said it would not, and it was not said to anybody at that bank.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off that bank and went out flat over the top of it, and nothing at those boards took any of it down.

He stood still until the sound of it was finished and then he went on along the top of that table.

There is a particular quiet that comes into a yard after a number has gone across it, and that yard was in it for a while and then came out of it again, and the whole of what he had to do with the morning was in that, and he did it.

At about two he took the cloth off the top of that table and rolled it once and put it under his arm, and stood at the near end of that table with his hands empty, looking along the eleven feet at nothing, for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall.

Then he took the cloth out again and unrolled it and went back to work.

---

The stones at the foot of that low wall took the sun at about half past three, and the man at the foot of it was standing where he had been standing since about nine. A man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his coat folded beside him on the stones.

"Good afternoon," said the man of sixty-one across the four feet.

"Afternoon," said the man at the foot of the wall, and that was the whole of what either of them said to the other one that afternoon, and it was what they had been saying for the whole of that run.

At four o'clock the man of fifty-six came back to that wall for the second time that day, took the four off it one after another without a pause in them, put the flat of his hand on the stone under the bottom one, and said out loud in the voice he always uses for it that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people had the sound of it. Nobody asked him anything.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud.

She did it once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in the ordinary voice, and she gave the account of it out loud before she put a pen to any part of it.

"The two hundred and seventy-fifth morning," she said. "Nothing has been said out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes on it in my hand and nothing else does. That is the account of it and I have given it out loud before I have written a word of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut that book and fifty-six lines after, and the pen went back into the holder, and the boy put his thumb under the corner of the book to settle it against the rail.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have not said what he said it for, and about four of them have said it up the bank and about four of them have said it down at the boards, and about four of them have not said where they said it from.

He was still at that corner when the light went off the boards.

The goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of the nineteen in it, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stayed where they were standing until the boards had come down off the cart face down.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank, and he went along the top of that table from the near end to the far end once more and squared the corner with the back of his heel, and the table did not move, and then he picked up his cloth and went up the slope about nine feet behind her.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man of about thirty-four did not go over to that scale this morning and nobody said why, and the page did not move, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have not said what he said it for. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
