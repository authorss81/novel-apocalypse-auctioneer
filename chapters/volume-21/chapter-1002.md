# Chapter 1002: The Two Hundred And Eighty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Two Of The Figures In That Yard Stood Near One Another On The Same Morning And Nobody In About Nineteen People Put Them Together

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and forty-one days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and sixteen days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirty days. Eight hundred and ninety-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and ten days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and eighty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and nineteen days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and nothing else went into the air of it that anybody answered, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-seven lines and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand without a line. Two figures in that yard stood near one another on this morning, on two different rows of the same page, and nobody put them in a sentence. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

There was a reason said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and at that time the back of that book was not a place a reason could go, so nothing went on it for that reason, and that reason is counted nowhere now and has not been counted since.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and sixteen mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-six mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-six mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-fifth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-seventh of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and forty-first night of that run, having slept on six hundred and forty of them, and this is the one hundred and forty-second morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-fifth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and one days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirty-first afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The stove at the far end of that table had been going since before the light came over the bank, and by nine it was hot enough that he had to take his coat off over the arms of it and hang it on the rail behind him.

He works at that end because of the stove and because that is where he has worked for nine years, and everything he makes wants the same two things out of him: that it come out sound, and that it come out round.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight. The boy was standing at them with his arms along the top of the rail and the count went out of him before a hand had moved anywhere in that yard, and it is still thirty, and there is still no figure after it, and this morning he put the not-saying into the same breath as the count instead of saying it after, which is the only thing about that boy that changes.

The wall at the far end of the ground had a man at it before there was light enough to see by, and he had his four figures off it and came away from it and stood where he stands on the mornings when nothing else is happening at that end of the yard.

---

A little after nine the second table was carried a foot further along and the near rail went with it, and the man at that end of the table stood with his boot on the wedge until the two men carrying it were clear of him.

The man of about thirty-two had his own table up by then, one corner of it in a mark he had scratched in the dirt with a nail some time back, and he took the cloth along the top of it and back again without hurry. Down the slope came the man of thirty-eight at about half past nine with his hands showing and got to work at about four feet off the near end of the first table.

At the near end of the ground the wheel went on turning, and everything else in that yard went over the top of that sound.

---

He had two hoops on the bench in front of him and the morning's whole work was in them.

The first one he had brought down out of the last bend two days ago, and it was out of round by about the width of a finger at one place only, and the second one he had finished an hour before the light.

He laid the two of them side by side on the top of his bench, end to end, the way you would lay two things down side by side to see whether the pair of them match.

---

The pair of them did not match, and there is nothing in that to look at.

That is the part of his trade nobody outside it believes. He has held two pieces of bent wood in his hands and known they were different while a man with a rule was measuring both of them and getting the same figure on each. The difference is not in the width. The difference is under your hand when you run a finger round the inside of it, and where it turns, and how it feels about turning.

He put a finger in the first one and went round it. He put a finger in the second one and went round that.

The second one was right. It had wanted to come out sound for two days and had needed him to leave it alone longer than he wanted to leave anything alone, and it had come out sound this morning.

He put both of them back in the fire basket on the anvil side and got a third piece of bar out of the rack where it lies.

---

Two of the figures in that yard came within a short distance of one another on this morning. They were on two different rows of the same page and one of them was a round figure with nothing after it and the other stood about thirty days off it, and about nineteen people were standing in that yard while both of them stood there.

Not one of them put the two together. There was nothing in that yard that would have taken a sentence with the two of them in it, the same way there is no bench in that yard to lay two things down side by side on.

He thought about that for about as long as a thing like that takes, and then he stopped thinking about it, because the bar was waiting and it goes cold.

---

She came down the slope at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she weighed what was in it and wrote the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk, and she said nothing out loud to anybody at all that day.

The chalk goes on the edge of that board and never anywhere else. He has looked at that board from about ten feet away, across a table, more times than he could put a figure to, and he has never once walked over and read a line of it.

At the north end the man who digs loam was in the same strip of turned ground and the spade was going in and coming out of one place and then the next place along.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air the way he puts it out every morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet took it up after him out of step with one another, and it came off the bank and went down the slope and was gone.

Nobody at those tables answered it. Nobody has ever answered it. It is a figure that arrives and it is a figure that leaves and in between there is nothing at all.

The wheel went on turning through the whole of that, at the speed it goes at.

---

The light came over the top of the low wall a few minutes after half past three and lay down along the stones at the foot of it.

The man at the foot of that wall was standing in the strip of it because he stands in whatever light that wall puts down. The man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him with his coat folded beside him where he folds it.

"Good afternoon," the one on the stones said.

"Afternoon," said the man at the foot of the wall, and neither of them said anything else, and they had not said anything else to each other in any of it.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall again and had his four off it and got all four of them, and he put his palm flat on the stone under the lowest one and said, out loud, in the voice he uses for that wall:

"That is what these four say, and I could not tell you what they are for."

There were about nineteen people close enough to hear a man say a thing at that bank, and it was the hundred and seventy-fifth time that sentence had gone out into that yard, and not one of them asked him anything about it, and there is nobody in that yard who has asked him anything since before the middle of the winter came on.

---

The clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she gave the account of it out loud before she had the pen near one word of it.

"The two hundred and eighty-sixth morning," she said. "Nothing has been said out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so I put this morning down in my own hand and there is no line in it. That is all of the account of it and it went out loud before any of it was written."

She wrote it under the last line and took no line for it, and the back of that book had fifty-seven lines on it when she shut it and had fifty-seven lines on it after.

---

The turner came off his treadle at about half past four and let the wheel stand, which is the only silence that yard makes in a day.

The goodnight went across about four feet at a time and about four people out of about nineteen said it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet stayed where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He banked the stove, put his coat back on over the arms of it, and went up that bank with the rest of them, and the two hoops were still lying on the top of his bench where he had put them, side by side, one of them sound and one of them out.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book, and it did not move on this morning. Two figures in that yard stood near one another on two different rows of the same page and nobody in about nineteen people put them in a sentence. A man who makes round things laid two hoops side by side on his bench and found by running a finger round the inside of them that the pair of them did not match, and nobody at that bank could have found that out with a rule. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
