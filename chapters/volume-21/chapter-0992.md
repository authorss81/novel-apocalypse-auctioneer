# Chapter 0992: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Figure On One Of The Rows Of That Lot Book Came To Six Hundred Days This Morning, An Exact Hundred With No And In It, And The Turner's Wheel Went On Turning Through It And Nobody At That Bank Looked Up

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-one days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and six days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty days. Eight hundred and eighty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and nine days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-five marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

Nothing said out loud in this yard this morning goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes on it in the clerk's own hand. A figure on one of the rows of that book came to six hundred days this morning, and the turner's wheel went on turning through it and nobody looked up. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

One reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the back of that lot book did not become a place a reason could go until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing was written for it then, and it is not counted anywhere in this district.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and six mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-six mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-six mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-fifth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and seventeenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-first night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-second morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-fifth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-one days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-first afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The hoop had come out right on the second bend and gone out of shape on the third for four days, and the man who makes round things had got it right again twice in that stretch and out of shape again both times, and he had stopped telling anybody about it, which is not the same thing as not telling anybody about it.

He was at the far end of the second table with the small stove going and a piece of iron in the tongs and a tool he has not put down in that yard in fifteen years.

He does not say one word out loud in that yard. Nobody at that bank has ever heard one out of him, including on the mornings when something has gone wrong with him, and the only thing anybody knows about it is that when a thing goes wrong he puts the iron down flat on the stone and stands there with his back to the stove for a while, and the fire does the talking for him.

He is somewhere in his forties and has made round things at that end of that table for fifteen years, which is the same stretch of years the man at the end of that table has been at the other end of it, and neither of them has ever said so out loud, and nobody at that bank has ever put the two of them together.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved on anything.

"Thirty," he said. "And no figure after it, because I am saying no figure."

At that wall the man of fifty-six had been before the light and had got all four of them. Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it and the man at the end of that table put his boot on the wedge and left it there. The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and went to the empty end of his own table and worked his route without breaking it for anything.

At the near end of that ground a wheel on a stand went on turning, and the man at it was about forty-one and had been at it longer than the near rail had been on the near side of that table, and the sound it makes is the plainest sound in that yard.

---

About half way through that morning he put the toe of his boot on the treadle and held it there and let the wheel come round slow, and he did it for a piece long enough that anybody standing near him would have noticed, and nobody standing near him noticed, and then he took his foot off and it came back up to the speed it goes at.

There is a thing you can do with a wheel like that. You can take the noise down a bit for as long as you like and put it back afterwards, and the sound goes on coming from the same place either way, and the only difference is in what the man at the wheel has in his ears, and in what he has in his ears it is that he can hear the whole of the yard behind the noise of his own work.

He had done that on a good many mornings. It was not a practice and it was not a policy and nobody in that yard had ever asked him about it, and there was nobody in that yard he would have told.

What he heard at about ten to eleven on the morning in question, with it going slow, was the boy at the boards counting nothing in particular under his breath, and the far end of the table with the fire in it, and a pair of people nine feet off the boards talking about something with a handle on it, and the man of thirty-four wiping a table that did not need wiping.

And under all of that, going across that yard and coming back off the bank at about half past one, was the number of the man who keeps a tally, and about four voices after it out of step with each other.

He let it come back up to speed.

There is a thing about a wheel that a man who has done it for twenty years knows without ever having been told, and it is that the wheel does not care what anybody in the yard is doing. It will go on at the same speed for a person saying a thing and for a person saying nothing. It went on at the same speed through that morning, and through the morning before it, and through the two hundred and some mornings before that, and it will go on at the same speed this afternoon when the boy is putting that book away and the light has gone off the boards.

Nobody at that bank looked up while it was slow, and nobody at that bank would have known that it had been slow, and there is nothing in that yard that keeps any count of how often that wheel changes.

---

The man of about thirty-four was at about four feet off the near end of the second table all morning with his hand flat on the corner and his cloth in the other hand, and he did not go over to the scale that stands about four feet off the other end of that same table, and nobody at that bank asked him about it, and he said nothing out loud to anybody at any point of it.

He is not the only man in that yard carrying a thing he does not do, and he has never once been the sort of man the others at that bank think about asking, because he is at the near end of a table and keeps his head down.

The man at the wheel knew he was there. That wheel carries everything that happens within about nine feet of it, and a man who stands still and does not speak is easier to hear than a man who moves.

---

The man who makes round things put the hoop down flat on the stone at about two and looked at it along its length from the edge, the way a person looks along a line and not at a heap.

It had come out right.

He picked it up and put the edge of his thumb on the third bend and went along it with the thumb, and then he set it down and picked up the tongs and went back to the fire and did not say one word to anybody in that yard.

It had come out right the way a thing comes out right when the fire is a shade less than it was and the iron has been at it a shade longer than it wants to be, and he had got it by leaving it, which is not a thing he could have taught anybody.

He worked the rest of that afternoon with the fire down and the door of that stove open and his hands slow, and at about half past three he put the hoop over the top of a nail in a post to see whether it would lie flat, and it would, and he left it there and it was still there when the light went off those boards.

---

At about half past two he put the heel down on the treadle again and let it go slow a second time and looked along the ground while it did.

A man of thirty-four was at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his cloth going along the top of it and back. A woman was at a scale about four feet off him with her hand on the upright. The boy was at the boards. The man of thirty-eight was going up a slope with his hands showing and would come across and come back down and go in at the same corner, and there were two people at the boards talking about something with a handle on it.

That is all of that yard. It is about a hundred feet from one end of it to the other with a bank at the back of it, and everything that has happened in it since it was a yard has happened in that hundred feet, and he had about eleven feet of ground of his own at the near end of it.

He took his heel off and the noise came back.

By about half past three the sun had come over that low wall and was lying along the stones at the foot of it, and the man standing in it had not moved out of it since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting on the stones about four feet off him with his forearms across his knees. They said good afternoon to each other and he said afternoon back and neither of them said one word after that.

At about four o'clock that man of fifty-six took the four figures off that wall for the second time that day, got all four of them, laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one, and said in the ordinary voice that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people had that for the hundred and sixty-fifth time running, and not one of them asked him anything.

And the clerk read that morning out loud once at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account of it out loud before she put a pen to it, and then wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut the book and fifty-six lines after.

---

The light went off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank stayed on the bank.

The woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that slope. The man who makes round things put his fire out in that stove and went up behind her with the tongs in his hand, because there is nowhere at the top of that bank to keep them.

And the man at the wheel came off his treadle at about half past four and stood at the near end of his own ground with his hands down at his sides and let it stand still, which is the first thing he has done to it every afternoon for longer than there has been a second table in that yard.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A figure on one of the rows of that book stood at six hundred days this morning, and the wheel went on turning through it and nobody at that bank looked up. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
