# Chapter 0972: The Two Hundred And Fifty-Sixth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Number Went Out Into Four Hundred Yards Of Air And Nobody Said A Figure Off Her Own Board

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eleven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred days. Eight hundred and sixty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and eighty-nine days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and twenty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and fifteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and the five things this district does not have are five and unpaid.

Nobody put one word to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning. No reason was said out loud and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

One reason was said out loud in that yard, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and that was before the back of that lot book was a place any reason could go, and it went nowhere then and is not counted anywhere now, and the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in that yard has never been given out loud either.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-six mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-six mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has been at the foot of that bank two hundred and fifty-six mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-fifth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the six hundred and ninety-seventh of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eleventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ten of them, and this is the one hundred and twelfth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and forty-fifth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it under a heading that still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty, and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move. It is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines when the clerk shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-one days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and first afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

Ninety-eight is the toll and it is spent. Three of the first thirty-three lines on that page were never read by anybody in this district.

---

She was at the foot of the bank before the boards went up, with the cloth still knotted round the scale and the board under her arm, and she had been standing there long enough that the dew had dried on the back of the board.

There is a thing about being the first thing in a yard. After nine years of it she no longer minded the cold, or the waiting, or the walk down with the basket. What she minded, and had minded for a long time, was the arithmetic of the morning. Everything in that yard that mattered was written down twice: once where anybody could read it, and once where nobody ever would.

She had never said that out loud to anybody either.

The boards went up before the second table was out of the way of them. At the wall, the man of fifty-six said the four off the stone and got all four of them, and put his hand flat under the bottom one and left it there while he looked at the four again, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything about any of it.

At about ten to eight the boy called the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and told the boards straight after it that no figure was going with it this morning, and then went and stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back and looked at the ground until the boards were not new to him any more.

About nine, two men who did not speak to each other while they did it carried the second table a foot further along the hard ground. The near rail came with it and set down hard enough to put a small jump in the near end of it. The man at the end of that table set his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks and sat down again.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table with his back to the boards. Nothing was said to him and he said nothing, and he has not asked that yard for anything in any stretch of mornings anybody there can remember.

The turner's wheel went on turning from the time he started it, a sound so ordinary that people stopped hearing it the way they stop hearing the wind. The man of about thirty-two put his own table up about four feet along from the second table, squared it with the back of his heel, and took the cloth along the top of it and said nothing. At the far end of the second table the man who makes round things set a thing he had turned down on end, looked at it, turned it over, and set it down the other way up.

---

She came down the slope again at about half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set the scale up about four feet off the near end of the second table.

She untied the knot and folded the cloth over the back of the leg and weighted it with the stone out of her pocket. Then she weighed what was at the top of the basket, in order, and put each figure on the edge of her own board in chalk, low down, in the gap left at the end of the last one.

She put the chalk in her pocket and took it out again twice while she was standing there, and both times she put it back without marking anything.

There is a clean stretch of wood at the end of that board, about the length of her hand, where the figures run out and the chalk has never been. Nine years is a long time to keep a figure to yourself. She had counted some of them along her own forearm once, in a winter, with the tip of the chalk, and had got to a number and stopped, and she has never told anybody what the number was and she has never written it on the board.

She weighed what was left in the basket and put those figures on too. She said nothing to anybody.

---

At about half past one the man who keeps a tally stood where he stands and put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it.

About four people said it after him, out of step with each other and none of them at the same moment as any other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank a half beat late each time. The turner heard it over his own wheel and did not stop turning, and the man next to him on the second table went on with what he was doing and did not look up.

Nobody wrote it down. There was a boy at those boards who hears every number that goes into the air in that yard, and what he did with the number the man of fifty-six said was to read four figures off a wall twice and get them right, and what he did with the number the man of forty-eight said was to say the lane count out loud and then tell the boards that no figure was going with it.

She was at her own scale with her hand flat on the upright. She did not turn round, and she said nothing, and there was nobody near enough to have heard her if she had.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that nobody in that yard said a figure off her own board out loud this morning, and that about four of them have not said whether a woman has to be asked before a figure of hers can be said, and that about four of them have said the boy said one this morning at those boards, and that about four of them have not said what anybody in that yard does with a figure once it is out of a mouth.

The boy has been at those boards long enough to know what is written on them and long enough not to have asked about it. He says the lane count out loud because that is the thing at those boards that gets said out loud, and he says out loud that he is saying no figure because a boy of seventeen who says a figure and leaves nothing after it is a boy who has said a figure. He does not know whether that is right. He has decided it is worth the risk either way.

---

He had put his elbows on the boards and his chin on his hands when the woman who keeps a scale came down the slope at about half past twelve.

He watches her the whole way. Not the way a boy watches a woman, the way he watches the boards: he knows when she sets the scale up, he knows the half minute she stands with her hand on the upright before anything goes on it, he knows which end of her own board she starts at, and he knows that she writes the figures low down.

Orren Pike has never asked her a single thing about any of that. He has never asked the man of fifty-six a single thing about the four on the wall, either, and there is a stretch of mornings in which the two of them were the only people in that yard who had said nothing to anybody at all, and neither of them knew the other one was doing it.

That morning she took the chalk out of her pocket twice in about a quarter of an hour without marking anything, and Orren Pike saw both of those, and he wrote nothing down, because there is a place on those boards for a number brought down that lane in somebody's own hand and there is no place on those boards for a woman standing at a scale with a bit of chalk, and he is seventeen and he has been at those boards long enough to know the difference between the two.

---

At about half past three the sun was on the stones at the foot of that low wall and about four feet of it was in shadow by then, and the two men on those stones said good afternoon to each other, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and nothing else. Nobody went near him. He was not asked one question.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-fifth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud, once, in the ordinary voice, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and about nineteen people at the foot of that bank heard every word of it, and it was read out before she put her pen into it.

"The two hundred and fifty-sixth morning," she said. "There is no line going on the back of that book today and the morning goes on in my hand, and I have said that out loud so that nobody has to go and look."

Then she wrote the morning in her own hand under the last line on that page and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-four lines before her hand went down on it and fifty-four lines after.

---

About half past four the light came off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard went off them with it, said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of eleven feet stayed where they were for a while afterwards and then went up. The boards came down and the second table stood where it stands.

And then the woman who keeps a scale put the last thing she had out of the basket on the scale by itself. She weighed it. She wrote the figure on the edge of her own board in the clean stretch at the end of it, where the chalk had never been, and she put the cloth over the scale and knotted it and went up that bank.

She did not say it.

---

Fifty-four lines are on the back of that lot book this morning and not one of them is in the lot book itself. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. The name in the column does not satisfy the rule and the heading over it still says whoever. A woman has weighed things at that bank for nine years and has kept every figure of her own to herself. The count down that lane has not moved and the fifth of the five is unpaid. Nobody asked anybody anything about any of it.
