# Chapter 0972: The TWO HUNDRED AND FIFTY-SIXTH Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month The Man Who Keeps A Tally Said His Number At About Half Past One And Four People Repeated It Out Of Step With Each Other And The Woman At Her Own Scale Said Nothing This Morning And The Page At The Back Carries Fifty-Four Lines And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and eleven days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-six days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred days. Eight hundred and sixty-one days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-five days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is.

Five hundred and eighty days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month. The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-three days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and eighty-nine days past a printing it did not make.

Five hundred and twenty-nine marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and fifteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table. Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. Four documents are not owned here. Four places can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it.

Nobody put one word to the man of fifty-six out loud this morning. No reason was said out loud this morning. Nobody said no. So that page did not move. The morning went on in her own hand. Five is what this district does not have, and it is not paid.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning and it was before that page was a place a reason could go, and no line on that page was written for a reason before the two hundred and eighth morning because that page was not a place a reason could go before that morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason was said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-six mornings and moved this morning from two hundred and eighty-five. The near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-six mornings and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. The number of mornings a man has been at the foot of that bank is two hundred and fifty-six, on the word of the man who keeps it. It was said before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-fifth morning and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. That is the six hundred and ninety-seventh of those mornings. The lane count is thirty, and it did not move. It is not moved by a decision. That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it. It has a column with one name. The heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines. It did not carry a fifty-fifth. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four and got them both times, and that is the six hundred and ninety-seventh of those mornings, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet heard him, and nobody asked him anything out loud this morning.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and eleventh night of that run, having slept on six hundred and ten of them, and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and first afternoon running, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that wall and was not asked one question this morning, and about nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning, and there are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert, and neither of those two distances is what anybody in that yard thinks about on a morning.

The record has stood one hundred and seventy-one days, and the page at the back took no line this morning. She read that morning out loud before she wrote it. Nothing came down that lane. None of it is in the lot book. None is on the page at the back. The second space there is empty.

Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards at about ten to eight, said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure this morning, and nobody said anything back to him.

---

---

The boards went up before the second table was out of the way of them. The woman who keeps a scale was already standing at the foot of that bank with her scale beside her and the cloth still knotted round it, and she had been there long enough that the dew had dried on the back of the board she carries.

At about a quarter to eight the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and left it there while he looked at the four again, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything about any of it.

---

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and then said no figure and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and went to the near end of the first table and stood there looking down the ground.

---

About nine the second table was carried a foot further along by two men who did not speak to each other while they did it, and the near rail came with it and set down hard, and the man at the end of that table set his own boot on the wedge and sat down again.

---

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table with his back to the boards. Nothing was said to him and he said nothing, and he has not asked that yard for anything in any stretch of mornings anybody here can remember.

---

The turner's wheel went on turning from the time he started it, and the man of about thirty-two put his own table up about four feet along from the second table and squared it with the back of his heel and took the cloth along the top of it and said nothing. At the far end of the second table the man who makes round things set a thing he had turned down on end and looked at it and turned it over and set it down again the other way up.

---

The woman who keeps a scale came down the slope at about half past twelve carrying the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and untied the knot and folded the cloth over the back of the leg and weighted it. She weighed three things in a row. She wrote each figure on the edge of her own board in chalk, low down, in the gap left at the end of the last one, and she said nothing to anybody.

---

She had put the chalk in her pocket and taken it out again twice while she was standing there, and both times she put it back without marking anything. There is a place at the end of that board where the figures run out and there is a clean stretch of wood about the length of her hand, and it was still clean when the light went off it. Nine years is a long time to keep a figure to yourself. She had counted them on her own forearm once, in a winter, with the tip of the chalk, and got to a number and stopped, and she has never told anybody what the number was and she has never written it on the board.

---

At about half past one the man who keeps a tally stood where he stands and put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it. Four people said it after him, none of them at the same time as any other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank. She was at her own scale with her hand flat on the upright when he did it, and she did not turn round, and she did not say anything, and there was nobody near enough to have heard her if she had.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that nobody in that yard said a figure off her own board out loud this morning, and that about four of them have not said whether a woman has to be asked before a figure of hers can be said, and that about four of them have said the boy said one this morning at those boards, and that about four of them have not said what anybody in that yard does with a figure once it is out of a mouth.

The boy has been at those boards long enough to know what is written on them and long enough not to have asked about it. He says the lane count out loud because that is the thing at those boards that gets said out loud, and he says out loud that he is saying no figure because a boy of seventeen who says a figure with nothing after it is a boy who has said a figure.

---

About half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones four feet off him. They said good afternoon. A man of sixty-one said good afternoon. He said nothing else. Nobody went near him. He was not asked one question.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-fifth time running, and nobody asked him anything. He has been coming to that wall long enough that there is a place on the stone where his hand goes, and the stone is a different colour there than it is either side of that place.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice, and about nineteen people at the foot of that bank heard every word of it, and it was read out before she put her pen into it.

---

"The two hundred and fifty-sixth morning," she said. "There is no line going on the back of that book today and the morning goes on in my hand, and I have said that out loud so that nobody has to go and look."

---

Then she wrote the morning in her own hand under the last line on that page and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-four lines before her hand went down on it and fifty-four lines after.

---

About half past four the light came off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard went off them with it, said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of eleven feet stayed where they were a while. The boards came down and the second table stood where it stands.

---

And then the woman who keeps a scale put the last thing she had out of the basket on the scale by itself, weighed it, and wrote the figure on the edge of her own board in the clean stretch at the end of it, and put the cloth over the scale and knotted it and went up that bank. She did not say it.

---

That page carries fifty-four lines. None of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. Seven is what a clerk has counted. Fourteen is what this district has made. Five is what this district does not have. The fifth of the five is unpaid. A line there is not a payment. A gatepost sheet carries four hundred and eleven. By how much, nobody has done. The toll is ninety-eight. It is spent. It may not be raised or paid twice. The low wall's ground is public lot. Since the one hundred and seventeenth afternoon. It is not a payment of that. The bid ran four hundred and fifty days. It was not run. It is not a lot. It will not be. The heading over that name says whoever. The name does not satisfy the rule. The second space on that form is empty. Nobody has asked for it to be filled. The other space is on different paper. It is empty as well. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-one days. Nobody asked what it is days of. Nobody asked the man of fifty-six one thing. Nobody asked the man of thirty-eight one thing. No reason was said out loud this morning. No line went on that page. A woman has weighed things there nine years. She has not been given anything. She has not asked for anything. He has no bed, no wage, no office. He has asked for none of the three. The lane count is thirty. It did not move. That page is at fifty-four lines. Nobody asked anybody anything about any of it.
