# Chapter 0995: The Two Hundred And Seventy-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At The North End The Man Who Digs Loam Said A Figure Out Loud, Once, And It Was Not His, And The Clerk Read That Morning Out Loud And Wrote Nothing And Nobody Answered Him

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and nine days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-three days. Eight hundred and eighty-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and ninety-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twelve days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and thirty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

A figure went out loud at the north end of this yard this morning and it was not that man's, and no reason was said out loud in this yard, so that page did not move, and the clerk read that morning out loud and wrote the morning in her own hand and took no line for it. Nobody answered him. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

The one hundred and eighty-second morning was a morning on which a person said a reason out loud in that yard, and that book was not yet a place a reason could go, so it was not written down, and no count of it exists anywhere.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and nine mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and fifty-nine mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and seventy-nine mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and seventy-eighth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twentieth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-fourth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-three of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-fifth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and sixty-eighth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-four days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-fourth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The spade had gone into the same place in that strip four days running and it had stopped going in.

He could get the blade in to the depth of the hand and then it wanted its own way, and the ground at the bottom of the cut had begun to come up into his boot before the blade was down, which is the thing that happens to loam that has been turned over in one wet stretch too often.

He stood at the north end with the spade in both hands and looked at that cut for a while.

What came out of him was a number.

---

It was not his number. He had not come down that bank that morning with any idea of saying anything, and he had not said a figure out loud in that yard in any morning of his life, and the number that came out was a number that was in his head because a man who does the same piece of ground every day for long enough ends up with the length of it in him without ever having asked for it.

He said it out loud, once, in the ordinary voice, about as far as the air goes at that end of that yard, and the wind took about half of it.

There were people behind him. There had been people at that bank since before the boards went up. He did not turn round.

---

Nobody answered him.

The wheel at the near end of that ground kept on at the speed it goes at. The man of thirty-eight came down the slope and went in at his own corner and worked his route. The boy was at the boards with the lot book wedged against the rail and did not look up the bank. Nobody at that bank said one word about it, and nobody asked him where the number came from, and nobody asked him what it was a number of, and nobody asked him to say it again.

The man at the wheel had all of it over his own work and it made no difference to him at all.

The man of about thirty-four was at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hand on the corner and did not turn his head. The woman at the scale about four feet off him had the chalk in her hand and did not turn round either. And the man of fifty-six, who had been at that wall before the light and got all four of them, was standing at the near end of the first table with his arms down at his sides and had the whole of it too.

---

The clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, in the ordinary voice, and she gave the account out loud before she put her pen to anything.

"The two hundred and seventy-ninth morning," she said. "Something was said out loud at the north end of this yard this morning and no line goes on the back of that book for it, so the morning goes on in my hand and nothing else does. That is the account of it and I have given it out loud before I have written any of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut that book and fifty-six lines after, and the boy put his thumb under the corner of it to settle it against the rail.

---

What he could not get past, for the rest of that day, was the speed of it.

He had had a question in that yard for a good many mornings. He had had it at that bank and he had put it to a woman at those boards and been given one word back, and it had gone nowhere out loud like everything else anybody says there, and he had gone back to the north end with it and got on with the ground.

This was different and he could not have told anybody how it was different, because it was not a question and it had not been built like one and it had not been worn round itself the way a question gets when it has sat on a person for years. It had come out of him the way a number comes out of a man when he has been standing over the same piece of ground for four days and his body has it.

And about four hundred yards of that yard had heard it, and the yard had done with it what the yard does with everything anybody puts into it.

He went back into the cut at about two and put the spade in where it had been, and it would not go, and he tried it in another place and it went in as though the ground had been made for it, and he worked there for the rest of the afternoon and the blade came up clean every time.

There was a word for what the woman at those boards had given him and it was not a long word and it is not this record's to print, and it had been sitting in his head for a good many mornings doing nothing whatever, and the number that had come out of him at the north end that morning had not been waiting behind it. It had come out of the same place a spade comes out of.

That was the part he could not get past, when he thought about it at about half past three with the sun coming over the low wall and a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off the man in the strip.

He had come to that bank a long time ago with a question in him about where figures go when they go out loud, and he had got it down to the shortest shape it had and had put it out loud into a yard, and a woman at a board had given him one word and would not give him a second one and nobody had ever asked her for one.

And this morning, at the north end, without asking anybody anything, he had said a number of his own out loud into about four hundred yards of air, and the yard had taken it exactly the way it had taken the question.

Which is either an answer or nothing at all, and he had no way of telling which, and he had nobody to put it to.

---

The rest of that yard went on the way it goes.

The boards were up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure.

At that wall the man of fifty-six had been before the light. Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table got his boot up on the wedge under the leg that rocks. The man of thirty-two put his own table up and squared it and went along the top of it with a cloth.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table from the empty end, and said nothing out loud at any point of it.

The wheel at the near end of that ground did not miss a beat through any of it.

She came down at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk, and she said nothing out loud to anybody that morning.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off that bank and went out flat, and nothing at those boards took it down.

At about two Orren Pike came down with his hands empty and stood at the near end of the first table and looked at the north end of that yard for a while, and then went back up the slope, and nobody stopped him and he said nothing.

---

By about half past three the sun had got over that low wall and was lying along the stones at the foot of it, and the man there had not moved out of it since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him on those stones. Good afternoon went across the four feet between them and one word came back, and neither of them said anything after that.

At about four o'clock that man of fifty-six took the four off that wall for the second time that day, got all four of them, put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and said in the ordinary voice that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people had that for the hundred and sixty-eighth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank, and the man who digs loam went up after her with the spade over his shoulder and the blade of it washed and the handle wet through, and he had not said another word out loud in that yard after the one he had said at the north end that afternoon.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A figure went out loud at the north end of that yard this morning, once, and it was not that man's, and nobody answered him, and the clerk read that morning out loud and wrote the morning and took no line for it. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
