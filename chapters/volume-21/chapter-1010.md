# Chapter 1010: The Two Hundred And Ninety-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Half Past One She Said One Figure Of Her Own Out Loud Once At Her Own Scale In About Nineteen People's Hearing And Nobody Answered Her And Nobody Thanked Her And Nobody Asked Her What She Weighs Things For And The Page At The Back Of That Lot Book Did Not Move And Did Not Carry A Sixtieth Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and forty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty-four days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirty-eight days. Eight hundred and ninety-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and thirteen days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and eighteen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and eighty-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and ninety-one days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twenty-seven days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and sixty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and fifty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

At about half past one this morning she said one figure of her own out loud, once, at her own scale, in about nineteen people's hearing, and nobody answered her, nobody thanked her and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and no reason was said out loud by anybody in that yard, so the page at the back of that lot book stood at fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-nine lines when she shut it and did not carry a sixtieth line. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

One reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line was written for it on that morning, and it is counted nowhere now and is not going to be counted.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty-four mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy-four mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-four mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and ninety-third morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirty-fifth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and forty-ninth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and forty-eight of them, and this is the one hundred and fiftieth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and eighty-third time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and nine days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirty-ninth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

Nine years is a thing you can put your hand out flat on.

She has done it that way twice today, without meaning to, once at the top of that bank with the basket on her arm and once here at about four feet off the near end of the second table with the cloth off her own scale. Her hand went out flat and it covered about a foot of air and she thought: that is the whole of the nine years. Not one of them is any thicker than the rest.

---

She came down that bank at half past twelve the way she comes down it, with the basket on her arm and the chalk in the pocket of her apron and no reason at all in her head.

That last part was new. On the mornings when she has said a figure out loud in that yard she has come down that slope with the thing arranged in her head, and she has arranged it on the way down, and the arranging has been what the morning was for.

Today she had not arranged anything. The wind came over the top of the bank about eleven and she had been at the top of it with her back to it getting her hair out of her face.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the count came out loud at them off that boy before anybody in that yard had moved a hand, and it is thirty, and this morning he put the two halves of it together in one breath, which he has been doing lately.

Thirty. And that is all of it this morning.

The man of fifty-six had his four off that wall before there was light on it and got all four of them.

---

The yard did what it does.

A wheel went round at the near end of that ground at the speed it goes at and did not change and did not stop. A man came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and went out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the bank and back down and in at the same corner, and he said nothing out loud at any point of that day, and nobody in that yard asked him anything at any point of that day.

At the far end of the second table a man had his stove going. Just after nine the second table was carried a foot further along and the near rail came with it and the man at the end of that table stood with his boot on a wedge until the men carrying it were past.

At about four feet off the near end of that table the man of about thirty-four stood with his cloth over his hand and looked at the corner of it.

---

At half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the ordinary voice.

About four people said it after him out of step with one another and it came back off the top of that bank, and that is a thing that yard has done every morning for longer than she has been standing at it.

She waited for that one to be finished the way a person waits for a cart to get past a gate.

---

Then she put a bar on her own pan.

The pan wanted to go, because the wind wanted it to go, and she stood there and let it want to go, and it came still, and the figure was the figure it always is for that thing, and she said it out loud.

She said one figure, once, in the ordinary voice, at her own scale, in about nineteen people's hearing.

She did not say it louder than the man with the tally says his. She did not say it twice. She did not put her head round to see whether anybody had heard it.

---

Nobody answered her.

Nobody asked her what she weighs things for. Nobody asked her where the figure came from. Nobody said thank you to her and nobody said one word of anything at all, and the wheel went on turning through the whole of it, and a man with a cloth in his hand forty feet away went on with his cloth.

She wrote the figure on the edge of her own board in chalk, in the gap she leaves at the end of each one, and she went to the basket for the next thing.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that they have not said what the difference is.

---

The afternoon of that morning went on in the ordinary way and she was in the middle of it the way she has been in the middle of an afternoon for nine years.

The light came over the top of the low wall a little after half past three and lay down along the stones at the foot of it. The man standing in that strip of it had been standing in it since about nine. The man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones with his coat folded beside him.

"Good afternoon," the one on the stones said.

"Afternoon," said the man at the foot of the wall, and neither of them said anything else.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall and had his four figures off it and got all four of them and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one and left it lying there.

"There is nobody in this yard could tell you what these four are for," he said out loud to that stone.

About nineteen people heard that sentence for the hundred and eighty-third time running. Nobody asked him anything. About four people at the top of eleven feet had not asked him a question about any part of it since before the middle of the winter came on.

---

The clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account of it out loud before she had the pen near any part of it, as she has done for a long run of mornings.

"The two hundred and ninety-fourth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my hand and there is no line in it, and that is the whole of the account of it and it went out loud before the writing of it."

She wrote the two hundred and ninety-fourth morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it.

Fifty-nine lines were on that page when she shut the book. Fifty-nine lines were on it after.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in and stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

She knotted the cloth over her scale and put the basket on her arm.

Then she put the chalk in the pocket of her apron, where it lives, and stood there at about four feet off the near end of the second table while about nineteen people went up that bank without her.

---

She has gone up that bank at the end of that day for nine years. There has never once been a reason to stand at the foot of it after the others have gone.

She stood there until the yard was empty.

---

The board was where it stands. The chalk came out of the pocket and she took the bar she had weighed last and set it on the pan and let the pan come still and read the figure off it, and she wrote it on the edge of her own board in chalk, low down, in the gap at the end of the last one.

She did not say it out loud.

There was nobody in that yard to say it to. There had been about nineteen people in that yard four hours ago and now there was a wheel standing still at the near end of the ground and a low wall with nothing on it and a man at the foot of that wall who does not go up that bank at all.

---

She put the chalk back in the pocket of her apron.

She tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank, and the figure on the edge of her own board was still wet enough to take a thumb if anybody in that yard came back down for it, and nobody came back down.

That figure is hers. It is not in the lot book and it is not on the page at the back of that lot book and it has not gone down that lane and it is not on a wall, and nobody has been told what it weighs and nobody in that basin will be told.

---

Fifty-nine lines are on that page and not one of the fifty-nine is in the lot book, and it did not move on this morning and did not carry a sixtieth line. At about half past one she said one figure of her own out loud, once, at her own scale, in about nineteen people's hearing, and nobody answered her and nobody thanked her and nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and after the last of the others had gone up that bank she chalked one more figure on the edge of her own board and did not say that one, and that figure is not in the lot book and not on that page and not down that lane and not on a wall. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. The sheet at that gatepost carries the figure it carries and its size of error is uncomputed, unexplained and unpaid.
