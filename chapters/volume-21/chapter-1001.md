# Chapter 1001: The Two Hundred And Eighty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Two Hundred Days Went By On A Figure At The Back Of That Lot Book And Nobody In That Yard Asked What It Is The Two Hundredth Of

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and forty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fifteen days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-nine days. Eight hundred and ninety days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and eighteen days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud in that yard this morning and nobody said no and nobody asked anybody anything at all, so the page at the back of that lot book did not move and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand and took no line. The record came to two hundred days on this morning and nobody in that yard asked what it is the two hundredth of. The lane count is thirty, it did not move, and nothing came down that lane. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard once, on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, so it was not written down then, and it is counted nowhere in this district, and no figure has been said out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fifteen mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-five mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-five mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-fourth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fortieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-nine of them, and this is the one hundred and forty-first morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-fourth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirtieth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He squared the corner at ten with the heel of his hand, which is not what he does, and then looked at the corner he had just moved and put it back where it had been with his boot.

There is a thing a man does at the end of a job when he has not decided what to do with his hands, and he had been doing it since a morning eleven mornings back, and this morning he found he was doing it a quarter of an hour before the job was finished.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the boy was at them with his elbows on the top rail and the lot book wedged against the near rail where it lives, and he said the count out loud to nobody in particular, which is thirty, and then he told that yard in so many words that there was no figure coming after it.

"You get thirty," he said. "And I'm not saying anything after it."

Nobody asked him for anything after it. That has been the shape of that boy's morning for longer than the second table has stood on that ground.

At the far end of the same yard the man of fifty-six had a wall to himself before there was any light on it, and he had his four off it and came away from it and stood at the near end of the first table for as long as a thing like that takes, and went back.

---

The second table went a foot further along the hard ground a little after nine, and the near rail came with it because the rail goes where the table goes, and the man at the end of that table came round the outside of it and got his boot up on the wedge under the leg that rocks and left the weight of the morning on that boot.

A man of about thirty-two set his own table up on ground that would hold it, put a corner of it straight against a mark he had left in the dirt with the heel of his hand on some morning in the past, and ran the flat of his cloth along the top from that end to the other.

Down the slope at about half past nine came the man of thirty-eight with his hands showing, and he went to the empty end of his own table and started out along the line of pieces on it and round the end of it and up the slope and back, the way he has gone every morning of that run.

---

She came down that bank at half past twelve with the basket on her arm, and she set her scale up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she put a bar on the pan and waited for it, and she wrote the figure on the edge of her own board in chalk.

He watched her do that from where he stood with his cloth over his hand.

He has watched her do it for the whole nine years she has stood at that bank. It is the only piece of writing that happens in that yard in a morning that belongs to the person who made it.

---

What he had been carrying since the morning he said his sentence out loud was not a plan.

That is the honest shape of it. There was no list with an order to it and there was nothing on it that could be done and there was no hour marked on it. There was a sentence he had said at about eleven in the ordinary voice, and it was out in the air of that yard, and about four people at the top of eleven feet had taken it in, and then nothing had come back down to him out of the air at all.

He had expected something. That was the whole of his expectation, and he had not admitted to any of them that he had expected something.

---

By about one he had worked out something he had not been working on.

He had been keeping a count. Not a written one — he never has written anything down in that yard and there is no paper at that end of the second table. A count he keeps the way a man keeps the number of times he has crossed a road, without deciding to.

Somewhere in the middle of that morning the figure he had been carrying came round onto a number that has a shape to it, a number a man could say out loud and be heard saying something.

He stood at his corner with the cloth hanging off his hand and worked out what would happen if he said it.

---

What would happen was that about nineteen people would hear a sentence out of him about how long a table had stood in one place, and there would be no way on earth for anybody in that yard to know what to do with it.

There is a kind of sentence that comes out of a yard. He had heard one go on the morning he had stood there and watched the wind take it. He has never in his life heard anybody at that bank say how long anything had stood, or what anything was the second of, or what the hundredth of anything was, and it was not that anybody had forbidden it.

It was that the yard did not have anywhere to put a sentence of that kind, and neither did he.

He put his hand flat on the corner of the table, and the corner did not need it, and he took it off again.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he keeps for it, and about four people said it after him out of step with one another, and it came back off the face of that bank and did not get as far as the tables.

The turner did not take his heel off the treadle. The wheel at the near end of that ground went on at the speed it goes at, and everything that was said at that bank that day went over the top of it and stopped at the bank.

At the north end the man who digs loam had his strip open and the blade of the spade going into a place in it that had not been there yesterday.

---

The sun came over the top of the low wall a little after half past three and lay down along the stones at the bottom of it, and the man standing in that strip of light stepped into it the way he steps into it, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones with his coat folded beside him.

"Good afternoon," the man on the stones said across those four feet.

"Afternoon," the man at the foot of the wall said, and that was the whole of it, and it was the whole of it the day before, and it has been the whole of it for longer than either of them would say.

---

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six was at his own wall for the second time that day and took the four figures off it, all four of them, in the ordinary order he takes them in, and laid his hand flat on the cold stone underneath the lowest one.

"I do not know what these four of them are for," he said out loud to the stone.

About nineteen people in that yard had heard him say it for the hundred and seventy-fourth time running, and nobody asked him one thing about any of it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet had not asked him anything since before the middle of the winter.

---

The clerk came along the boards to the near end of the second table at about four with the book open in her hand and stopped where she stands every afternoon.

She gave the account of that morning out loud before she had the pen near any part of it, which is what she does.

"The two hundred and eighty-fifth morning," she said. "Nothing has gone out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so this morning goes down in my hand and there is nothing else in it. I have said all of that out loud before a mark of it goes on the page."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own small careful writing and took no line for it at all, and the page at the back of that book carried fifty-seven lines before she shut it and carried fifty-seven lines after.

---

He heard her read it out. He heard the whole of it, and he had heard her read the account of a morning out loud more times than he could have put in order, and this morning he listened to it the way he listened to the wind coming over the top of that bank and dying out in the ground behind it.

There is no figure in it for her either. She had not said a figure out loud that day. Nobody up that bank had said anything about the fact that the record at the back of that book had stood another day, and he had a whole morning in which he could have said something about it and had not, and the reason he had not was that there was nobody in that yard to say it to, and he could not have told anybody why that was.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of the nineteen in it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not join in and stood where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

He folded his cloth over his hand and went up the slope behind the second table, and she went up it ahead of him with the basket on her arm and the cloth knotted over her scale, and the four feet of ground between them came up the slope with them and did not get any smaller.

At the boards the boy put his thumb under the corner of the lot book to settle it against the rail, and shut nothing, and wrote nothing, and had said his count out loud at ten to eight and had told that yard there was nothing after it.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book, and the page did not move on this morning. A figure of two hundred days came to the back of that book and nobody in that yard asked what it is the two hundredth of. A man at a corner of the second table worked out what would happen if he said a sentence about a whole number and found there is nowhere in that yard to put such a sentence, and he did not say it. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
