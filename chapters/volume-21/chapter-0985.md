# Chapter 0985: The Two Hundred And Sixty-Ninth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Nobody Said A Reason And Nobody Said No, And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud At About Four And Wrote It On That Page In Her Own Hand Without A Line, And The Pen Went Down Beside The Front Of That Book And Not Into It

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty-four days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-three. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-nine days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirteen days. Eight hundred and seventy-four days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-eight days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-three days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-three days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty-six days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and two days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-two marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-eight marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

One reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go until the two hundred and eighth morning, so nothing was written for it then, and that reason is not counted anywhere and never has been counted anywhere.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-nine mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-nine mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-nine mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-eighth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and tenth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twenty-fourth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-three of them, and this is the one hundred and twenty-fifth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fifty-eighth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-six lines when the clerk sat down this morning. The record has stood one hundred and eighty-four days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fourteenth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She had the book open on her knees at about nine and she read the top of the page down to the bottom of it without touching the pen, which she does most mornings, and which has never once got her anything either way.

Fifteen years is a long time to sit in a place at the foot of a bank with a pen in your hand and a page in front of you and a rule about what goes on it. She had got so good at the reading of it that she no longer needed to be quiet about it, and she had got so good at the quiet that she did not know which of the two had come first.

There is a worn place in the paper about four inches from the foot of that page.

Fifteen years ago the pen she used had a nib that had to be dipped into an ink that stood on those boards in a bottle, and that bottle went missing on a morning, and about four people looked for it for a day and a half, and nobody in this district ever worked out who had it. The pen she has now does not have to be dipped and the ink is in the stem of it and there is nothing at those boards to go missing.

She has never told anybody at that yard what that is worth and nobody has ever asked her what it is worth, and there are about nineteen people at the foot of that bank who would not have understood the difference if she had. It has been there for years. She has never ruled it out. Nobody in that yard has ever asked her about it and she has never mentioned it, and the line that went on yesterday went over the top of it and did not sit level with the ones above it, and she had seen that yesterday and had written it anyway.

---

At about ten to eight Orren Pike said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and stood at the near end of the first table until somebody told him to move along, and he moved.

He says the lane count before her hand moves on anything, which he does every morning, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a boy who says a number and then says out loud that he is saying no number has never once been asked what the two of those are for, and that about four of them have not said whether he would go on doing it if nobody ever answered him.

The man of fifty-six had said the four off that stone and got all four of them, and he was not standing at that wall at that moment.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it. The man at the end of that table took the wedge side of it without being asked, put his boot on, and did not take it off again until the light had gone off the boards. The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table and said nothing out loud at any point of it. The turner's wheel went on turning and said nothing.

The frame was lying flat on the turf at the top of that bank with the rebate face up, and the woman of about fifty-two was sitting on the low stone beside it with her back to the top of that bank, and she had not come down that bank for three mornings now, and nobody at the foot of that bank had gone up it to ask her why not.

---

She came down that bank at about half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk.

She said nothing out loud this morning, and she had thought about saying something at about half past one and had not, and the not doing it took about half of what the doing it had taken the last time.

At the north end of that yard the man who digs loam had his strip open and the spade going into the same place in it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the ground at that end of that yard has been open four days running and the spade has gone into the same place in it every one of them, and that about four of them have not said how deep it is.

---

The man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air at about half past one in the voice he uses for it, and about four people took it up after him out of step with each other, and it came back off the bank. Nobody at those boards wrote any part of it down.

About half past three the sun came over that low wall and lay along those stones, and a man of sixty-one was sitting up on them about four feet off the man standing at the foot of it with his arms resting on his knees. They said good afternoon, and he said the one word back, and he said nothing else that afternoon, and nobody went near the pair of them for the rest of it.

He was back at that wall at about four and he said the four off it a second time and got all four of them again, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one. What he said, in the ordinary voice, was that he still does not know what the four of them are for. About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifty-eighth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

And then the clerk read that morning out loud. She was up off her stool for it and standing about four feet off the near end of the second table, and she did not go back to the stool until she had said the whole of it. Her pen did not go near the page until after that.

"The two hundred and sixty-ninth morning," she said. "Nobody has said a reason out loud in this yard this morning and nobody has said no either, so there is nothing for the back of that book to do and the morning goes on it in my hand. I have said that out loud, as I do, and there is no more of it than that."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it. The page carried fifty-six lines before she shut it and fifty-six lines after, and the line that went on yesterday is still there over the top of the worn place where it does not sit level with the others, and nobody at those boards said a word about any of it while she was doing it.

Then she put the pen down on the boards beside the front of that book, which by then was lying open on the boards with its back to the light, and not into the holder, and left it lying there.

Orren Pike came along the near side of the second table with his arms full of nothing, the way he does when he has finished with the boards and has not been told to do anything else, and he looked down at the pen lying there and then at the back of that book, and he did not ask her about the pen and he did not ask her about the page, and he went on along the table and up the slope.

About four people at the top of eleven feet watched that pen lie where it was lying and about four of them have not said what they thought a clerk would be doing with a pen for the last half hour of an afternoon.

She shut the book and kept her hand flat on the cover of it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched her do that and did not say anything to her about it, because nobody in that yard had any standing to ask a clerk why her pen was where it was.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stayed exactly where they were.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, knotted it, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank, and the pen was lying on the boards beside the front of that lot book when she went past it, and nobody picked it up.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. The clerk read that morning out loud and wrote nothing under it but the morning, and the pen went down beside the front of that book and not into it. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that she has read a morning and written nothing on two mornings running, and that about four of them have not said what is going to break the run. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
