# Chapter 0997: The Two Hundred And Eighty-First Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And At About Half Past One A Figure Of Her Own Went Out Loud At Her Own Scale And About Four Feet Away At The Other End Of The Same Table The Man Of About Thirty-Four Said One Reason Out Loud, And The Page Took One Line Carrying The Morning And Not The Reason

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-six days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-five. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and eleven days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-five days. Eight hundred and eighty-six days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and five days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-five days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and seventy-eight days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and fourteen days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-four marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

One reason was said out loud this morning at about four feet off the near end of the second table, so one line went on the page at the back of that lot book, and the line carries the morning and carries nothing else. Nobody answered him and nobody thanked him. A figure of her own went out into the open air of that yard in the same minute at her own scale. Nobody has said since whether the two went out together. The lane count is thirty and it did not move. Nothing came down that lane.

Somebody said a reason out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, on a morning when the back of that lot book was not a place a reason could go, and no line went on that page for it, and that reason is not counted anywhere in this yard or in any record of this district.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and eleven mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-one mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-one mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eightieth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-second of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-sixth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-five of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-seventh morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventieth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-six lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-six days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-sixth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

It happened inside one short minute and it was not, at the time, anything a person could have held.

There were four things in that yard that morning that went into the open air of it, and three of them belonged to men who say the same figure every day at the same hour and one of them belonged to a woman at a scale who has said a figure out loud in that yard on three mornings now and on no others.

At about half past one she put a bar on her own pan and waited for it to come still and then said one figure out loud, once, at her own scale, in the ordinary voice.

About four feet from the other end of the same table, the man of about thirty-four put down his cloth and said one reason out loud, once, in the ordinary voice.

---

Nobody answered either of them.

The wheel at the near end of that ground went on at the speed it goes at. The man of thirty-eight came along the top of his own table and round the end and did not break his route. The man at the far end of the second table put his thumb on the rim of what he was holding and turned it a quarter round and did not look over. The boy was at the boards with the lot book wedged against the rail and did not turn round.

Nobody asked her what she weighs things for. Nobody asked him to say it again. Nobody asked him why. Nobody thanked him and nobody thanked her.

About nine people at the top of eleven feet had both of them.

---

What the yard did with it was not a thing anybody could have reported afterwards.

A reason said out loud at that bank goes into about nineteen people and there is a thing that can be said afterwards, whether it is about a wedge under a leg or about a frame somebody has carried down a slope four times, and somebody in that yard will be the one who said it.

A figure said out loud at a scale goes into about the same number of people and there is nothing to say afterwards, because there was never a shape to it. It comes out, it is in the air, and then it is in as many heads as were standing there, and none of those heads has it in it the way it would have it if a person had said a thing with a person in it.

He had known that. He had known it about her figures for nine years and had never once been in a position to use the knowing.

This morning the two of them went out of that yard about four feet apart, and about four feet of ground and the whole of the air of that yard had both of them in it, and nobody in that yard has said since whether the two went out together.

---

The clerk read that morning out loud.

She did it once at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in the ordinary voice, and she gave the account of it out loud before she put her pen to any part of that page.

"The two hundred and eighty-first morning," she said. "One line goes on the back of that book this morning, and nothing else does. It was said out loud at about half past one at the near end of this table and it was a reason, and the line carries the morning and carries nothing else. I have given the account out loud before I have written a word of it, and nobody in this yard is going to be told which words it was in."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took one line for it, and the line carried the two hundred and eighty-first morning and carried nothing else at all, and the page carried fifty-six lines before she shut that book and fifty-seven lines after.

There is a worn place in that paper about four inches from the foot of the page, where an old pen stood and was let stand too long, and the line went over the top of it and did not sit level with the ones above it, and she had seen that and had written it anyway.

---

Orren Pike was at those boards at about half past one and he had his elbows on them and his face turned a few degrees towards the near end of the second table, and he heard a figure go out of a woman at a scale and a sentence go out of a man at a table, and he did not write either of them down, and he has never once written down anything anybody said in that yard, and there is no figure for how many times he could have and did not, because there is no figure for how many times he could have.

What he did do was look along the line of pieces on the top of the man of thirty-eight's table and count them, because that is a thing he can do at those boards with his hands where he is, and he counted them twice and got the same number both times.

Nobody at those boards said one word about the page while she was writing on it.

He went back to his corner of the second table and picked up his cloth and went along the top of it from the near end to the far end and back again, and she went back to the basket at the foot of her own scale and put the next thing on the pan and waited for it to come still.

Neither of them looked at the other one for the rest of that afternoon and neither of them went near the other one's end of that table, and the four feet of ground between them stayed four feet of ground, and the light came across it as it came across everything else that day.

---

The boards were up at about ten to eight and the count had been said out loud at them before a hand in that yard had moved, which was thirty, with nothing put after it, and the boy saying out loud that there was nothing after it.

That wall at the end of that ground had a man at it before the light, and he had got all four of them.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table got his boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks and left it there for the rest of the day. The man of thirty-two put his own table up on the hard ground, squared it at the near corner with the back of his heel, and went along the top of it with a cloth.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table and said nothing out loud at all that morning. A wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning.

She came down that bank at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off that bank, and the man of about thirty-four put down his cloth a minute or so later and said a reason out loud, and neither of the two of them turned round.

---

By about half past three the sun had come over that low wall and was lying along the stones at the foot of it. The man standing in that strip had been in it since about nine and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his coat folded beside him on the stones. Good afternoon went across the four feet and one word came back.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six took the four off that wall for the second time that day, got all four of them, laid his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said out loud in the ordinary voice that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and seventieth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

---

The light went off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stayed where they were standing until the boards came down off the cart face down.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank, and about four feet behind her the man of about thirty-four picked up his cloth and went up the slope with it hanging over his hand, and neither of the two of them said anything on the way up.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book, and one of the fifty-seven went on this morning over the top of the worn place about four inches from the foot of the page. Nobody answered him and nobody thanked him. A figure of her own went out loud at that scale in the same minute and took nothing, and about four feet is the whole of the distance between the two ends of that table. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
