# Chapter 0976: The Two Hundred And Sixtieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Second Number Went Into That Yard On The Same Morning, And It Was Not Hers, And A Man At The Far End Of The Second Table Who Has Said Nothing Out Loud For As Long As Anybody Can Remember Said One

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and fifteen days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and four days. Eight hundred and sixty-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-four days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-three days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and nineteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. Four documents are not owned in this district. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put anything to the man of fifty-six, and nobody put anything to the man who makes round things either. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line was written for it then and it is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-ninth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and first of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and fifteenth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and fourteen of them, and this is the one hundred and sixteenth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and forty-ninth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-five days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and fifth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is spent and is not going to be raised. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The fire was going in the small stove at the far end of the second table, and the man who makes round things had his sleeves rolled and a thing in his hand.

He had been at that end of that table for a long stretch of mornings. The only words anybody there has ever had from him have been about where to put a thing down. He was not a man anybody expected a sound out of, and the wheel at the near end had been turning all morning at the pitch it turns at, and the two of them together made a noise in that yard that is best described as a floor.

He took the thing out of the fire and looked at the place on it that had been in.

Then he put it back in.

He took it out again and looked at the same place. He has been doing that at the same end of that table for longer than the near rail has been on the near side of it, and the reason for it is on the face of the thing and not on the table: a round thing that has been in a fire has one place where the colour has gone further than the rest, and until that place matches the rest of the ring you have not made the thing, you have made a thing with a fault in it that a person will find in a year.

He turned it in his hand. He put his thumb on the rim.

---

It was about one when he said it.

He said a number, once, in the ordinary voice, to the man next to him, in the way a man says a thing to the man he is working beside when the thing has been sitting on him all morning and has finally come out as a figure. He did not raise it and he did not say it a second time and he did not say it to anybody else. He said it the way a man says a thing to the man he is working beside, which is to say with his face still on the work.

Then he put the thing on the stone to cool and turned it over and picked it up again and turned it over back, and he said nothing else for the rest of the morning.

Nobody answered him. Nobody asked him what the number was about. Nobody asked him where the number came from and nobody asked him to say it again. The man next to him went on with what he was doing and did not look up. Nobody at those boards put a line on the page at the back of that lot book. The man at the end of the second table put his head down over his own work.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man at the far end of the second table said a number out loud at about one this morning and that about four of them have not said what it was about, and that about four of them have said that man has not said a number out loud in any stretch of mornings anybody there can remember, and that about four of them have not said what changed.

He went on at that end of the table. He took the thing out of the fire at about half past two and looked at the place on it, and this time the place matched the ring all the way round, and he set it down on the stone on its own side and left it there to go cold.

---

She came down that bank at about half past twelve, before that, and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud all morning.

She heard both of the numbers go out that morning. Hers had gone out on the morning before that, once, and nobody had taken it up, and this one went out of a man at the far end of a table with his thumb on the rim of his work.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and the turner heard it over his own wheel and said nothing.

About four hundred yards of air had taken two numbers that day. Nothing at those boards had moved for either of them.

She said nothing about either of them, then or afterwards, and she could not have told you afterwards which one she had heard first.

---

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that two figures went out of that yard on one morning and that about four of them have not said which of the two heard both, and that about four of them have not said what either number was about, and that about four of them have said the clerk read the morning out loud the same way she reads it every morning, and that about four of them have not said what anybody thought of that either.

The man of about thirty-two squared his own table with the back of his heel and took the cloth along the top of it and said nothing. The man of thirty-eight worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table and said nothing. The man of fifty-six read the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and got all four of them, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet did not ask him anything about any of it. At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure this morning, and then stood at the near end of the first table and looked at the boards until they stopped being interesting to him.

---

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him. They said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else. Nobody went near him. He was not asked one question.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and forty-ninth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud at about four feet off the near end of the second table, once, and she read it before she wrote anything, and about nineteen people at the foot of that bank heard it.

"The two hundred and sixtieth morning," she said. "There is nothing going on the back of that book and the morning goes on it in my hand, and I have said that out loud and I will say it again tomorrow."

Then she wrote the morning in her own hand under the last line on that page, and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-four lines before she shut it and fifty-four lines after.

---

By about half past four the light had come off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of eleven feet went on talking it over after it.

The boards came off the cart and went down the bank face down, the way they are always put away. And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and went up that bank with the basket on her arm, and the man who makes round things put the fire out in his stove at the far end of the second table and went up behind her, and neither of them said anything to the other on the way up.

---

Fifty-four lines are on that page and none of them is in the lot book. Two numbers went out of that yard today — one at about one, with a thumb on the rim of a thing that had been in a fire twice, and one at about half past one into about four hundred yards of air — and not one of the two is on that page. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. Nobody has said what either of the two was about, including whoever said them.
