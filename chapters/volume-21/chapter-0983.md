# Chapter 0983: The Two Hundred And Sixty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And Six Hundred Days Past A Printing It Did Not Make Went By, And Nobody In That Yard Looked Up

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty-two days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and eleven days. Eight hundred and seventy-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-six marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put one word to the man of fifty-six out loud. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning a person said a reason out loud in that yard and nothing was written for it, because the back of that lot book did not become a place a reason could go until the two hundred and eighth morning, and that reason is not counted anywhere and is not going to be counted.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-seven mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-seven mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-seven mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-sixth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and eighth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twenty-second night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-one of them, and this is the one hundred and twenty-third morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fifty-sixth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-five lines and carried fifty-five lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and eighty-two days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twelfth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

There was nothing on that morning that anybody had to look up for.

That is not a thing anybody in that yard would have said about it, and nobody did, and it is the whole of what happened: the boards came up, the light came across the eleven feet a foot at a time, the second table went a foot further along the hard ground, and every person at that bank did the thing they had done the day before, in the order they had done it in the day before, and the morning went by at the speed that yard goes by.

Six hundred days is a round figure and it arrived without anybody noticing it, the way the six hundredth of anything arrives in a yard that is not keeping the count.

---

At the wall the man of fifty-six said the four off the stone and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and two men came down the bank about nine feet behind him and one of them said something about the weather and neither of them said it to him.

Before nine the second table went a foot further along the hard ground and the near rail went with it, and the man at the end of that table was standing in the gap they made without waiting to be asked to. He put his boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks and stayed on it until the legs were down and the two of them had gone, and then he sat.

The frame was lying flat on the turf at the top of that bank with the woman of about fifty-two sitting on the low stone beside it, her back to the top of that bank and her face to the yard, and the bag was on the grass behind her, and she had not come down that bank this morning.

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down the slope with his hands showing and started at the empty end of his table, and he worked at about four feet off the near end of it without speaking all morning.

Over at the near end of that ground the wheel went on turning. At the far end of the second table the man who makes round things had the small stove going at his elbow and took a thing out of the fire and looked at the place where it had been in and put it back in.

---

She came down that bank at half past twelve and set the scale up about four feet off the near end of the second table, and then worked through the basket one piece at a time, in the order she always works through it.

She weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk, and the light came onto the edge of that board at about a quarter to one, and she stood with her hand on the upright and the chalk in the other one.

She said nothing out loud.

She was aware, while she was standing there, of having said a figure out loud on two mornings and of the yard not having done one single thing about either of them, and she found that she was not troubled by it. That was the whole of what she found. She had expected to be troubled, and she was not, and she went back to the basket and took the next thing out of it.

Nine years of standing at that bank had taught her what a number is for, which is to be the same on the next morning. She had taught herself nothing else and nobody had offered her the chance.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it.

About four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, none of them at the same moment as any other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank. The turner had all of it over his own wheel and it made no difference to him at all. The man at the end of the second table said it. Two men at the first end said it, one right behind the other, so close together that they ran into each other.

The man of about thirty-four did not say it.

He was standing at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his own table squared and the cloth over his shoulder and his hand on the corner, and he heard the whole of it go across that yard three times, and he did not say it, and he did not do anything at all instead of saying it, which is what a person does when they have decided about something.

She noticed. Nobody else in that yard would have noticed, because it is not a thing that yard watches for.

Something said to the man beside him about the wind at about half past one did not get said to him either, and he went on with his hand on the corner of that table and his eyes on the ground in front of his own boots.

Orren Pike said his lane count and then said out loud that he was saying no figure, and then stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back and waited for the boards to stop being new to him. They stopped being new to him a long time back. He had gone on doing it anyway, and about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a boy who keeps saying a number and then says out loud that he is saying no number is doing the two most useful things anybody can do at those boards, and that about four of them have not said what either of the two is for.

She stood at her scale with her hand flat on the upright and watched him not say a number that about four other people in that yard said out loud in the ordinary way, and she understood then that there is no difference between the two kinds of man except that one of them has done a thing out loud in that yard once and the other one has not, and that the yard does not have a place for the one who has.

---

At about two the cart came through the gap in that bank.

The gap is worn round at the edges by wheels and it is about the width of a cart and no more, and the man of about twenty-nine who drives it came through it without slowing, with the near wheel still dragging a little over the stones, and the dust of it went up the bank behind him and settled on the boards.

He did not stop. He did not put a hand up to anybody at the foot of that bank, and nobody at the foot of that bank put anything out towards him, and by the time the dust had come down the boards were clean again and there was nothing to show that anything had gone through that gap at all.

---

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a man at about four feet off the near end of the second table did not say a number this afternoon that about four other people said out loud, and that about four of them have not said what it is to hear your own yard take a number up four times and keep your mouth shut, and that about four of them have said he said a thing out loud in this yard a number of mornings back and the page at the back of that lot book took one line for it, and that about four of them have not said what he thinks that gets him.

---

At about half past three the light came over that low wall and lay down along the stones. The man at the foot of the wall had his hands behind his back and had not sat down and had not gone anywhere. A man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him. They said good afternoon to each other across the four feet, and he said one word back, and that was the whole of the talking they did that afternoon.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for, and about nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifty-sixth time running, and nobody asked him anything about any of it.

After that the clerk read the morning out loud, in the ordinary voice and once only, and she had given the whole of the account before she had written any part of it.

"The two hundred and sixty-seventh morning," she said. "Nothing has been said out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes on it in my hand and that is the account."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-five lines before she put her hand flat on the cover and fifty-five lines after.

---

The light came off the boards at about half past four. About four people out of about nineteen said the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard, and the rest of that yard stood about where it was standing while the boards came down off the cart.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale, knotted it, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank with the chalk in her pocket and every figure she had weighed that morning on the edge of her own board and none of them said.

---

Fifty-five lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. Six hundred days went past a printing it did not make this morning and nobody in that yard looked up, because that yard is not keeping that count. A cart came through that gap at about two and went on up the bank. A man stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his mouth shut while about four people said a number out loud. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid and nothing came down that lane.
