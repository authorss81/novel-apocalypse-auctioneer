# Chapter 0984: The Two Hundred And Sixty-Eighth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And A Woman Of About Fifty-Two Said One Reason Out Loud, Once, From The Top Of That Bank Without Coming Down, And The Page Took One Line Carrying The Morning And Not The Reason, And She Was Not Thanked

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty-three days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and eighty-two. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-eight days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twelve days. Eight hundred and seventy-three days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-seven days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and ninety-two days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and sixty-two days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty-five days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and one days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and forty-one marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-seven marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

One reason was said out loud this morning by a woman at the top of that bank who did not come down it, so one line went on the page at the back of that lot book, and the line carries the morning and carries nothing else. Nobody answered her and nobody thanked her. The lane count is thirty and it did not move. Nothing came down that lane.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, on a morning when that page was not yet a place a reason could go, and no line was written for it then, and it is not counted anywhere now and is not counted anywhere in this block of mornings either.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-eight mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-eight mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-eight mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-seventh morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and ninth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twenty-third night of that run, having slept on six hundred and twenty-two of them, and this is the one hundred and twenty-fourth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fifty-seventh time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-six lines and carried fifty-five lines when the clerk sat down. The record has stood one hundred and eighty-three days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirteenth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She was at the top of that bank from before the boards went up and she stayed there, which is a thing she has done for two mornings now and which about four people at the foot of that bank have noticed and about four of them have not mentioned.

The frame was on the top of the bank with her. She had carried it up with the rebate facing in, and she had put the bottom edge of it down flat on the turf where the wind could not get under it, and she had sat down on the low stone beside it with her back to the top of that bank and her face to the yard.

From where she sat she could see the whole of that ground, which is a thing very few people can do.

She had carried that frame up that bank four times now and she was not carrying it anywhere to have it looked at. Whoever had made it had cut a rebate round the inside of it an inch and a bit deep for something that goes in and comes out again, and the corners of that rebate are worn through where the wood has been lifted out and put back a number of times, and there is nothing in this district now that a frame like that is the frame of.

She had not put that thought anywhere and she was not going to, and the bag with the form in it was against her hip where she could feel the corner of it through the cloth.

Orren Pike came down that bank at about ten with an armful of boards and stopped about six feet off where she was sitting and turned round to look at the light on the top of that bank. He did not come any nearer her and he did not say good morning to her, and she did not say good morning to him either, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet saw the two of them standing there and neither said a word to the other. She could see the boards and the second table and the near rail and the first table, and the gap in that bank, and the man of thirty-eight's line of pieces along the top of his table, and a woman about four feet off the near end of the second table standing at a scale in a piece of cloth with her hand on the upright.

She had come up that bank with the frame four times now.

---

At about ten to eight the boy said the lane count out loud at those boards, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure, and went and stood at the near end of the first table with his hands behind his back.

At the wall the man of fifty-six had already said the four and got all four of them.

Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table was already standing outside it with his boot up on the wedge, waiting for the legs to stop moving. The man of about thirty-two put his own table up and squared it with the back of his heel and went along the top of it with the cloth. The turner's wheel went on turning at the near end of that ground. The man of about thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and started at the empty end of the table and said nothing out loud at all that morning.

---

She came down that bank at about half past twelve and set up about four feet off the near end of the second table and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud to anybody.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air and about four people said it after him out of step with each other.

---

At about two, from the top of that bank, without coming down it, the woman of about fifty-two said one thing out loud, once.

She did not stand up to do it. She stayed where she was sitting on the low stone with her back to the top of the bank and the frame flat on the turf beside her, and she said it in the ordinary voice, at the top of her own, the way a person says a thing across a yard, and about nineteen people at the foot of that bank heard the whole of it because the ground is flat and the day is quiet and the turner's wheel is the only thing in it.

"I have brought that frame down this bank four times," she said, "and I have never once had anything to put in the rebate, and I am going to keep bringing it down, and that is the whole of what I have got to say about it."

---

Nobody answered her.

The turner's wheel went on turning. The man of thirty-eight did not break his route. The man of about thirty-four stood at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hand on the corner of his own table and did not turn round. Two people at the boards looked up at the bank and then looked back down at the boards, which is what people in a yard do when a thing is said at them from above it.

Nobody thanked her. Nobody asked her to say it again. Nobody asked her what the frame was for. Nobody went up that bank afterwards and stood near her, and she stayed on the low stone where she was sitting, and the frame stayed flat on the turf.

---

The woman who keeps a scale heard it.

She had the chalk in her hand and she heard a woman on the top of that bank say one plain sentence into about four hundred yards of air, and she stood still for the length of it with her hand on the upright, and then she went on with the thing in her hand.

What she thought about it, for as long as she thought about it, was that a person had said one sentence out loud at the top of that bank and it was going to cost that person nothing whatever, and that she had done the same thing at the foot of it eleven mornings back and it had cost her nothing whatever too, and that the two of them had got the same thing out of it and that this was the first time in nine years that anybody in that yard had said a sentence out loud within about nine feet of her.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman said a plain thing out loud from the top of that bank this afternoon and did not come down, and that about four of them have not said what it is to hear somebody talk about their own hands from above you, and that about four of them have said nobody answered her, and that about four of them have not said what they would have said if somebody had.

---

By about half past three that low wall had been laying a strip of sun about four feet long along those stones for the better part of an hour, and the man at the foot of it was standing in it where he had been standing since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his arms hanging over his knees. Good afternoon was said across the four feet between them and one word came back over it, and then nothing at all.

The man of fifty-six was still at that wall when the sun came down onto the stones, and at about four o'clock he said the four off that stone a second time and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and said out loud, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for. It was in the hearing of about nineteen people and it was the hundred and fifty-seventh time running. Nobody asked him anything about it, or about that wall, or about his hand.

The clerk read that morning out loud. She did it from where she sits, once, in the ordinary voice, and she had the whole of the account of it said at that bank before she put a hand on that page.

"The two hundred and sixty-eighth morning," she said. "One line goes on the back of that book. It was said out loud at about two and it was a reason, and the line carries the morning and it does not carry the reason, and nobody in this yard is going to be told which words the reason was in. That is the account, and I have given it out loud before I wrote it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line on that page in her own hand, and took one line for it, and the line carried the morning and carried nothing else, and the pen went back into the holder, and nobody at those boards said one word about the page while she was writing on it.

---

By about half past four the light had gone off the boards, and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and did not go up.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and went up that bank with the basket on her arm.

The woman of about fifty-two stood the frame up off the turf and carried it under her arm and went on up the slope with the bag on her shoulder, and she did not thank anybody for the afternoon and nobody thanked her, and the second space on that form went up with her in the bag and it was still empty.

---

Fifty-six lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. One of the fifty-six went on this morning, over the top of the worn place about four inches from the foot of the page, and it carries the two hundred and sixty-eighth morning and carries nothing else. Nobody asked that woman what the frame was for. Nobody thanked her. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
