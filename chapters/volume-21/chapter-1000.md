# Chapter 1000: The Two Hundred And Eighty-Fourth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Page At The Back Of That Lot Book Did Not Move On That Morning, And The Man Of About Sixty-Four Was Asked Nothing At The Foot Of That Low Wall, Which Is The One Hundred And Fortieth Morning Running Of That

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-nine days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-eight. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and fourteen days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-eight days. Eight hundred and eighty-nine days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and three days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and eight days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-eight days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighty-one days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and seventeen days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-seven marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-three marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud in this yard this morning and nobody said no, and a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and was asked nothing, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand and took no line. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, before the back of that lot book was a place a reason could go, and no line was written for it, and it is not counted anywhere now and is not going to be counted.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and fourteen mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-four mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-four mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-third morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-fifth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-ninth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-eight of them, and this is the one hundred and fortieth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-third time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-nine days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-ninth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He stands at the foot of that low wall from about nine until the light goes off those boards, and he has done it on every morning of a run that is longer than the second table has stood on that ground.

He is about sixty-four. There is no bench there and there is no stone for sitting on and there is nothing at the foot of that wall for a person to do, and he has never once tried to make there be.

What is there is a strip of about four feet of sun that comes over that wall at about half past three and lies down along those stones for the better part of an hour, and he is in it when it comes.

---

The boards went up at about ten to eight and the boy said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure.

Nobody in that yard has ever asked the man at the foot of that wall a question, on any morning, for any reason at all. Not on the mornings when he has been the only thing moving in that part of the ground. Not on the mornings when a sentence has gone out loud about four hundred yards from him and everybody else in that yard has turned round to see where it came from.

Not this morning.

He has been the one thing in that yard nobody asks anything of for a hundred and forty mornings now, and there is nothing about that in his face, and there is no arrangement in that yard that keeps it so, and nobody has ever mentioned it to him because there is nobody there who has ever noticed a thing about it.

He came to that wall on the morning he started, and there was a man of about sixty-four already standing at it, and they had said good morning to each other, and he had never in his life asked that man one question about anything and has not asked him one since.

There are about nine feet of ground between that wall and the boards. He has spent every morning of a run of mornings standing in the same about four feet of it, and the ground he is not standing on has got the same amount of wear on it as the ground he is, which is the only thing anybody could tell anybody about where a man spends his days.

He does not know it as a run of mornings. He has not got anything in his head at all about being asked nothing, and if it were put to him he would not have a way of putting it into words, and he has never needed one.

---

The man of fifty-six was at that wall at about a quarter to eight and got all four of them, and came away from it and went and stood at the near end of the first table for about as long as it takes a man of fifty-six to say four figures off a wall, and went back to it.

At about half past nine the man of thirty-eight came down the slope with his hands showing and worked his route without breaking it. Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table put his boot on the wedge. The man of thirty-two put his own table up, squared it at the near corner, and went along the top of it with a cloth. A wheel on a stand at the near end of that ground went on turning.

The man of about thirty-four was at about four feet off the near end of the second table with his hand flat on the corner of it and did not go over to the scale that stands about four feet off that end of the same table.

She came down at half past twelve with the basket on her arm and weighed what was in the basket and put the figures on the edge of her own board in chalk and said nothing out loud to anybody that morning.

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air, and about four people said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off that bank.

At the north end the man who digs loam had his strip open and the spade going into a new place in it, having found one on the ground before this one.

The boy stood at the near end of the first table for a while at about two with his elbows on the boards and looked along that yard at nothing in particular, and nobody stopped him and he said nothing.

---

The sun came over that low wall at about half past three and lay down along the stones at its foot.

The man at the foot of it was standing where he had been standing since about nine and he stepped the two steps into that strip of sun without hurrying and stood in it with his hands down at his sides.

A man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him on those stones with his coat folded beside him. He has been sitting about four feet off him every afternoon of that stretch of mornings and he has said one word back every afternoon of that stretch of mornings, and that is the whole of what has passed between the two of them in any of it.

Good afternoon was said across the four feet and afternoon was said back, and neither of them said anything else at all.

---

The light goes off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank did not join in.

He does not go up that bank at the goodnight. There is nothing at the top of that bank for him and he has never said that he knows of.

At about half past four the man of fifty-six was at that wall for the second time that day and got all four of them off it and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said out loud, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and seventy-third time running, and nobody asked him anything.

And the clerk read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account of it out loud before she had her pen to any of it.

"The two hundred and eighty-fourth morning," she said. "Nothing has been said out loud in this yard this morning that goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes on in my hand and there is nothing else in the account of it. I have given the account out loud before I have written a word of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-seven lines before she shut that book and fifty-seven lines after, and the pen went back into the holder and the book went onto the boards against the rail and the boy put his thumb under the corner of it.

---

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that bank.

She had not said a figure out loud that morning. She has said one out loud on a morning eleven mornings back and on one at about half past one two mornings back and on one yesterday, and there is nobody at that yard who has said one word to her about any of the three of them.

He watched her go up that slope from where he was standing in the last of that sun on the stones, the way he has watched that bank empty itself every afternoon of that stretch of mornings, and then he looked back at the wall in front of him, which is a low wall about as high as his hand, and there is nothing on it.

At the far end of that ground the man of fifty-six was still at his own wall with his hand on the stone under the bottom one, and about four feet off the near end of the second table a man was putting his cloth away, and the boards came down off the cart face down and went up that bank, and that yard was empty by the time the light had gone off it altogether.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man of about sixty-four stood at the foot of that low wall from about nine until the light went off those boards and was asked nothing, and this is the one hundred and fortieth morning running of that. Nothing came down that lane on any morning of that block of ten and the lane count is thirty on all ten of them. The fifth of the five is unpaid. The count of counted months is seven and is on nothing. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read, and that figure is three on this morning and is not going to be lower.
