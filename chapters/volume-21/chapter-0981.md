# Chapter 0981: The Two Hundred And Sixty-Fifth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And She Said A Second Figure Of Her Own Out Loud, Once, And Nobody Asked Her Where The Number Came From And Nobody Asked Her What She Weighs Things For, And The Page Took No Line

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twenty days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-nine. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and ninety-five days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and nine days. Eight hundred and seventy days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and eighty-four days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-nine days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-nine days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and sixty-two days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety-eight days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty-eight marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and twenty-four marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put one word to the woman who keeps a scale out loud about the figure she said at about half past one. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

A reason was said out loud in that yard on the one hundred and eighty-second morning, and it was said on a morning when the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, so no line went on that page for it, and that reason is not counted anywhere now.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety-five mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and forty-five mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and sixty-five mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and sixty-fourth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and sixth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twentieth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and nineteen of them. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and fifty-fourth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-five lines and carried fifty-five lines when the light went off it. The record has stood one hundred and eighty days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and tenth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

She came down that bank before the boards went up, which she had done twice in nine years, and stood at the foot of it with the basket on her arm and did not set the scale up.

That was the whole of the reason she had come early. She wanted the yard to fill before she put anything on. Nine years of coming down at half past twelve and finding four people at that bank and eleven by the time the light was right on the edge of her board, and this morning she wanted the other order of it, and she got the other order of it, and it turned out to make no difference whatever, which she had known it would not before she came down.

The boards went up while the light was still on the eleven feet. The boy got the corner of the lot book back against the rail it had been wedged against and did not look at the page it fell open to.

"Thirty," he said, to the boards and to anybody standing near them. "And no figure after it, because I am saying no figure."

He has said that in that voice every morning for longer than the second table has stood on that ground. Orren Pike is seventeen and he is the only person at those boards who hears every figure that goes into that yard, and he has never once written one of them down, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet have got so used to the two sentences that they have stopped hearing either of them, which he does not mind, because the point of saying a thing out loud at those boards was never to be heard by everybody.

---

At the wall the man of fifty-six said the four off the stone and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and left it there while the light came across and reached the place his hand was.

The top figure is the one that goes, because rain has been getting into it for years and taking the sharpness off one corner, so it has to be read by what is left rather than by what was cut. He reads it last, every morning, because it arrives after three that have already told him what kind of morning his eyes are having. He has not got it wrong in seven hundred and six mornings and he does not intend to start.

Two of them came down the bank about nine feet behind him and one said something to the other about the wind and neither of them said it to him. He did not turn round.

---

Just after nine the second table was carried a foot further along the hard ground and the near rail came with it and set down hard enough to shake the legs. The man at the end of that table came round the outside of it, put his own boot on the wedge under the leg that rocks, and stood there a while with his weight on it before he sat down at all.

The man of thirty-eight came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and went to about four feet off the near end of the first table and started at the empty end of it, which is the correct way round and has been for a long time. He did not stop for anything. He did not look round when the boards came down off the cart behind him.

At about ten the man of about thirty-two put his own table up, squared it with the back of his heel at the corner nearest the near rail, once and hard, and took the cloth along the top of it from the far end back to the near end. The turner's wheel went on turning over the whole of that and said nothing. At the north end the man who digs loam had his strip open and did not come out of it.

---

She untied the knot and folded the cloth over the back of the leg and weighted it with the stone she has carried in that pocket since before that yard had a second table on it. She set the scale up about four feet off the near end of the second table and put her hand flat on the upright and looked along it for a while.

There is a thing about the ninth year of standing at a scale that nobody warns a person about. The first year, everything that comes onto that pan is a job. Somewhere in the second year it becomes a thing you know. And somewhere after that, without any morning being different from the one before it, you find that you have stopped weighing what comes to that bank and started weighing what the bank is doing, which is a different thing to do with a pair of hands and takes the same length of time.

She put a bar on the scale and waited for it to come still. The wind was coming over the top of the bank flat and the pan wanted to go with it, and she put the heel of her own hand against the upright and waited it out, and then it came still and she wrote the figure on the edge of her own board in chalk, low down, in the gap at the end of the last one.

She put the chalk in her pocket.

There is a bare stretch of wood at the end of that board about the length of her hand where the chalk has never been put. She has never written anything on it. She has never run a thumb along it to find where the last one is, and she has never once wondered whether the board is nearly finished with, and both of those are one decision and she made it years ago.

She had the wind on her back and the light coming along the eleven feet toward her, and she had a basket with four things left in it, and none of that had changed in nine years, and she found herself noticing how little of the morning had been spent on the part of it she had come early for.

---

At about half past one she said a second figure of her own out loud.

She took the chalk out again to do it. She had it out for no reason a person could name, which is that she has found on two mornings now that a figure in her hand is not the same as a figure in the air, and the difference between them is not a small one, and she has never had anybody in that yard to put the difference to.

Nobody was close to her. About four feet off the near end of the second table is not far from anybody if somebody comes that way, and about nine people were up the bank, and the turner was turning, and the man at the end of that table had his head down over his own work, and the boy had his back to her with his elbows on the boards.

She waited for the light to come onto the edge of the board so that the chalk would shine, and then she looked along the yard instead of at her own board, and she said it.

One figure. In the ordinary voice. Once.

She did not put it out into about four hundred yards of air the way the man who keeps a tally does. She gave it to about as far as the corner of that yard goes and no further.

---

Nobody answered her.

The turner's wheel went on turning. The man at the far end of that table put his thumb on the rim of the thing he was holding and turned it a quarter round and set it down on the stone and did not look over. The man of thirty-eight kept his route along the top of his table and did not break it and did not look up.

Nobody asked her what she weighs things for. Nobody asked her where the number came from. Nobody asked her to say it again. Nobody thanked her, and nobody said anything at all about it, and the figure did not go across that yard and did not come back off the bank, and about four people at the top of eleven feet heard it and let it go past them.

She went on weighing.

Her hands were not steady for the next thing in the basket and she let them be unsteady, because a still hand that is lying is the one thing worse than a hand that is not. The one after that came to rest and the figure was the figure, and she wrote it on the edge of her own board in the ordinary way.

---

What she had not been ready for was the rest of the afternoon.

She had thought, standing there with the chalk in her hand, that whatever was going to happen would happen quickly, because things do when they are said into a yard. What she had not allowed for was the other thing: that nothing would happen, and that the nothing would then have to be got through.

At about two she stood with both hands on the upright and looked along the whole length of that yard at the tables. Orren Pike was at the boards with his back to her and his elbows on them and he had not turned round, and standing there she understood that if the boy had turned round it would have been worse, because a boy turning round is a boy about to repeat it, and then a number of hers would have been said a second time by a person of seventeen who did not know what he was doing.

At about half past two the light was coming across the eleven feet for the last time that day, and she picked the chalk up and put it down again without marking anything, and she had never once done that in nine years.

At about three she tied the cloth over the scale and knotted it and left the basket at the foot of the leg, because carrying it up the bank and coming down for it would have meant two trips, and a person does two trips when she is thinking about something.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number out into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, so that it went across that yard three times over and came back off the bank. Over his own wheel the turner heard all of it, and the wheel did not change its speed. Nothing at those boards took it down.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman at a scale said one figure of her own out loud this morning and a man who keeps a tally said one out loud at the same hour, and that about four of them have not said which of the two the yard heard, and that about four of them have said the clerk read the morning out loud the same as she reads it every morning, and that about four of them have not said what anybody thought of that either.

---

At about half past three the sun came over that low wall and lay down along the stones at its foot, and the man standing at the foot of it was in the same place he had been in at nine. A man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his forearms on his knees. They said good afternoon across the four feet between them, and a man of sixty-one said afternoon, and he said nothing else at all.

About four o'clock the man of fifty-six said the four off that wall and got all four of them, and put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one and said, in the ordinary voice, that he still does not know what the four of them are for. About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and fifty-fourth time running. Nobody asked him anything.

And then the clerk got up off her stool and read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, in the ordinary voice, and she read it before she put her pen to anything.

"The two hundred and sixty-fifth morning," she said. "Nothing goes on the back of that book this morning, so the morning goes on it in my hand and there is nothing else in the account of it. If you did not take it in, I will say it again."

Nobody said it back to her. She sat down and wrote the morning under the last line on that page in her own hand and took no line for it, and the pen went into the holder, and the page carried fifty-five lines before she put her hand flat on it and fifty-five lines after.

---

The light went off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard went with it, said by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank stayed on the bank and did not say it.

And then the woman who keeps a scale untied the cloth, folded it, and put it in the basket, and stood where she was until the last of the others had gone over the top. She took the chalk out of her pocket and looked at the two she had done last and did not say either of them.

Then she went up that bank.

---

Fifty-five lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. Two figures of hers went out into the open air of that yard this morning and the second of them went out at about half past one in the ordinary voice, and neither of the two is on that page and neither of them is in that book and neither of them came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. Nobody asked her where the number came from. Nobody asked her what she weighs things for. Nobody thanked her and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
