# Chapter 1006: The Two Hundred And Ninetieth Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Page At The Back Of That Lot Book Took Two Lines In One Morning About Four Hundred Yards Apart And A Figure Of Her Own Went Out Loud In The Same Minute As The First Of Them And The Clerk Read The Morning Out Loud Once And Wrote Two Lines Carrying The Morning And Not The Reasons

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and forty-five days is the age of that figure.

The board carries nine hundred and four. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and twenty days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and thirty-four days. Eight hundred and ninety-five days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and nine days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and fourteen days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and eighty-four days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighty-seven days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and twenty-three days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and sixty-three marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-nine marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

The man of about thirty-four said one reason out loud, once, at about eleven this morning, about four feet off the near end of the second table, and the woman who keeps a scale said a figure out loud, once, at her own scale in the same minute, and the man who digs loam said one reason out loud from the north end at about a quarter past eleven without coming down, and the page at the back of that lot book took two lines this morning and both of them carry the morning and neither of them carries a reason. The lane count is thirty and it did not move and nothing came down that lane.

On the one hundred and eighty-second morning somebody said a reason out loud in that yard and the page at the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, so that reason went into the air and was not written, and it is not counted anywhere.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and twenty mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and seventy mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and ninety mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-ninth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and thirty-first of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and forty-fifth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and forty-four of them, and this is the one hundred and forty-sixth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-ninth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-nine lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood two hundred and five days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and thirty-fifth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

Fifteen years, and the thing about this morning was not that something unusual had happened at that bank.

It was that for the first time in all of them, there had been two things to say out loud.

---

She came to the boards at about ten to eight with the book under her arm and let that boy say the count before she put a hand on anything. It is thirty. He put it out into the air at those boards in the voice he keeps for it and put nothing after it.

She had a hundred and something mornings of that count in her ear and she had never once let it get to her, and it did not get to her that morning either, and she noticed herself not letting it, which is a different thing from not noticing.

The man of fifty-six was at his wall before there was light on it and had his four off it. She saw the shape of him against the stone from about sixty feet off and turned away and did the ordinary morning.

---

The yard does what it does and you set yourself against it or you do not.

A wheel goes round at the near end of that ground at the speed it goes at. A man carries a table along the hard ground and a second table follows it a foot behind with a rail on the near side of it, and a man at the end of that table stands with his boot on a wedge until the men carrying it have gone by. A man comes down a slope at about half past nine with his hands showing and works out along the top of his own table and round the end of it and up the slope and back in at the same corner.

A woman comes down that bank at half past twelve with a basket on her arm and sets up about four feet off the near end of the second table.

None of that goes on the back of that book. All of it goes on her, which is what she has got instead of a wage, and she has never thought of it as a trade because there is nobody in that yard to trade it with.

---

At about eleven the man of about thirty-four said something.

She was at the boards with the book open against the rail and she had the pen in her hand and she heard it come out of him in the ordinary voice, at the speed he uses for a thing he means, about four feet off the near end of the second table.

In the same minute a figure came out of the woman's scale four feet further along that same table, in the ordinary voice, once, and neither of the two of them was louder than the other.

She did not put the pen to the page. That is what she wants to say about the part she is certain of. Her hand stayed where it was and the nib was about an inch off the paper and she did not write one word of it down, because what had gone out in that ground that minute was not two of the things this book takes, and she was not going to be the one to decide otherwise.

---

Nobody answered either of them.

The wheel went over the top of it. The man of thirty-eight went along his own route. The man at the far end of that table had a thing in the fire and did not look up over it.

She stood at the boards with the pen in her hand and waited, the way she waits on the back of every other morning, to find out whether anything else was going to come out of that yard. Nothing did.

---

At about a quarter past eleven the boy came along the boards to where she was standing.

He had not run. He had walked, which is the way he does everything at those boards, and he came up on her left side where she could see him out of the corner of her eye without turning her head.

"There was one at the north end," he said.

She looked at him then. He was seventeen and he had been at those boards for a good part of his life and he had never in that time come and told her anything about that yard at any hour.

"I heard it," she said.

"Then it goes down as a line," he said, which is not a question and is not an answer to anything, and he went back to his elbows on the rail.

---

About four hundred yards separates the north end of that ground from the boards.

She had been at those boards for fifteen years and she knew what that distance does to a sound, and she knew that a man who says something at the north end of that strip does not say it so that the boards will get it. He says it so that whoever is standing next to him will get it.

Whatever had gone out of that yard at a quarter past eleven had gone out of it into about four feet of air at the north end, and about four people had heard it there, and it had come the rest of the way on the boy.

---

She had two lines to write and one morning to write them in.

She stood at those boards with that book open against the rail and worked out the shape of what she was going to say out loud, because she says it out loud first. That is the whole of the rule in this yard and nobody has ever asked her where it comes from and she has never been asked what a line on that page is for.

The hard part of this morning was not the two lines. It was the one account.

She had to stand at about four feet off the near end of the second table at about four o'clock in the afternoon and say out loud, in the ordinary voice, one thing about one morning, and the one thing had to cover the reason that was said at this end of that ground at about eleven and the reason that was said at the north end at about a quarter past eleven.

She had never had to do that before and she got it right the first time she said it, and she was pleased with herself about it for about as long as it took to put the pen back in its holder.

---

The afternoon of that morning went on.

At about half past three the sun came over the top of the low wall and lay down along the stones at the foot of it, and the man standing in that strip of it said good afternoon to a man of sixty-one sitting about four feet off him on the cold stones, and got one word back.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six had his four figures off that wall and got all four of them and laid his hand flat on the stone under the lowest one.

"I still could not tell you what these four are for and that is the whole of what I know," he said out loud to the stone.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and seventy-ninth time running, and nobody asked him anything, and nobody in that yard had asked him anything since before the middle of the winter.

---

At about four o'clock she walked along the boards to the near end of the second table with the book in her hand and stopped where she stops.

"The two hundred and ninetieth morning," she said. "Two reasons were said out loud in this yard this morning. One was said at this end of this ground at about eleven and the other was said at the north end at about a quarter past eleven. Two lines go on the back of that book for them, one over the other, and neither of the two lines carries what was said in it. I have given the whole of that account out loud before I have put a mark of it on the page."

---

Then she wrote.

She wrote the two hundred and ninetieth morning under the last line in her own small careful hand and took a line for it, and she wrote it again, because there had been a second reason and the measure takes one line for each one, and she put the pen down after the second one and did not add anything under it.

There is a worn place in that paper about four inches in from the foot of the page where an old pen stood and was let stand too long in a year nobody in this district can name. The first of the two lines went over the top of it and did not sit level with the lines above it. She saw that happen and she wrote the second one under it anyway, and the second one sat about a hair lower than the first, and she left both of them.

---

The page at the back of that lot book carried fifty-seven lines when she shut it that afternoon and carried fifty-nine lines after.

She put the pen back into the holder and set the book down on the boards against the near rail, and that boy put his thumb under the corner of it to settle it and took his thumb away again.

---

The light came off those boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said that afternoon by about four people out of about nineteen, and about nine people at the top of that bank stayed where they were until the boards came down off the cart face down.

Nobody thanked her. Nobody has thanked her on any morning in fifteen years and she stopped noticing that about eleven years ago, and this morning she noticed it again.

Nobody asked her how it was that two lines went down on one morning, and nobody asked her whether she had written down the other thing that went out in that yard that morning, and nobody has asked her what a line on that page is for, and she has never told anybody what she makes of the fifteen years.

She went up that bank with the book under her arm and a page behind it that was two lines heavier than the page she had brought down with her that morning.

---

Fifty-nine lines are on that page and not one of the fifty-nine is in the lot book, and it took two lines on this morning, one for the reason the man of about thirty-four said at about eleven about four feet off the near end of the second table and one for the reason the man who digs loam said from the north end at about a quarter past eleven without coming down, about four hundred yards away, and both of the lines carry the morning and neither of them carries a reason. A figure of her own went out loud in the same minute as the first of those two and nobody answered her and nobody thanked her. The clerk read one morning out loud and wrote two lines and nobody asked her how that is done and nobody asked her what a line is for. The lane count is thirty and nothing came down that lane. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read. The fifth of the five is unpaid.
