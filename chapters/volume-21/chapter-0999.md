# Chapter 0999: The Two Hundred And Eighty-Third Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And The Man Of Thirty-Eight Worked At About Four Feet Off The Near End Of The First Table From About Half Past Nine Until About Four And Said Nothing Out Loud At All, And The Lane Count Is Thirty And It Did Not Move This Morning And It Is Not Moved By A Decision

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and thirty-eight days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and ninety-seven. The train on that siding has stood one thousand two hundred and thirteen days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and twenty-seven days. Eight hundred and eighty-eight days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Six hundred and two days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Six hundred and seven days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and seventy-seven days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and eighty days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is six hundred and sixteen days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and fifty-six marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and forty-two marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places in this district can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made, and five is what this district does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

The man of thirty-eight worked at that table from about half past nine until the light went off those boards and said nothing out loud at all, and nothing said out loud in this yard this morning goes on the back of that book, so the morning went on it in the clerk's own hand. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

One morning out of all of them, the one hundred and eighty-second morning, carried a reason said out loud in that yard, and that page was not a place a reason could go on that morning, so it was not written down, and that reason is not counted anywhere.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank three hundred and thirteen mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and sixty-three mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-three mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and eighty-second morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the seven hundred and twenty-fourth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and thirty-eighth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and thirty-seven of them, and this is the one hundred and thirty-ninth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and seventy-second time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-seven lines and carried fifty-seven lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and ninety-eight days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and twenty-eighth afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of that bank and the culvert.

The toll of ninety-eight is paid and spent and may not be raised again or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

He came down the slope at about half past nine with his hands showing, the way he comes down that slope every morning of his life at that bank, and went to about four feet off the near end of the first table and started at the empty end of it.

He is thirty-eight. There is no bed in this district for him and no wage and no office, and he has not asked for the one or for the other or for the other, and he said a long time ago that he was not going to be thanked, and nobody at the top of that lane has ever offered him anything.

There is a place at about four feet off the near end of that first table where the grain has been rubbed out of the top by the going of him. Nobody gave it to him. He works out along the top of the table from the empty end, round the end, up the slope, across, back down, and in at the same corner, and he has not broken that route for anything in this morning or in any morning anybody at that bank can remember.

---

The boards were up and the boy had said the count out loud at them before anybody's hand had moved, which is thirty, and said no figure after it, and said out loud that he was saying no figure.

That is the whole of what is said at those boards on that morning before the light is properly across them, and it goes out into about four feet of air and is not repeated by anybody.

The man of fifty-six had been at that wall before there was any light on it and had got all four of them off it and had come away from it. Just after nine the second table went a foot further along and the near rail came with it, and the man at the end of that table got his boot up on the wedge under the leg that rocks and left it there.

The man of about thirty-two put his own table up, squared it at the near corner with the back of his heel, and went along the top of it with his cloth.

The wheel at the near end of that ground went on turning.

---

The piece in his hand at ten was a length of stuff with a rough edge on one side of it and a good edge on the other, and the work was to take the rough side off it in the one place where the last man to do it had left it, and to leave the good edge alone.

He has done that piece of work so many times that he does not look at his hands while he does it.

At about eleven he put it down and squared it along the near line of that table and found it about a half-inch out, which is the worst it has been in a long while, and he did not leave it.

He took the hand end of the line and drew it and looked along the line from where he was standing rather than from above it, because a line can be judged from the end and a heap cannot, and then he went back over the two pieces nearest that corner and took the thickness off the bottom one with the blade of a knife he keeps for that.

The thing about the going of a man over a table for a year is that he knows where his own feet are. His feet had been about four inches further along that ground than they had been at the start of the morning, and neither his hands nor his head had noticed.

---

Nobody in that yard asked him anything.

The boy at the boards looked over at about half past ten and looked away again. The woman at the scale about four feet off the near end of the second table looked across at his table while she waited for a pan to come still and did not say anything, and he did not look up while she was doing it.

He had a knife in his pocket and he has had one in that pocket for years, and it is not for eating and it is not for wood. It is for the one place on a table where the line has gone wrong, and a man who does the same table every morning for a year finds that place before he looks for it.

There is a rhythm to it that he could not describe to anybody and would not want to describe. About nine inches of the top of that table, twice as he goes along it, is where his own hands put the most weight, because that is the part of the table a man leans into when he is about to turn a piece over. That nine inches has been rubbed out of the grain. Everything else on that table still has a grain in it.

At about half past twelve he took the piece he was on down to the ground end of that table and laid it on the line and squared it, because a line can be counted by looking along it and a heap cannot, and then he went and started at the empty end of that table again with the next one.

That is the whole of what happened in his working day on that morning and he was not asked a question out loud at any point of it.

---

At about half past one the man of about forty-eight who keeps a tally put his number into about four hundred yards of air in the voice he uses for it, and about four people at the top of eleven feet said it after him out of step with each other, and it came back off that bank and went out flat over the top of it.

The man at the wheel had all of it over his own work and it made no difference to him.

Nothing came down that lane. The lane count is thirty and it is not moved by a decision, and nothing came down that lane this morning and nothing has come down it on any morning the man of thirty-eight has stood at that table.

About four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a boy who says a number and then says out loud that he is saying no number has never once been asked what those two of them are for, and that about four of them have not said whether he would go on doing it if nobody ever answered him.

---

The sun got over that low wall at about half past three and lay itself down along the stones at its foot, and the man standing in that strip had been in it since about nine, and a man of sixty-one was sitting about four feet off him with his forearms on his knees.

Good afternoon went across the four feet between them and one word came back over it.

At about four o'clock the man of fifty-six took the four off that wall for the second time that day, got all four of them, put his hand flat on the stone under the bottom one, and said out loud in the ordinary voice that he still does not know what those four of them are for.

About nineteen people heard it for the hundred and seventy-second time running.

Nobody asked him anything.

And the clerk read that morning out loud, once, at about four feet off the near end of the second table, and gave the account of it out loud before she put her pen to any of it.

"The two hundred and eighty-third morning," she said. "Nobody has put a question to anybody out loud in this yard this morning and nothing said out loud here this morning goes on the back of that book, so the morning goes on it in my hand. That is the account of it and I have given it out loud before I have written a word of it."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and the page carried fifty-seven lines before she shut that book and fifty-seven lines after.

---

He worked at that table from about half past nine until about four, and he said nothing out loud at all.

At about four he put the last piece he had in his hand down on the line along the near edge of that table and squared it off to the corner, because a line can be counted by looking along it and a heap cannot.

Then he took the cloth and went along the top of that table from the near end to the far end and back along it again, which is what a man does with a cloth, and the pieces along that line were square with the corner and the corner was square with the ground.

The light went off the boards at about half past four and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in and stayed where they were standing until the boards had come down off the cart face down.

And the woman who keeps a scale tied the cloth over her scale and knotted it and put the basket on her arm and went up that slope, and about four feet behind her he went up with his hands showing.

---

Fifty-seven lines are on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. A man of thirty-eight worked at about four feet off the near end of the first table from about half past nine until about four and said nothing out loud at all, and nobody in that yard asked him anything out loud. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning, and it is not moved by a decision, and a person going up that lane is not a page and a person not asking is not a page either. He has no bed and no wage and no office and has asked for none of the three. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read and the fifth of the five is unpaid.
