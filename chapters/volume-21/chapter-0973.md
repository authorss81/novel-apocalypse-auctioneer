# Chapter 0973: The Two Hundred And Fifty-Seventh Morning After The Thirtieth Of The Eighth Month, And She Said One Of Her Own Figures Out Loud, Once, And Nobody Answered Her And Nobody Thanked Her

The figure on the sheet at that gatepost is four hundred and eleven. Seven hundred and twelve days is the age of that figure.

The board carries eight hundred and seventy-one. The train on that siding has stood one thousand one hundred and eighty-seven days. Nobody has entered anything for nine hundred and one days. Eight hundred and sixty-two days separate the second of January and this morning.

Four hundred and fifty days is how long the bid was open, and it was not run, and it is not open. Five hundred and seventy-six days is how far behind the figure on the second line of that lot book is. Five hundred and eighty-one days is how long the rule said out loud in that yard has stood. Six hundred and fifty-one days is how long it has been since the first day of the eighth month.

The ninth of the nine printed nights is seven hundred and fifty-four days back and no night has been named. A body four hundred miles off is five hundred and ninety days past a printing it did not make. Five hundred and thirty marks have been cut off that board, and there are five hundred and sixteen marks in chalk along the edge of that second table.

Seven is what a clerk has counted, and it is on nothing. This district does not own four documents. Four places here can read those three lines. Four conditions have no end on it. Fourteen is what this district has made and five is what it does not have, and the fifth of the five is unpaid.

No reason was said out loud this morning and nobody said no, so that page did not move, and the morning went on in the clerk's own hand. Nobody put anything to the man of fifty-six. Nobody thanked the woman who keeps a scale and nobody asked her what she weighs things for. The first asking was not on that page and it was not on anything and nobody is able to recover it.

There was one morning, the one hundred and eighty-second morning, when somebody said a reason out loud in that yard, and the back of that lot book was not yet a place a reason could go, so nothing was written then, and that reason is not counted anywhere now, and no figure has been given out loud in that yard for the number of mornings a reason has been said out loud in it.

The second table has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and eighty-seven mornings, and the near rail has stood on the near side of it for two hundred and thirty-seven mornings, and nobody thanks the man who fitted it. A man has stood at the foot of that bank two hundred and fifty-seven mornings, on the word of the man who keeps it, and he said that number before it was entered. The table of the man of about thirty-two has stood at the foot of that bank for the two hundred and fifty-sixth morning, and it is not the second table and it is not the near rail. The man of fifty-six has read four figures off that wall on the six hundred and ninety-eighth of those mornings. A man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall on the six hundred and twelfth night of that run, having slept on six hundred and eleven of them, and this is the one hundred and thirteenth morning running of nobody asking him anything. The man of fifty-six's flat answer is the hundred and forty-sixth time running.

That lot book is on the boards. It has five lines on it and a column with one name in it, and the heading over that column still says whoever. The second space on that form is empty and nobody has asked for it to be filled. The lane count is thirty and it did not move this morning. Nothing came down that lane. The page at the back carries fifty-four lines and carried fifty-four lines when she shut the book. The record has stood one hundred and seventy-two days.

A man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him for the two hundred and second afternoon running. About nine people were standing on ground that has been public lot since the afternoon of the one hundred and seventeenth morning. There are about eleven feet between that wall and the top of this bank and about four miles between the top of this bank and the culvert.

The toll stands at ninety-eight, spent, and it cannot be raised or paid twice. Three of the first thirty-three lines were never read.

---

The turner's wheel had been going since before the light came across the eleven feet, and by about half past twelve the sound of it had become the thing the yard was quiet on top of.

She came down the bank with the basket on her arm. She had thought about this morning on and off since the middle of the winter, and what she had never been able to get past was never the doing of it. She had weighed iron in that yard for nine years and she knew to within a small margin what anything would come to before she put it on. What she had not been able to get past was the arithmetic of it: that a figure said by a woman at a scale in that yard was a thing that had happened to her, and not a thing that had happened to the yard.

Then the boy had said no figure out loud at those boards, the way he does, and had gone off to look at the ground.

She untied the knot, folded the cloth over the back of the leg and weighted it with the stone. She set the scale up about four feet off the near end of the second table, where the light would come onto the edge of her board by about a quarter to one, and she put her hand on the upright and looked along it for a while.

Then she took a bar out of the basket and set it on.

---

She waited for it to come still. It came still, and she wrote the figure on the edge of her own board in chalk, low down, in the gap left at the end of the last one.

Then she put the chalk in her pocket.

Nine years. Nine years of the same pan and the same stone and the same two feet of board, and every figure she had ever written had gone up that bank on the arm of a woman who was not thanked for it and did not expect to be. She had got so good at the not being thanked that she had almost stopped hearing it, the way a person stops hearing a road.

What she had never worked out was whether it was the hearing that was the thing, or the saying.

She took the chalk back out.

Nobody was close to her. That was the other thing. She had been waiting nine years for somebody to be close to her at the moment she did it, and there had never been anybody close to her at the moment she did anything, because a woman standing at a scale in that yard was a woman doing a thing to herself.

She waited until the light came onto the edge of the board and made the chalk shine, so that whoever looked would be able to see the figure she had written, and then she looked at the yard instead of at her own board, and she said it.

One figure. In the ordinary voice. Once.

She did not raise it. She did not say it to anybody in particular, and she did not say it twice, and she did not put it into about four hundred yards of air the way the man who keeps a tally does. She said it about as far as a person says a thing to a room.

---

Nobody answered her.

The turner's wheel went on turning. The man at the far end of the second table put his thumb on the rim of the thing he was holding and turned it a quarter round and set it down on the stone, and he did not look over. The turner did not stop and did not look up. The man of thirty-eight kept his own route at about four feet off the near end of the first table and did not break it.

Nobody asked her what she weighs things for. Nobody asked her where the number came from. Nobody asked her to say it again. Nobody thanked her. Nobody told her that it was the first time, and nobody told her that it was not.

About four people at the top of eleven feet had heard it. Nobody took it up. Nobody said it after her, out of step with each other, the way the yard takes up a number when a number goes out. It went out and it did not come back off the bank.

She went on weighing.

Her hands were not steady for the next thing in the basket and she let them be unsteady, because the only thing worse than a shaky hand at a scale is a still one that is lying. Then the next one after that came to rest and the figure was the figure, and she wrote it on the edge of the board in the ordinary way.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that a woman at that scale said one figure of her own out loud at about a quarter to one this morning and got it right first time, and that about four of them have not said what it is to be the only person in a yard who has done a thing, and that about four of them have said nobody asked her anything about it, and that about four of them have not said whether anybody at those tables heard her at all.

And about four people at the top of eleven feet have said since that the back of that lot book took no line this morning and that about four of them have not said what a yard is supposed to do with a figure that a person has said out loud at it, and that about four of them have said the clerk read the morning out loud the same as she reads it every morning, and that about four of them have not said what anybody thought of that either.

---

The rest of the afternoon was the longest part of it.

She had thought, when she was standing there with the chalk in her hand, that whatever was going to happen would happen quickly, because things do when they are said into a yard. What she had not allowed for was the other thing: that it would not happen, and that the not happening would then have to be got through.

She weighed what was left in the basket. She put the figures on the edge of her own board in the ordinary way, low down, in the gaps left at the end of the last ones, and she did not say any of those either. She took the cloth off the leg and folded it and put it over the scale and then took it off again because the light had gone off the edge of the board by then and there was no point in a folded cloth.

At about two she stood with her hands on the upright and looked along the yard at the tables.

Orren Pike at the boards had his back to her and his elbows on them. He hears everything that goes into that yard — she knows that, everybody knows that, it is the whole of his job and he does it well and he has never once written a number down — and he had not turned round. Not once. And she understood, standing there, that if the boy had turned round it would have been worse, because a boy turning round is a boy about to repeat it, and then it would have been said a second time by a person who was sixteen and did not know what he was doing.

At about half past two the turner's wheel was still going and the light was coming across the eleven feet for the last time that day, and she picked the chalk up and put it down again without marking anything, which was the fourth time she had done that in an hour, and she has never counted the chalk-ups in a morning before and she started counting them now.

At about three she put the cloth over the scale and knotted it and left the basket on the ground at the foot of the leg, because carrying it up the bank and coming back down for it would have meant two trips and two trips is a thing a person does when she is thinking about something.

Then she stood and waited for the clerk to read the morning out loud, and she heard it, and it was the account she has heard every morning for longer than the man of thirty-eight has stood at that table, and the page carried fifty-four lines before the clerk shut the book and fifty-four lines after.

---

The man of fifty-six said the four off that wall at about a quarter to eight and again at about four, and got them both times. About nineteen people heard him say that he still does not know what the four of them are for, for the hundred and forty-sixth time running, and nobody asked him anything.

At about half past three a man of about sixty-four was at the foot of that low wall and a man of sixty-one was on the stones about four feet off him, and they said good afternoon, and a man of sixty-one said good afternoon and said nothing else.

And then the clerk read that morning out loud, once, in the ordinary voice, before she put her pen to anything.

"The two hundred and fifty-seventh morning," she said. "Nothing goes on the back of that book today, so the morning goes on in my hand, and that is all I have to say about it and I have said it out loud."

Then she wrote the morning under the last line in her own hand and took no line for it, and about nine people at the top of eleven feet watched her do it, and the page carried fifty-four lines before she shut the book and fifty-four lines after.

---

At about half past four the light had gone off the boards and the goodnight that gets said at the end of a day in that yard was said by about four people out of about nineteen. About nine people at the top of that bank did not join in, and they stayed.

And the woman who keeps a scale went on standing at her own scale after the rest of that yard had started going up. She put the last thing in the basket on the scale and weighed it and wrote the figure on the edge of her own board, and she did not say that one either, and she was not going to.

Then she tied the cloth over her scale, knotted it, put the basket on her arm and went up that bank.

There was one figure of hers in about four hundred yards of air behind her, and it was not on any page, and it was not going to be.

---

Fifty-four lines stand on that page and not one of them is in the lot book. Three of the first thirty-three were never read. A figure of hers went out of that yard this morning at about a quarter to one and it is not on that page and not in that book and not down that lane, and no page in this district has ever had one of hers on it. The fifth of the five is unpaid. Nobody asked her what she weighs things for, and nobody answered her, and nobody thanked her.
